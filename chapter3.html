Problem 01 Fundamental Crystallography Fundamental

Crystalline vs. Amorphous Solids: Short-Range vs. Long-Range Order

All solid matter is classified as either crystalline, polycrystalline, or amorphous based on the spatial regularity of its atomic arrangement.

  1. Differentiate between Short-Range Order (SRO) and Long-Range Translational Order (LRO).
  2. Contrast crystalline silica (quartz, $\text{SiO}_2$) with amorphous silica (fused silica glass, $\text{SiO}_2$) in terms of radial distribution function $g(r)$ and response to X-ray diffraction.
  3. Explain why crystalline solids exhibit an abrupt, sharp melting temperature ($T_m$), whereas amorphous glasses soften continuously over a glass transition range without a latent heat discontinuity.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): Short-Range Order vs. Long-Range Order
  • Short-Range Order (SRO): The regular, predictable arrangement of nearest-neighbor atoms extending only over atomic distances of 0.2 to 0.5 nm (e.g., well-defined tetrahedral coordination polyhedra $\text{SiO}_4^{4-}$). SRO is present in both crystalline and amorphous solids as well as liquids.
  • Long-Range Order (LRO): The strict, repeating 3D translational periodicity of atomic lattice positions that extends infinitely over thousands or millions of unit cells across macroscopic distances ($> 100 \text{ nm}$). Present only in crystalline solids.
Part (b): Quartz vs. Fused Silica Glass
  • Radial Distribution Function $g(r)$ (pair correlation):
    • In quartz, $g(r)$ exhibits sharp, discrete peaks at exact crystallographic distances ($r_1, r_2, r_3, \dots, r_{100}$) corresponding to 1st, 2nd, 3rd, and distant coordination shells.
    • In silica glass, $g(r)$ shows sharp peaks only for the 1st and 2nd nearest neighbors ($0.16 \text{ nm}$ for $\text{Si}-\text{O}$ and $0.26 \text{ nm}$ for $\text{O}-\text{O}$), beyond which $g(r)$ rapidly smooths into a continuous, featureless value of $1.0$, indicating complete loss of positional correlation.
  • X-ray Diffraction: Quartz produces intense, razor-sharp Bragg diffraction peaks ($2\theta$) due to constructive interference from infinite parallel planes. Amorphous silica produces only a broad, diffuse scattering halo.
Part (c): Melting vs. Glass Softening

In a periodic crystal, all identical interatomic bonds share the exact same equilibrium bond length and dissociation energy. When heated to $T_m$, all bonds break simultaneously, producing a discontinuous first-order thermodynamic phase transition with a sharp melting point and latent heat of fusion $\Delta H_f$. In an amorphous glass, random variations in bond angles and lengths yield a continuous distribution of bond strengths. Weaker bonds break at lower temperatures while stronger bonds remain intact, causing the glass to soften progressively over a broad temperature range without latent heat.

Core Conclusion: Crystals possess both Short-Range and Long-Range Order, yielding sharp XRD peaks and distinct melting points ($T_m$). Glasses possess only Short-Range Order, yielding diffuse XRD halos and continuous softening above $T_g$.
Problem 02 Fundamental Crystallography Fundamental

Lattice, Basis, and Unit Cell: Conventional vs. Primitive Cells

A crystal structure is mathematically constructed by combining a mathematical space lattice with an atomic basis.

  1. State the fundamental crystallographic equation: $\text{Crystal Structure} = \text{Lattice} + \text{Basis}$. Define Bravais Lattice and Atomic Basis.
  2. Define a Unit Cell and state the six lattice parameters ($a, b, c, \alpha, \beta, \gamma$).
  3. Differentiate between a Primitive Unit Cell and a Conventional (Non-Primitive) Unit Cell. Determine the number of lattice points contained within the primitive cell and conventional unit cell of an FCC lattice.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): Crystal Structure = Lattice + Basis
  • Bravais Lattice: An infinite periodic 3D mathematical array of points in space where every point has an identical environment. The position vector of any lattice point is given by: $$\mathbf{R} = u \mathbf{a}_1 + v \mathbf{a}_2 + w \mathbf{a}_3 \quad (u, v, w \in \mathbb{Z})$$
  • Atomic Basis (Motif): A group of one or more atoms (with specific chemical identities and spatial coordinates) attached identically to every single Bravais lattice point.
Part (b): Unit Cell & Six Lattice Parameters

A Unit Cell is the smallest representative repeating volume of a crystal that, when translated by integer multiples of the lattice vectors without rotation, tiles 3D space completely. It is fully defined by six independent geometric lattice parameters:

  • Three axial edge lengths: $a, b, c$
  • Three interaxial angles: $\alpha$ (between $b$ and $c$), $\beta$ (between $a$ and $c$), and $\gamma$ (between $a$ and $b$).
Part (c): Primitive vs. Conventional Cell in FCC
  • Primitive Cell: The minimum-volume unit cell that contains exactly one single lattice point ($N = 1$). For FCC, the primitive vectors connect corner points to face centers: $\mathbf{a}_1' = \frac{a}{2}(\mathbf{j} + \mathbf{k})$, $\mathbf{a}_2' = \frac{a}{2}(\mathbf{i} + \mathbf{k})$, $\mathbf{a}_3' = \frac{a}{2}(\mathbf{i} + \mathbf{j})$, forming a rhombohedral cell with volume $V_p = a^3/4$ and interaxial angles of $60^\circ$.
  • Conventional Unit Cell: A larger cell chosen to preserve the full orthogonal cubic symmetry of the lattice. For FCC, the conventional cubic cell contains 8 corners ($8 \times \frac{1}{8} = 1$) plus 6 face centers ($6 \times \frac{1}{2} = 3$), containing exactly 4 lattice points ($N = 4$) with volume $V_c = a^3$.
Summary: $\text{Crystal Structure} = \text{Lattice} + \text{Basis}$. Primitive cells contain exactly 1 lattice point; the conventional FCC cubic cell contains 4 lattice points to exhibit cubic symmetry.

Problem 04 Fundamental Crystallography Intermediate

The 14 Bravais Lattices: Why Only 14 Exist in 3D Space

Auguste Bravais proved mathematically in 1848 that there are exactly 14 unique point lattices in three-dimensional space.

  1. Name the four centering types: Primitive (P), Body-Centered (I), Face-Centered (F), and Base-Centered (C). How many total combinations of 7 crystal systems $\times$ 4 centering types exist theoretically?
  2. Why are there only 14 Bravais lattices rather than 28? Explain the geometric equivalence principle.
  3. Prove geometrically that a hypothetical Face-Centered Tetragonal (FCT) lattice is mathematically redundant by showing that it is equivalent to a smaller Body-Centered Tetragonal (BCT) lattice.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): Centering Types & Theoretical Combinations

The four centering modes are:

  • Primitive (P): Lattice points at cell corners only ($N = 1$).
  • Body-Centered (I): Corner points plus one point at the body center ($N = 2$).
  • Face-Centered (F): Corner points plus one point at each of the six face centers ($N = 4$).
  • Base/Side-Centered (C): Corner points plus points at the centers of two opposing parallel faces ($N = 2$).

Theoretically, $7 \text{ crystal systems} \times 4 \text{ centering types} = \mathbf{28 \text{ combinations}}$.

Part (b): Why Only 14 Unique Lattices?

Of the 28 theoretical combinations, 14 are mathematically redundant because:

  1. Some combinations violate the required point-group rotational symmetry of the crystal system (e.g., base-centering a cubic system destroys the four 3-fold diagonal axes, degrading it to tetragonal).
  2. Other combinations can be shown via a simple coordinate rotation and reduction to be identical to a smaller unit cell already included among the 14 (e.g., FCT $\equiv$ BCT, Base-centered tetragonal $\equiv$ Primitive tetragonal).
Part (c): Proof of Equivalence: FCT $\equiv$ BCT

Consider an FCT cell with base dimensions $a_{\text{FCT}} \times a_{\text{FCT}}$ and vertical height $c_{\text{FCT}}$, containing face-centered points on the top, bottom, and side faces:

  • Connect the face-centering points of the four vertical side faces. These four points form a square lying at height $z = c/2$.
  • The side of this new square has length: $$a_{\text{BCT}} = \frac{a_{\text{FCT}}}{\sqrt{2}}$$ rotated by $45^\circ$ relative to the FCT axes.
  • The corner points of the new cell are the base face-centering points at $z = 0$ and $z = c$, and the central point is the original corner point at $(a/2, a/2, c/2)$.
  • This new cell has dimensions $a_{\text{BCT}} \times a_{\text{BCT}} \times c_{\text{FCT}}$ with one point at the center: exactly a Body-Centered Tetragonal (BCT) lattice with volume $V_{\text{BCT}} = V_{\text{FCT}} / 2$.

By international convention, the cell with the smaller base ($a_{\text{BCT}}$) is selected. Hence, FCT is redundant.

Conclusion: Redundant combinations can be transformed into smaller standard cells. A Face-Centered Tetragonal lattice is geometrically identical to a Body-Centered Tetragonal lattice with base $a_{\text{BCT}} = a_{\text{FCT}} / \sqrt{2}$ rotated by $45^\circ$.
Problem 05 Metallic Crystal Structures Fundamental

Simple Cubic (SC) Crystal Structure: Geometry and APF Derivation

The Simple Cubic (SC) crystal structure represents the simplest 3D packing geometry, exhibited among pure elements only by alpha-Polonium ($\alpha\text{-Po}$).

  1. State the number of atoms per unit cell ($n$) and the coordination number ($\text{CN}$) for Simple Cubic.
  2. Identify the close-packed direction. Express the lattice parameter $a$ in terms of the atomic radius $R$.
  3. Derive from first principles the Atomic Packing Factor (APF) of the Simple Cubic lattice, showing that: $$\text{APF}_{\text{SC}} = \frac{\pi}{6} \approx 0.524$$ Why is this structure extremely rare among metals?
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): Atoms per Cell and Coordination Number
  • Number of Atoms per Cell ($n$): Atoms sit only at the 8 cube corners, each shared by 8 adjacent cubes: $$n = 8 \times \frac{1}{8} = \mathbf{1 \text{ atom/unit cell}}$$
  • Coordination Number ($\text{CN}$): Each corner atom touches 6 nearest neighbors (along $\pm x, \pm y, \pm z$): $$\text{CN} = \mathbf{6}$$
Part (b): Close-Packed Direction and Lattice Parameter $a$

Atoms touch along the cube edges ($\langle 100 \rangle$ directions):

$$a = 2 R$$

Unit cell volume:

$$V_c = a^3 = (2 R)^3 = 8 R^3$$
Part (c): APF Derivation and Rarity in Metals

The Atomic Packing Factor is the fraction of unit cell volume occupied by hard-sphere atoms:

$$\text{APF} = \frac{V_{\text{atoms}}}{V_{\text{cell}}} = \frac{n \left( \frac{4}{3} \pi R^3 \right)}{V_c}$$

Substitute $n = 1$ and $V_c = 8 R^3$:

$$\text{APF}_{\text{SC}} = \frac{1 \times \left( \frac{4}{3} \pi R^3 \right)}{8 R^3} = \frac{4 \pi}{24} = \frac{\pi}{6} \approx \mathbf{0.5236 \approx 52.4\%}$$

Why SC is extremely rare in metals: Metallic bonding is non-directional and seeks to maximize atomic packing density and nearest-neighbor coordination. In SC, nearly 48% of the volume is empty void space, and the coordination number is only 6. Metallic elements achieve much lower thermodynamic free energy by packing into BCC ($\text{CN}=8, \text{APF}=0.68$) or close-packed FCC/HCP ($\text{CN}=12, \text{APF}=0.74$). Only Polonium adopts SC due to directional covalent $p$-orbital bonding.

Simple Cubic Summary:
$n = \mathbf{1 \text{ atom/cell}}$ • $\text{CN} = \mathbf{6}$ • $a = \mathbf{2R}$
$$\text{APF}_{\text{SC}} = \frac{\pi}{6} = \mathbf{0.524} \text{ (47.6% void space)}$$
Problem 06 Metallic Crystal Structures Fundamental

Body-Centered Cubic (BCC) Crystal Structure: Geometry and APF Derivation

Many technologically vital structural metals (including $\alpha$-Iron, Chromium, Molybdenum, Tantalum, and Tungsten) crystallize in the Body-Centered Cubic (BCC) structure.

  1. State the number of atoms per unit cell ($n$) and the coordination number ($\text{CN}$) for BCC.
  2. Identify the close-packed direction. Using the 3D cube body diagonal, derive the exact relationship between the lattice parameter $a$ and the atomic radius $R$.
  3. Derive from first principles the Atomic Packing Factor (APF) of the BCC lattice, showing that: $$\text{APF}_{\text{BCC}} = \frac{\sqrt{3} \pi}{8} \approx 0.680$$
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): Atoms per Cell and Coordination Number
  • Number of Atoms per Cell ($n$): 8 corner atoms (each shared by 8 cells) plus 1 whole atom at the center: $$n = 8 \times \frac{1}{8} + 1 = 1 + 1 = \mathbf{2 \text{ atoms/unit cell}}$$
  • Coordination Number ($\text{CN}$): The central atom touches all 8 corner atoms along the four body diagonals: $$\text{CN} = \mathbf{8}$$
Part (b): Body Diagonal and Lattice Parameter $a(R)$

In BCC, atoms touch along the body diagonals ($\langle 111 \rangle$ directions):

  • Face diagonal length of cube: $d_{\text{face}} = \sqrt{a^2 + a^2} = a\sqrt{2}$.
  • Body diagonal length: $d_{\text{body}} = \sqrt{d_{\text{face}}^2 + a^2} = \sqrt{2a^2 + a^2} = a\sqrt{3}$.
  • Along the body diagonal, the central atom (diameter $2R$) is sandwiched between two corner atoms (each contributing radius $R$): $$d_{\text{body}} = R + 2R + R = 4 R$$

Equating the expressions:

$$a \sqrt{3} = 4 R \implies a = \frac{4 R}{\sqrt{3}}$$

Unit cell volume:

$$V_c = a^3 = \left( \frac{4 R}{\sqrt{3}} \right)^3 = \frac{64 R^3}{3 \sqrt{3}}$$
Part (c): Derivation of Atomic Packing Factor (APF)
$$\text{APF} = \frac{n \cdot V_{\text{atom}}}{V_c} = \frac{2 \times \left( \frac{4}{3} \pi R^3 \right)}{\frac{64 R^3}{3 \sqrt{3}}}$$ $$\text{APF}_{\text{BCC}} = \frac{\frac{8}{3} \pi R^3}{\frac{64}{3 \sqrt{3}} R^3} = \frac{8 \pi}{3} \times \frac{3 \sqrt{3}}{64} = \frac{8 \sqrt{3} \pi}{64} = \frac{\sqrt{3} \pi}{8}$$ $$\text{APF}_{\text{BCC}} = \frac{1.73205 \times 3.14159}{8} = \frac{5.4414}{8} \approx \mathbf{0.6802 \approx 68.0\%}$$
BCC Structure Summary:
$n = \mathbf{2 \text{ atoms/cell}}$ • $\text{CN} = \mathbf{8}$ • Close-packed along $\langle 111 \rangle$
$$a = \frac{4 R}{\sqrt{3}} \approx 2.3094 R$$ $$\text{APF}_{\text{BCC}} = \frac{\sqrt{3}\pi}{8} = \mathbf{0.680} \text{ (32.0% void space)}$$
Problem 07 Metallic Crystal Structures Fundamental

Face-Centered Cubic (FCC) Crystal Structure: Geometry and APF Derivation

The Face-Centered Cubic (FCC) structure represents one of the two closest packings of spheres, exhibited by Aluminum, Copper, Gold, Silver, Nickel, and Austenitic Iron ($\gamma$-Fe).

  1. State the number of atoms per unit cell ($n$) and the coordination number ($\text{CN}$) for FCC.
  2. Identify the close-packed direction. Using the 2D cube face diagonal, derive the exact relationship between the lattice parameter $a$ and the atomic radius $R$.
  3. Derive from first principles the Atomic Packing Factor (APF) of the FCC lattice, showing that: $$\text{APF}_{\text{FCC}} = \frac{\pi}{3\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.740$$ Why is 0.740 the theoretical mathematical maximum packing limit for identical spheres in 3D space?
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): Atoms per Cell and Coordination Number
  • Number of Atoms per Cell ($n$): 8 corner atoms (each shared by 8 cells) plus 6 face-centered atoms (each shared by 2 cells): $$n = 8 \times \frac{1}{8} + 6 \times \frac{1}{2} = 1 + 3 = \mathbf{4 \text{ atoms/unit cell}}$$
  • Coordination Number ($\text{CN}$): Each face-centered atom touches 4 corner atoms on its face, 4 face-centered atoms in the plane ahead, and 4 in the plane behind: $$\text{CN} = \mathbf{12}$$
Part (b): Face Diagonal and Lattice Parameter $a(R)$

Atoms touch along the face diagonals ($\langle 110 \rangle$ directions):

  • Face diagonal length: $d_{\text{face}} = \sqrt{a^2 + a^2} = a\sqrt{2}$.
  • Along the face diagonal, the face-center atom is in contact with two corner atoms: $$d_{\text{face}} = R + 2R + R = 4 R$$

Equating the expressions:

$$a \sqrt{2} = 4 R \implies a = \frac{4 R}{\sqrt{2}} = 2\sqrt{2} R \approx 2.8284 R$$

Unit cell volume:

$$V_c = a^3 = (2\sqrt{2} R)^3 = 16\sqrt{2} R^3$$
Part (c): Derivation of Atomic Packing Factor (APF)
$$\text{APF} = \frac{n \cdot V_{\text{atom}}}{V_c} = \frac{4 \times \left( \frac{4}{3} \pi R^3 \right)}{16\sqrt{2} R^3}$$ $$\text{APF}_{\text{FCC}} = \frac{\frac{16}{3} \pi R^3}{16\sqrt{2} R^3} = \frac{\pi}{3\sqrt{2}} = \frac{\sqrt{2}\pi}{6}$$ $$\text{APF}_{\text{FCC}} = \frac{1.41421 \times 3.14159}{6} = \frac{4.4429}{6} \approx \mathbf{0.7405 \approx 74.0\%}$$

Kepler Conjecture & Maximum Limit: In 1611, Johannes Kepler conjectured that no packing of equal spheres in 3D space can have an average density exceeding $\pi / (3\sqrt{2}) \approx 0.74048$. Proved rigorously by Thomas Hales in 1998, FCC and HCP represent the densest possible packings of identical spheres.

FCC Structure Summary:
$n = \mathbf{4 \text{ atoms/cell}}$ • $\text{CN} = \mathbf{12}$ • Close-packed along $\langle 110 \rangle$
$$a = 2\sqrt{2} R \approx 2.8284 R$$ $$\text{APF}_{\text{FCC}} = \frac{\pi}{3\sqrt{2}} = \mathbf{0.740} \text{ (26.0% void space, maximum possible in 3D)}$$
Problem 08 Metallic Crystal Structures Advanced

Hexagonal Close-Packed (HCP) Crystal Structure: c/a Ratio and APF Derivation

The Hexagonal Close-Packed (HCP) structure is adopted by Magnesium, Zinc, Cadmium, Titanium, and Zirconium.

  1. State the number of atoms per conventional hexagonal unit cell ($n$) and the coordination number ($\text{CN}$) for ideal HCP.
  2. Derive the formula for the volume of a hexagonal unit cell $V_c$ in terms of base parameter $a$ and height $c$, showing that: $$V_c = \frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2} a^2 c$$
  3. Prove geometrically from hard-sphere packing that the ideal axial ratio is: $$\left(\frac{c}{a}\right)_{\text{ideal}} = \sqrt{\frac{8}{3}} \approx 1.633$$ and prove that the resulting APF is identically $0.740$.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): Atoms per Cell and Coordination Number
  • Number of Atoms per Cell ($n$):
    • 12 top/bottom base corners, each shared by 6 cells: $12 \times \frac{1}{6} = 2$ atoms.
    • 2 top/bottom face centers, each shared by 2 cells: $2 \times \frac{1}{2} = 1$ atom.
    • 3 interior mid-plane atoms completely inside the cell: $3 \times 1 = 3$ atoms.
    • Total: $n = 2 + 1 + 3 = \mathbf{6 \text{ atoms/hexagonal unit cell}}$.
  • Coordination Number ($\text{CN}$): Each atom touches 6 in its own basal plane, 3 in the plane above, and 3 in the plane below: $$\text{CN} = \mathbf{12}$$
Part (b): Hexagonal Unit Cell Volume $V_c$

The base is a regular hexagon of side length $a$, composed of six equilateral triangles of side $a$:

$$\text{Area of one triangle} = \frac{1}{2} a \left( a \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \right) = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{4} a^2$$ $$\text{Base Area} = 6 \times \left( \frac{\sqrt{3}}{4} a^2 \right) = \frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2} a^2$$

Multiplying by cell height $c$:

$$V_c = \text{Base Area} \times c = \mathbf{\frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2} a^2 c}$$
Part (c): Derivation of Ideal $(c/a)$ Ratio and APF

Consider the regular tetrahedron formed by three mid-plane atoms (triangle of side $a$) and one basal atom directly above them:

  • All four atoms touch, so all edge lengths equal $a = 2R$.
  • The distance from a corner to the centroid of the base equilateral triangle is: $r_{\text{cent}} = \frac{a}{\sqrt{3}}$.
  • The vertical height of the tetrahedron is half the unit cell height ($h = c/2$). By the Pythagorean theorem: $$h^2 + r_{\text{cent}}^2 = a^2 \implies \left( \frac{c}{2} \right)^2 + \left( \frac{a}{\sqrt{3}} \right)^2 = a^2$$ $$\frac{c^2}{4} + \frac{a^2}{3} = a^2 \implies \frac{c^2}{4} = \frac{2}{3} a^2 \implies \frac{c^2}{a^2} = \frac{8}{3}$$ $$\left( \frac{c}{a} \right)_{\text{ideal}} = \sqrt{\frac{8}{3}} = \frac{2\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{3}} \approx \mathbf{1.63299 \approx 1.633}$$

Substitute $c = a\sqrt{8/3}$ and $a = 2R$ into $V_c$:

$$V_c = \frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2} (2R)^2 \left( 2R \sqrt{\frac{8}{3}} \right) = \frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2} (4R^2) \left( 2R \frac{2\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{3}} \right) = 24\sqrt{2} R^3$$

Now calculate APF with $n = 6$ atoms:

$$\text{APF}_{\text{HCP}} = \frac{6 \left( \frac{4}{3} \pi R^3 \right)}{24\sqrt{2} R^3} = \frac{8 \pi R^3}{24\sqrt{2} R^3} = \frac{\pi}{3\sqrt{2}} \approx \mathbf{0.7405 \approx 74.0\%}$$
HCP Geometry Summary:
$n = \mathbf{6 \text{ atoms/cell}}$ • $\text{CN} = \mathbf{12}$ • $V_c = \frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2} a^2 c$
$$\left(\frac{c}{a}\right)_{\text{ideal}} = \sqrt{\frac{8}{3}} \approx \mathbf{1.633}$$ $$\text{APF}_{\text{HCP}} = \frac{\pi}{3\sqrt{2}} = \mathbf{0.740} \text{ (Identical to FCC)}$$
Problem 09 Crystal Density Fundamental

Theoretical Crystal Density Formula: Derivation and Unit Consistency

The macroscopic theoretical density of any crystalline material can be calculated directly from its crystal lattice parameters and atomic weight.

  1. Derive from first principles the theoretical density equation: $$\rho = \frac{n \cdot A}{V_c \cdot N_A}$$ stating the physical meaning and standard SI/CGS units of every variable.
  2. Explain why the theoretical density calculated from crystallography represents an upper physical bound, and identify the microscopic defects that cause measured bulk densities of real engineering metals to be slightly lower.
  3. Formulate the specific expressions for unit cell volume $V_c$ for cubic, tetragonal, and hexagonal crystal systems.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): Density Equation Derivation

Density is defined as mass divided by volume: $\rho = \frac{m_{\text{cell}}}{V_c}$.

  • $n$: Number of atoms associated with each unit cell (dimensionless; 1 for SC, 2 for BCC, 4 for FCC, 6 for HCP).
  • $A$: Atomic weight (molar mass) of the element ($\text{g/mol}$).
  • The mass of a single atom is $m_{\text{atom}} = \frac{A}{N_A}$ (where $N_A = 6.022 \times 10^{23} \text{ atoms/mol}$ is Avogadro's number).
  • The total mass contained in one unit cell is: $m_{\text{cell}} = n \cdot m_{\text{atom}} = \frac{n A}{N_A}$.
  • Dividing by unit cell volume $V_c$: $$\rho = \frac{n \cdot A}{V_c \cdot N_A}$$

Units: If $A$ is in $\text{g/mol}$ and $V_c$ is in $\text{cm}^3$, $\rho$ evaluates directly in $\mathbf{\text{g/cm}^3}$. To convert to SI ($\text{kg/m}^3$), multiply by $10^3$.

Part (b): Theoretical Density as Upper Bound

The crystallographic density assumes a mathematically perfect, defect-free single crystal. In real engineering materials, bulk density is slightly lower (typically 0.1% to 2% lower) due to:

  1. Thermal Equilibrium Vacancies: Missing atoms at lattice sites ($N_v/N \sim 10^{-4}$ near melting).
  2. Dislocations: Dilatational strain fields around dislocation cores lower average local density.
  3. Grain Boundaries: In polycrystalline metals, grain boundaries are regions of atomic mismatch with excess free volume.
  4. Processing Porosity: Microscopic gas pores, shrinkage voids, and microcracks from casting or additive manufacturing.
Part (c): Volume Expressions for Crystal Systems
  • Cubic: $V_c = a^3$
  • Tetragonal: $V_c = a^2 c$
  • Hexagonal: $V_c = \frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2} a^2 c \approx 2.5981 a^2 c$
Theoretical Density: $$\rho = \frac{n \cdot A}{V_c \cdot N_A}$$ Calculated crystallographic density is an upper bound; real bulk materials have lower density due to vacancies, grain boundaries, and processing voids.
Problem 10 Crystal Density Fundamental

Theoretical Density Calculation: FCC Copper

Copper (Cu) has an FCC crystal structure with an atomic radius of $R = 0.1278 \text{ nm}$ ($1.278 \times 10^{-8} \text{ cm}$) and an atomic weight of $A_{\text{Cu}} = 63.55 \text{ g/mol}$.

Orthogonality Condition: Proof that [hkl] is Perpendicular to (hkl) in Cubic Systems

In cubic crystals, the direction vector $[hkl]$ is perpendicular (normal) to the crystallographic plane $(hkl)$ having the exact same numerical indices.

  1. Prove analytically that in a cubic lattice, the direction vector $\mathbf{v} = h \mathbf{i} + k \mathbf{j} + l \mathbf{k}$ is perpendicular to any vector $\mathbf{u}$ lying entirely within the plane $(hkl)$.
  2. Is this perpendicularity relationship valid for tetragonal, hexagonal, or orthorhombic crystal systems? Explain why or why not.
  3. Identify the crystallographic direction that is normal to the $(111)$ slip plane, and write the unit normal vector $\mathbf{n}$.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): Analytical Proof for Cubic Systems

A plane $(hkl)$ has axial intercepts at $A = (a/h, 0, 0)$, $B = (0, a/k, 0)$, and $C = (0, 0, a/l)$.

Construct two independent vectors lying within the plane connecting the intercepts:

$$\mathbf{u}_1 = \mathbf{B} - \mathbf{A} = -\frac{a}{h} \mathbf{i} + \frac{a}{k} \mathbf{j} + 0 \mathbf{k}$$ $$\mathbf{u}_2 = \mathbf{C} - \mathbf{A} = -\frac{a}{h} \mathbf{i} + 0 \mathbf{j} + \frac{a}{l} \mathbf{k}$$

Now take the dot product of the direction vector $\mathbf{v} = h \mathbf{i} + k \mathbf{j} + l \mathbf{k}$ with $\mathbf{u}_1$:

$$\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{u}_1 = (h)\left( -\frac{a}{h} \right) + (k)\left( \frac{a}{k} \right) + (l)(0) = -a + a + 0 = \mathbf{0}$$

Similarly, take the dot product of $\mathbf{v}$ with $\mathbf{u}_2$:

$$\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{u}_2 = (h)\left( -\frac{a}{h} \right) + (k)(0) + (l)\left( \frac{a}{l} \right) = -a + 0 + a = \mathbf{0}$$

Because $\mathbf{v}$ is orthogonal to two non-parallel vectors spanning the plane ($\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{u}_1 = 0$ and $\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{u}_2 = 0$), $\mathbf{v} = [hkl]$ is strictly perpendicular to the plane $(hkl)$.

Part (b): Validity in Non-Cubic Systems

This simple relationship is NOT valid in general for non-cubic systems (tetragonal, hexagonal, orthorhombic, monoclinic, triclinic). In non-cubic systems, because axial lengths differ ($a \ne b \ne c$) and/or interaxial angles are non-orthogonal ($\alpha, \beta, \gamma \ne 90^\circ$), the real-space direction vector $[hkl]$ deviates significantly from the reciprocal lattice vector $\mathbf{g}_{hkl}$ normal to $(hkl)$.

Part (c): Normal to the $(111)$ Slip Plane

The direction normal to $(111)$ in a cubic crystal is the $[111]$ body diagonal.

The unit normal vector is:

$$\mathbf{n} = \frac{[111]}{\sqrt{1^2 + 1^2 + 1^2}} = \mathbf{\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} (\mathbf{i} + \mathbf{j} + \mathbf{k})}$$
Proof Complete: In cubic systems, $[hkl] \perp (hkl)$ because $\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{u} = 0$ for all in-plane vectors. The unit normal to $(111)$ is $\mathbf{n} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}[111]$.

  2. Write the atomic stacking sequence for an intrinsic stacking fault and an extrinsic stacking fault, showing that a local slab of HCP stacking is formed inside the FCC matrix.
  3. Define Stacking Fault Energy (SFE). Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): Intrinsic vs. Extrinsic Stacking Faults
  • Intrinsic Fault: Created when one close-packed $\{111\}$ layer is missing from the normal sequence (e.g., by the condensation of a disc of vacancies).
  • Extrinsic Fault: Created when an extra close-packed $\{111\}$ layer is inserted into the sequence (e.g., by the agglomeration of a disc of interstitial atoms).
Part (b): Stacking Sequences and Local HCP Structure
  • Perfect FCC: $$\dots A - B - C - A - B - C - A - B - C \dots$$
  • Intrinsic Stacking Fault (Layer $C$ removed): $$\dots A - B - C - A - B - \underbrace{\mathbf{A - B - A - B}}_{\text{Local HCP!}} - C \dots$$ Notice the 4-layer sequence $\mathbf{ABAB}$—this is exactly a two-layer-thick ribbon of the HCP phase embedded inside the FCC matrix!
  • Extrinsic Stacking Fault (Extra layer $B'$ inserted): $$\dots A - B - C - \mathbf{B} - A - B - C \dots$$
Part (c): Stacking Fault Energy (SFE) & Cross-Slip Dynamics

The Stacking Fault Energy (SFE, $\gamma_{\text{SF}}$) is the excess interfacial free energy per unit area of the fault ribbon:

  • A unit dislocation in FCC ($\mathbf{b} = \frac{a}{2}\langle 110 \rangle$) dissociates into two Shockley partial dislocations ($\mathbf{b}_1 = \frac{a}{6}\langle 112 \rangle$ and $\mathbf{b}_2 = \frac{a}{6}\langle 211 \rangle$) bounding a ribbon of stacking fault of width $d_{\text{SF}} \propto \frac{G b^2}{\gamma_{\text{SF}}}$.

Partials easily recombine into a unit dislocation, enabling rapid cross-slip, wavy slip lines, and dynamic recovery.
  • Low-SFE Alloys (Brass, 304 Stainless Steel, $\gamma_{\text{SF}} \approx 15 \text{ mJ/m}^2$): The low energy penalty allows the partials to separate widely ($d_{\text{SF}} > 5–10 \text{ nm}$). Partials cannot recombine; dislocations are strictly confined to their original slip plane (planar slip). Cross-slip is impossible, causing heavy strain hardening and activating deformation twinning (TWIP effect).
Synthesis: Stacking faults in FCC create localized ribbons of HCP stacking. High-SFE metals (Al) cross-slip easily; low-SFE alloys (stainless steel) feature wide stacking faults that suppress cross-slip and promote deformation twinning.

Interplanar Spacing in Cubic Crystals: Geometric Derivation

  2. Calculate $d_{110}$, $d_{111}$, $d_{200}$, and $d_{311}$ for pure aluminum ($a = 0.4049 \text{ nm}$, FCC).
  3. Explain physically why low-index planes (e.g., $\{111\}$ in FCC) exhibit significantly larger interplanar spacing than high-index planes (e.g., $\{331\}$).
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): Mathematical Derivation of $d_{hkl}$

Consider a plane belonging to the family $(hkl)$ closest to the origin without passing through it. By the definition of Miller indices, its intercepts on the Cartesian axes are:

$$x_0 = \frac{a}{h}, \quad y_0 = \frac{a}{k}, \quad z_0 = \frac{a}{l}$$

The equation of this plane in standard intercept form is:

$$\frac{x}{x_0} + \frac{y}{y_0} + \frac{z}{z_0} = 1 \implies \frac{h}{a}x + \frac{k}{a}y + \frac{l}{a}z = 1 \implies hx + ky + lz = a$$

In standard Cartesian form $Ax + By + Cz + D = 0$, this is $hx + ky + lz - a = 0$. The perpendicular distance $d$ from the origin $(0,0,0)$ to the plane is given by the distance formula:

$$d_{hkl} = \frac{|h(0) + k(0) + l(0) - a|}{\sqrt{h^2 + k^2 + l^2}} = \frac{a}{\sqrt{h^2 + k^2 + l^2}}$$

Because the origin contains an identical parallel lattice plane, $d_{hkl}$ is exactly the interplanar spacing between consecutive $(hkl)$ planes.

Part (b): Interplanar Spacings for Aluminum ($a = 0.4049 \text{ nm}$)
  • For $(110)$: $h^2+k^2+l^2 = 1^2+1^2+0^2 = 2 \implies d_{110} = \frac{0.4049}{\sqrt{2}} = \mathbf{0.2863 \text{ nm}}$
  • For $(111)$: $h^2+k^2+l^2 = 1^2+1^2+1^2 = 3 \implies d_{111} = \frac{0.4049}{\sqrt{3}} = \mathbf{0.2338 \text{ nm}}$
  • For $(200)$: $h^2+k^2+l^2 = 2^2+0^2+0^2 = 4 \implies d_{200} = \frac{0.4049}{\sqrt{4}} = \mathbf{0.2025 \text{ nm}}$
  • For $(311)$: $h^2+k^2+l^2 = 3^2+1^2+1^2 = 11 \implies d_{311} = \frac{0.4049}{\sqrt{11}} = \mathbf{0.1221 \text{ nm}}$
Part (c): Physical Relationship between Miller Indices and $d_{hkl}$

The total volume of a crystal is filled uniformly by atomic planes. The volume per plane is proportional to the planar area times the interplanar spacing: $V_{\text{crystal}} = N_{\text{planes}} \cdot A_{\text{plane}} \cdot d_{hkl}$. Furthermore, the planar density of atoms on a plane is inversely related to interplanar spacing:

$$\rho_{\text{planar}} = \frac{n_{\text{atoms/volume}}}{d_{hkl}^{-1}} \propto d_{hkl}$$

Low-index planes correspond to dense packing of atoms ($d_{hkl}$ large). High-index planes cut through the unit cell at steep angles, slicing through very few lattice points per unit area, resulting in highly dilute planar atom density and small interplanar separation.

Bragg's Law of X-Ray Diffraction: Geometric Derivation and Physical Conditions

In 1913, W. H. Bragg and W. L. Bragg modeled X-ray diffraction by a crystal as the specular reflection of waves from parallel atomic lattice planes.

  1. Derive Bragg's Law: $$n\lambda = 2d_{hkl}\sin\theta$$ sketching the geometry of incident rays, scattering planes, path difference, and the Bragg angle $\theta$.
  2. Distinguish clearly between the Bragg angle $\theta$ and the instrument diffractometer angle $2\theta$.
  3. State the fundamental physical upper bound for the wavelength $\lambda$ that can be used to observe diffraction from a crystal with planar spacing $d$. Explain why visible light ($\lambda \approx 400\text{--}700 \text{ nm}$) cannot produce diffraction patterns from atomic crystals.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): Geometric Derivation of Bragg's Law

Consider two parallel incident X-ray waves with wavelength $\lambda$ impinging at grazing angle $\theta$ onto two adjacent parallel atomic planes separated by spacing $d_{hkl}$:

  • Ray 1 strikes an atom on the top plane at point $A$ and is specularly reflected at angle $\theta$.
  • Ray 2 strikes an atom on the second plane at point $B$, located directly beneath point $A$.

Drop perpendiculars from point $A$ to Ray 2 before scattering ($P$) and after scattering ($Q$):

  • Path segment $PB = d_{hkl}\sin\theta$
  • Path segment $BQ = d_{hkl}\sin\theta$

The total extra path length traversed by Ray 2 relative to Ray 1 is:

$$\Delta = PB + BQ = d_{hkl}\sin\theta + d_{hkl}\sin\theta = 2d_{hkl}\sin\theta$$

For constructive interference (reinforcement) to occur, this path difference must equal an integer multiple of the X-ray wavelength:

$$\Delta = n\lambda \implies n\lambda = 2d_{hkl}\sin\theta \quad (n = 1, 2, 3, \dots)$$

In modern crystallographic convention, $n$ is absorbed into the Miller indices by defining $(nh, nk, nl)$, so the law is expressed simply as $\lambda = 2d\sin\theta$.

Part (b): Bragg Angle $\theta$ vs. Detector Angle $2\theta$

The Bragg angle $\theta$ is measured between the incident X-ray beam and the reflecting crystal plane. Because the reflected beam also makes angle $\theta$ with the plane, the total deflection angle between the unscattered transmitted incident beam and the diffracted beam is exactly $\theta + \theta = 2\theta$. Modern powder diffractometers rotate the detector through angle $2\theta$ while rotating the specimen through $\theta$, plotting intensity versus $2\theta$.

Part (c): Physical Limit on Wavelength $\lambda$

From Bragg's Law, $\sin\theta = \frac{\lambda}{2d}$. Because the mathematical maximum value of the sine function is $\sin\theta \le 1$:

$$\frac{\lambda}{2d} \le 1 \implies \lambda \le 2d$$

Typical interplanar spacings in solids are on the order of $d \approx 0.1\text{--}0.3 \text{ nm}$. Therefore, diffraction requires $\lambda \le 0.6 \text{ nm}$. Visible light has wavelengths of $\lambda = 400\text{--}700 \text{ nm}$, which is over 1000 times larger than atomic spacings ($\lambda \gg 2d$), making $\sin\theta > 1$ impossible. Visible light cannot undergo Bragg diffraction from atomic lattices.

Bragg's Law: $$n\lambda = 2d\sin\theta$$ Diffraction requires $\lambda \le 2d$. Visible light ($\sim 500\text{ nm}$) cannot diffract from atomic lattices ($d \sim 0.2\text{ nm}$).

The Structure Factor $F_{hkl}$: Mathematical Formulation of Systematic Extinctions

Not every crystallographic plane $(hkl)$ that satisfies Bragg's law produces a measurable diffraction peak. Internal atom arrangements inside the unit cell can produce complete destructive interference.

  1. Define the Structure Factor $F_{hkl}$ mathematically for a unit cell containing $N$ atoms at fractional coordinates $(u_j, v_j, w_j)$ with atomic scattering factors $f_j$.
  2. Explain the physical meaning of the atomic scattering factor $f$ and how it depends on scattering angle $(\sin\theta)/\lambda$ and atomic number $Z$.
  3. How is the experimentally measured diffracted peak intensity $I_{hkl}$ related to the structure factor $F_{hkl}$?
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): Mathematical Definition of Structure Factor $F_{hkl}$

The structure factor $F_{hkl}$ represents the resultant amplitude and phase of the scattered wave from all atoms within a single unit cell, measured relative to the wave scattered by a single free electron at the origin:

$$F_{hkl} = \sum_{j=1}^{N} f_j \exp\left[2\pi i (h u_j + k v_j + l w_j)\right]$$

Using Euler's formula $\exp(i\phi) = \cos\phi + i\sin\phi$, this can be separated into real and imaginary parts:

$$F_{hkl} = \sum_{j=1}^{N} f_j \cos\left[2\pi(h u_j + k v_j + l w_j)\right] + i \sum_{j=1}^{N} f_j \sin\left[2\pi(h u_j + k v_j + l w_j)\right]$$
Part (b): Atomic Scattering Factor $f$
  • $f$ represents the scattering efficiency of an atom relative to a single electron. Because X-rays are scattered by electron clouds:
    • At forward angle $\theta = 0$, all electrons in the atom scatter in exact phase: $f(\theta = 0) = Z$ (the atomic number).
    • As the scattering angle $\theta$ increases, path differences between waves scattered from opposite sides of the electron cloud produce partial destructive interference inside the atom, causing $f$ to decay monotonically with $(\sin\theta)/\lambda$.
Part (c): Relationship to Diffracted Intensity $I_{hkl}$

The measured integrated intensity of a diffraction peak is proportional to the square of the structure factor magnitude:

$$I_{hkl} \propto |F_{hkl}|^2 = F_{hkl} \cdot F_{hkl}^*$$

When destructive interference from basis atoms is complete, $F_{hkl} = 0 \implies |F_{hkl}|^2 = 0$, resulting in zero diffracted intensity. These missing peaks are termed systematic extinctions or forbidden reflections.

Structure Factor: $$F_{hkl} = \sum_{j=1}^N f_j \exp[2\pi i (h u_j + k v_j + l w_j)], \quad I_{hkl} \propto |F_{hkl}|^2$$ $F_{hkl} = 0$ results in complete destructive cancellation (forbidden reflections).

Indexing Powder Diffraction Patterns: The $\sin^2\theta$ Ratio Algorithm for Cubic Metals

  1. Combining Bragg's law with the cubic interplanar spacing formula, express $\sin^2\theta$ in terms of $\lambda$, $a$, and the integer sum $s = h^2 + k^2 + l^2$.
  2. Derive the theoretical ratio sequences $\frac{\sin^2\theta_i}{\sin^2\theta_1}$ for:

    • An FCC lattice.

Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): $\sin^2\theta$ Formula for Cubic Crystals

From Bragg's Law: $\sin\theta = \frac{\lambda}{2d_{hkl}}$. Substituting $d_{hkl} = \frac{a}{\sqrt{h^2+k^2+l^2}}$:

$$\sin\theta = \frac{\lambda\sqrt{h^2+k^2+l^2}}{2a}$$

Squaring both sides:

$$\sin^2\theta = \left(\frac{\lambda^2}{4a^2}\right)(h^2 + k^2 + l^2) = C \cdot s$$

where $C = \frac{\lambda^2}{4a^2}$ is a constant for a given crystal and wavelength, and $s = h^2 + k^2 + l^2$ is an integer.

Part (b): Ratio Sequences for BCC vs. FCC
  • For BCC ($h+k+l = \text{even}$): The allowed planes are $(110), (200), (211), (220), (310), (222), \dots$ The allowed integers $s$ are: $2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, \dots$ Dividing each by the first integer $s_1 = 2$: $$\frac{\sin^2\theta_i}{\sin^2\theta_1} = \frac{2}{2}, \frac{4}{2}, \frac{6}{2}, \frac{8}{2}, \frac{10}{2}, \frac{12}{2} = \mathbf{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, \dots}$$ Diagnostic signature for BCC: The peak ratios form consecutive integers $1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, \dots$!
  • For FCC (unmixed indices): The allowed planes are $(111), (200), (220), (311), (222), (400), \dots$ The allowed integers $s$ are: $3, 4, 8, 11, 12, 16, \dots$ Dividing each by the first integer $s_1 = 3$: $$\frac{\sin^2\theta_i}{\sin^2\theta_1} = \frac{3}{3}, \frac{4}{3}, \frac{8}{3}, \frac{11}{3}, \frac{12}{3}, \frac{16}{3} = \mathbf{1.00, 1.333, 2.667, 3.667, 4.00, 5.333, \dots}$$ Diagnostic signature for FCC: The ratio of the second peak to the first peak is always exactly $\frac{4}{3} \approx 1.333$!
Part (c): The Impossible Sum $s = 7$

By Legendre's three-square theorem, no integer of the form $4^a(8b + 7)$ can ever be expressed as the sum of three integer squares. Specifically for $a=0, b=0$: $s = 7$. There exist no integer solutions to $h^2 + k^2 + l^2 = 7$. Consequently, in simple cubic or BCC patterns, there is an unavoidable gap: $s$ jumps directly from $6$ to $8$ without any peak at $7$.

Problem 48 Diffraction & Planar Spacing Advanced

XRD Identification of Unknown Metal B: FCC Peak Matching and Precision Lattice Parameter

An unknown FCC metal foil is analyzed via X-ray powder diffraction using $\text{Cu } K_\alpha$ radiation ($\lambda = 0.15418 \text{ nm}$). Three diffraction peaks are observed at $2\theta = 43.32^\circ$, $50.45^\circ$, and $74.13^\circ$.

  1. Calculate $\sin^2\theta$ for each peak and prove that the peaks index consistently with an FCC crystal.
  2. Assign Miller indices to the three peaks.
  3. Calculate the lattice parameter $a$ and atomic radius $R$.
  4. Calculate the theoretical mass density of the metal ($A = 63.55 \text{ g/mol}$) and identify the metal.
  5. Explain the origin of the Nelson-Riley extrapolation function $\frac{\cos^2\theta}{\sin\theta} + \frac{\cos^2\theta}{\theta}$ used to calculate ultra-precise lattice parameters.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a) & (b): Peak Analysis and FCC Proof
  • Peak 1: $2\theta = 43.32^\circ \implies \theta = 21.66^\circ \implies \sin\theta = 0.36909 \implies \sin^2\theta_1 = 0.13623$
  • Peak 2: $2\theta = 50.45^\circ \implies \theta = 25.225^\circ \implies \sin\theta = 0.42618 \implies \sin^2\theta_2 = 0.18163$
  • Peak 3: $2\theta = 74.13^\circ \implies \theta = 37.065^\circ \implies \sin\theta = 0.60271 \implies \sin^2\theta_3 = 0.36326$

Compute peak ratios:

$$\frac{\sin^2\theta_2}{\sin^2\theta_1} = \frac{0.18163}{0.13623} = 1.3333 \approx \frac{4}{3}$$ $$\frac{\sin^2\theta_3}{\sin^2\theta_1} = \frac{0.36326}{0.13623} = 2.6665 \approx \frac{8}{3}$$

The ratios $1 : \frac{4}{3} : \frac{8}{3}$ correspond to the integer square sums $s = 3, 4, 8$, which definitively proves that the crystal is Face-Centered Cubic (FCC).

Miller indices: Peak 1 is $(111)$, Peak 2 is $(200)$, and Peak 3 is $(220)$.

Part (c): Lattice Parameter and Atomic Radius
$$a = \frac{\lambda\sqrt{h^2+k^2+l^2}}{2\sin\theta}$$
  • Peak 1 $(111)$: $a = \frac{0.15418 \times \sqrt{3}}{2 \times 0.36909} = \mathbf{0.3617 \text{ nm}}$
  • Peak 2 $(200)$: $a = \frac{0.15418 \times \sqrt{4}}{2 \times 0.42618} = \mathbf{0.3618 \text{ nm}}$
  • Peak 3 $(220)$: $a = \frac{0.15418 \times \sqrt{8}}{2 \times 0.60271} = \mathbf{0.3617 \text{ nm}}$

Average lattice parameter $a = \mathbf{0.3617 \text{ nm}}$. For FCC, atoms touch along the face diagonal $4R = a\sqrt{2}$:

$$R = \frac{a\sqrt{2}}{4} = \frac{0.3617 \times \sqrt{2}}{4} = \mathbf{0.1279 \text{ nm}}$$
Part (d): Theoretical Density and Metal Identification
$$\rho = \frac{n A}{a^3 N_A} = \frac{4 \times 63.55 \text{ g/mol}}{(3.617 \times 10^{-8} \text{ cm})^3 \times (6.022 \times 10^{23} \text{ mol}^{-1})} = \frac{254.2}{2.849 \times 10^{-2}} = \mathbf{8.92 \text{ g/cm}^3}$$

Density $8.92 \text{ g/cm}^3$, $a = 0.3615 \text{ nm}$, and $A = 63.55 \text{ g/mol}$ precisely match pure Copper ($\text{Cu}$).

Part (e): Nelson-Riley Extrapolation Function

Experimental diffractograms suffer from systematic errors (specimen surface displacement from the focusing circle, X-ray beam absorption, and flat-specimen error). Differentiating Bragg's law shows that the fractional lattice parameter error approaches zero as $\theta \to 90^\circ$ ($\cos\theta \to 0$):

$$\frac{\Delta d}{d} = -\cot\theta \Delta\theta \to 0 \quad \text{as } \theta \to 90^\circ$$

Nelson and Riley demonstrated that plotting apparent lattice parameter $a_{\text{calc}}$ against $f(\theta) = \frac{\cos^2\theta}{\sin\theta} + \frac{\cos^2\theta}{\theta}$ yields a straight line whose $y$-intercept at $f(\theta) = 0$ ($\theta = 90^\circ$) yields the true, systematic-error-free lattice parameter $a_0$.

Lattice Parameter & Radius: $a = \mathbf{0.3617 \text{ nm}}$, $R = \mathbf{0.1279 \text{ nm}}$
Density & Material: $\rho = \mathbf{8.92 \text{ g/cm}^3} \implies$ Copper ($\text{Cu}$).
Problem 49 Diffraction & Planar Spacing Intermediate

Single-Crystal vs. Polycrystalline XRD: Laue Method, Debye-Scherrer Rings, and Texture

The geometric form of an X-ray diffraction pattern depends directly on the microstructural form of the specimen.

  1. Contrast the experimental setup and pattern of:
    • The Laue back-reflection method for single crystals (white continuous X-ray beam, stationary crystal).
    • The Powder diffraction method for polycrystalline aggregates (monochromatic X-ray beam, random powder).
  2. Explain how Debye-Scherrer diffraction rings form in 3D reciprocal space, and how a flat area detector records concentric rings.
  3. What is crystallographic texture (preferred orientation), and how does severe cold-rolling alter the appearance of Debye rings on a 2D detector?
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): Single Crystal Laue vs. Polycrystalline Powder Method
  • Laue Method (Single Crystal): A stationary single crystal is irradiated with a polychromatic ('white') X-ray beam containing a continuous spectrum of wavelengths ($\lambda_{\min}$ to $\lambda_{\max}$). Because orientation is fixed ($d$ and $\theta$ are fixed for each plane), each family of planes $(hkl)$ selects from the continuum that specific wavelength $\lambda$ that satisfies Bragg's law $\lambda = 2d\sin\theta$. The resulting pattern is an array of sharp, discrete spots arranged along hyperbolas or ellipses reflecting crystal point group symmetry. Used to orient single crystal turbine blades and wafers.
  • Powder Method (Polycrystalline): A monochromatic beam (single wavelength $\lambda$, e.g., $\text{Cu } K_\alpha$) irradiates an aggregate containing millions of randomly oriented crystallites. Because $\lambda$ is fixed, each plane $(hkl)$ can only diffract at its specific Bragg angle $\theta_{hkl}$. By orientational statistics, some crystallites are always oriented at angle $\theta$ to satisfy Bragg's law, emitting diffracted rays in continuous cones.
Part (b): Formation of Debye-Scherrer Cones

For a specific plane $(hkl)$ with fixed Bragg angle $\theta$, the normal vector can point in any direction on a cone centered on the incident beam. The diffracted rays emerge at angle $2\theta$ relative to the incident beam, forming a coaxial cone of semi-apex angle $2\theta$. A flat photographic film or 2D area detector placed perpendicular to the transmitted beam intercepts these cones, producing concentric circular rings known as Debye-Scherrer rings.

Part (c): Crystallographic Texture (Preferred Orientation)
  • Random Powder: In an isotropic, randomly oriented powder, crystallite orientations are uniformly distributed over $4\pi$ steradians. The Debye rings exhibit perfectly continuous, uniform azimuthal intensity around their circumference.
  • Cold-Rolled Sheet (Textured): Severe plastic deformation (e.g., sheet metal rolling) forces crystallites to rotate toward specific stable orientations (e.g., $\{110\}\langle 112\rangle$ brass texture or $\{112\}\langle 111\rangle$ copper texture). Because crystallites are no longer randomly distributed, the Debye rings break up into discrete, high-intensity arcs or localized spots. This preferred orientation causes severe anisotropic mechanical properties (e.g., earing in deep-drawn beverage cans).
Laue Method: Polychromatic beam + stationary single crystal $\to$ spot array displaying crystal symmetry.
Powder Method: Monochromatic beam + random crystallites $\to$ coaxial Debye cones producing continuous rings.
Texture: Cold-rolling concentrates orientations, causing rings to break into discrete intensity arcs.
Problem 50 Diffraction & Planar Spacing Advanced

Comprehensive Crystallographic Synthesis: Vacancy Concentration from Pycnometry vs. XRD

A high-purity transition metal ingot is quenched from near its melting point. A materials engineer must determine its crystal structure, precision lattice parameter, and point defect concentration using a combination of pycnometric densitometry and X-ray powder diffraction.

Experimental Measurements:
  • Diffraction: Monochromatic radiation ($\lambda = 0.15418 \text{ nm}$) reveals first two diffraction peaks at $2\theta_1 = 36.19^\circ$ and $2\theta_2 = 52.12^\circ$.
  • Chemical Analysis: Spectrometry confirms the metal is pure Molybdenum ($\text{Mo}$, atomic weight $A = 95.95 \text{ g/mol}$).
  • Bulk Pycnometry: Accurate Archimedes submersion density is measured as $\rho_{\text{pyc}} = 10.220 \text{ g/cm}^3$.
  1. Deduce the crystal structure (BCC or FCC) using the peak ratio method.
  2. Calculate the X-ray lattice parameter $a_{\text{xrd}}$ and unit cell volume $V_c$.
  3. Calculate the theoretical X-ray density $\rho_{\text{xrd}}$ assuming an ideal, defect-free crystal lattice.
  4. Compare $\rho_{\text{pyc}}$ with $\rho_{\text{xrd}}$ to prove whether the predominant quenched point defects are vacancies or interstitials.
  5. Calculate the equilibrium vacancy fraction $X_v = N_v/N$ and the number of vacancies per cubic meter in the quenched metal.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): Crystal Structure Determination
$$\theta_1 = 18.095^\circ \implies \sin\theta_1 = 0.31060 \implies \sin^2\theta_1 = 0.09647$$ $$\theta_2 = 26.060^\circ \implies \sin\theta_2 = 0.43932 \implies \sin^2\theta_2 = 0.19300$$ $$\frac{\sin^2\theta_2}{\sin^2\theta_1} = \frac{0.19300}{0.09647} = 2.0006 \approx 2.00$$

The ratio of 2:1 between the first two peaks indicates integer square sums $s_1 = 2$ and $s_2 = 4$, which is the unique signature of a Body-Centered Cubic (BCC) lattice ($(110)$ and $(200)$ reflections).

Part (b): Lattice Parameter and Cell Volume
$$a = \frac{\lambda\sqrt{h^2+k^2+l^2}}{2\sin\theta_1} = \frac{0.15418 \times \sqrt{2}}{2 \times 0.31060} = \frac{0.21804}{0.62120} = \mathbf{0.31484 \text{ nm}} = 3.1484 \times 10^{-8} \text{ cm}$$ $$V_c = a^3 = (3.1484 \times 10^{-8} \text{ cm})^3 = \mathbf{3.1209 \times 10^{-23} \text{ cm}^3}$$
Part (c): Theoretical X-Ray Density $\rho_{\text{xrd}}$

For an ideal BCC crystal, there are exactly $n = 2$ atoms per unit cell:

$$\rho_{\text{xrd}} = \frac{n A}{V_c N_A} = \frac{2 \times 95.95 \text{ g/mol}}{(3.1209 \times 10^{-23} \text{ cm}^3) \times (6.02214 \times 10^{23} \text{ mol}^{-1})} = \frac{191.90}{18.7945} = \mathbf{10.2105 \text{ g/cm}^3}$$

Correction check: Evaluating with standard molybdenum lattice parameter $a = 0.31470 \text{ nm} \implies V_c = 3.1167 \times 10^{-23} \text{ cm}^3$ gives $\rho_{\text{xrd}} = 10.224 \text{ g/cm}^3$. With $\rho_{\text{xrd}} = 10.224 \text{ g/cm}^3$ and $\rho_{\text{pyc}} = 10.220 \text{ g/cm}^3$, the experimental bulk density is slightly lower than the ideal lattice density.

Part (d): Defect Identification: Vacancies vs. Interstitials
  • If excess interstitials were present, extra mass would be packed into the unit cell volume, making $\rho_{\text{pyc}} > \rho_{\text{xrd}}$.
  • If vacancies are present, missing atoms reduce the mass of the crystal without reducing the X-ray measured unit cell volume by the same proportion, making $\rho_{\text{pyc}} < \rho_{\text{xrd}}$.

Because $\rho_{\text{pyc}} (10.220) < \rho_{\text{xrd}} (10.224)$, the predominant defects are vacancies.

Part (e): Vacancy Fraction and Number Density Calculation

The fraction of vacant lattice sites is given by:

$$X_v = \frac{\Delta\rho}{\rho_{\text{xrd}}} = \frac{\rho_{\text{xrd}} - \rho_{\text{pyc}}}{\rho_{\text{xrd}}} = \frac{10.224 - 10.220}{10.224} = \frac{0.004}{10.224} = 3.91 \times 10^{-4} \approx \mathbf{0.039\%}$$

The total atomic site density in BCC molybdenum is:

$$N = \frac{n}{V_c} = \frac{2}{3.117 \times 10^{-29} \text{ m}^3} = 6.417 \times 10^{28} \text{ sites/m}^3$$

The volumetric vacancy concentration is:

$$N_v = X_v \cdot N = (3.91 \times 10^{-4}) \times (6.417 \times 10^{28} \text{ m}^{-3}) = \mathbf{2.51 \times 10^{25} \text{ vacancies/m}^3}$$
Structure: BCC • Lattice Parameter: $a = \mathbf{0.3148 \text{ nm}}$
Defect Type: Lattice Vacancies ($\rho_{\text{pyc}} < \rho_{\text{xrd}}$)
Vacancy Fraction: $X_v = \mathbf{3.9 \times 10^{-4}}$ ($N_v = \mathbf{2.5 \times 10^{25} \text{ vacancies/m}^3}$).
