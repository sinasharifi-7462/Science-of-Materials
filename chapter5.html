Problem 01 Diffusion Fundamentals Fundamental

Atomic Mechanisms of Diffusion: Vacancy Diffusion vs. Interstitial Migration

Diffusion is the phenomenon of material transport by atomic motion within solid crystals.

  1. Describe the atomic mechanisms of:
    • Vacancy Diffusion (Substitutional / Self-Diffusion).
    • Interstitial Diffusion.
  2. Explain why the activation energy for interstitial diffusion ($Q_{\text{interstitial}} \approx 80\text{--}140 \text{ kJ/mol}$) is substantially lower than for vacancy diffusion ($Q_{\text{substitutional}} \approx 200\text{--}300 \text{ kJ/mol}$) in the same host metal.
  3. At $900^\circ\text{C}$, the diffusion coefficient of Carbon in FCC iron is $D_{\text{C}} \approx 1.5 \times 10^{-11} \text{ m}^2/\text{s}$, whereas the self-diffusion coefficient of Iron in FCC iron is $D_{\text{Fe}} \approx 2.0 \times 10^{-16} \text{ m}^2/\text{s}$. Compute the ratio $D_{\text{C}} / D_{\text{Fe}}$ and discuss its engineering significance.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): Atomic Mechanisms of Diffusion
  • Vacancy Diffusion: Host solvent atoms or substitutional solute atoms migrate from their normal lattice sites into adjacent unoccupied vacant sites. The process requires two coordinated events: a vacancy must first be present on an adjacent neighbor site, and the migrating atom must acquire sufficient thermal vibrational energy to squeeze through surrounding atoms into that vacancy. Operates for self-diffusion and substitutional solutes (e.g., Cu, Ni, Zn).
  • Interstitial Diffusion: Small solute atoms (e.g., H, C, N, O) migrate from one interstitial void (interstice) to an adjacent unoccupied interstitial void. Because host atoms occupy lattice sites and solute concentrations are small, the vast majority of adjacent interstitial voids are empty and immediately available.
Part (b): Why Interstitial Activation Energy is Much Lower ($Q_{\text{int}} \ll Q_{\text{sub}}$)

The total activation energy for vacancy-mediated diffusion consists of the sum of two distinct thermodynamic energies:

$$Q_{\text{substitutional}} = \Delta H_f + \Delta H_m$$

where $\Delta H_f$ is the enthalpy to create an adjacent vacancy, and $\Delta H_m$ is the enthalpy of atomic migration.

For interstitial diffusion, neighboring interstitial sites are naturally empty. No prior defect creation step is required ($\Delta H_f = 0$). Furthermore, interstitial atoms are much smaller than host atoms, so squeezing through interstitial bottlenecks requires significantly less elastic lattice distortion:

$$Q_{\text{interstitial}} = \Delta H_m \ll (\Delta H_f + \Delta H_m)$$

Consequently, interstitial diffusion proceeds with less than half the activation energy of substitutional diffusion.

Part (c): Numerical Ratio at $900^\circ\text{C}$ and Engineering Consequence
$$\frac{D_{\text{C}}}{D_{\text{Fe}}} = \frac{1.5 \times 10^{-11} \text{ m}^2/\text{s}}{2.0 \times 10^{-16} \text{ m}^2/\text{s}} = \mathbf{75,000 \approx 7.5 \times 10^4}$$

Carbon diffuses roughly 75,000 times faster through the iron lattice than iron atoms themselves! This enormous kinetic disparity enables rapid industrial case-hardening of steel (carburizing gears in hours at $900^\circ\text{C}$) while keeping the underlying iron grain structure intact.

Vacancy Mechanism: $Q = \Delta H_f + \Delta H_m$ (requires pre-existing vacancy).
Interstitial Mechanism: $Q = \Delta H_m$ only (adjacent sites are empty, smaller solute radius).
Kinetic Ratio at $900^\circ\text{C}$: Carbon diffuses $\mathbf{75,000\times}$ faster than iron in austenite.
Problem 02 Diffusion Fundamentals Fundamental

Interdiffusion vs. Self-Diffusion: Tracer Diffusion and Concentration Gradients

Diffusion processes can occur under uniform chemical composition or in the presence of chemical concentration gradients.

  1. Differentiate between Self-Diffusion and Interdiffusion (Chemical Diffusion).
  2. How is self-diffusion measured experimentally in the laboratory using radioactive or stable isotopes (tracer diffusion)?
  3. State the thermodynamic driving force for self-diffusion versus interdiffusion. Explain why diffusion can occasionally proceed "uphill" from lower to higher concentration (spinodal decomposition).
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): Self-Diffusion vs. Interdiffusion
  • Self-Diffusion: Atomic migration occurring in pure elemental crystals or homogeneous alloys in the absence of a chemical composition gradient. Atoms wander randomly by Brownian thermal motion; no net macroscopic mass transport occurs.
  • Interdiffusion (Chemical / Impurity Diffusion): Atomic migration occurring in a concentration gradient (e.g., across a Cu-Ni diffusion couple). Driven by chemical potential gradients, a net directional flux of solute and solvent atoms occurs to homogenize the system.
Part (b): Radioactive Tracer Diffusion Technique

To measure self-diffusion in pure nickel, a sub-micron film of radioactive isotope (e.g., $^{63}\text{Ni}$) is electroplated onto a polished pure nickel disc. The sample is annealed at temperature $T$ for time $t$. After cooling, thin parallel surface layers (a few micrometers thick) are sequentially shaved off using a precision lathe or microtome, and the radioactive activity of each layer is measured with a scintillation counter. Fitting the specific radioactivity profile $A(x)$ to the thin-film Gaussian solution:

$$A(x, t) = \frac{A_0}{\sqrt{\pi D t}} \exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{4Dt}\right)$$

plotting $\ln A$ versus $x^2$ gives a straight line whose slope $-\frac{1}{4Dt}$ yields the true self-diffusion coefficient $D^*$.

Part (c): Thermodynamic Driving Force and Uphill Diffusion

The true fundamental thermodynamic driving force for all diffusion is the gradient of chemical potential $\nabla\mu_i$, not the concentration gradient $\nabla C_i$:

$$J_i = -M_i C_i \frac{\partial\mu_i}{\partial x}$$

For ideal or dilute solutions, $\mu_i = \mu_i^0 + RT \ln C_i$, giving $\frac{\partial\mu_i}{\partial x} = \frac{RT}{C_i}\frac{\partial C_i}{\partial x}$, which reduces to Fick's first law $J = -D \frac{\partial C}{\partial x}$ ("downhill" diffusion from high to low $C$).

However, inside a thermodynamic miscibility gap (where $\frac{\partial^2 G}{\partial C^2} < 0$), the chemical potential gradient has the opposite sign of the concentration gradient. Atoms spontaneously diffuse from regions of low concentration toward regions of high concentration (uphill diffusion) during spinodal decomposition, phase-separating into A-rich and B-rich modulated nanostructures without nucleation.

Self-Diffusion: Random thermal motion in pure solids (zero net flux), measured via radioisotope tracers ($A \propto \exp(-x^2/4Dt)$).
Interdiffusion: Net directional transport in a concentration gradient.
Driving Force: Chemical potential gradient $\nabla\mu_i$. Uphill diffusion occurs when $\partial^2 G/\partial C^2 < 0$.
Problem 03 Diffusion Fundamentals Intermediate

The Kirkendall Effect: Marker Shift, Asymmetric Fluxes, and Vacancy Diffusion Proof

In 1947, Ernest Kirkendall published a landmark experiment on brass-copper diffusion couples that decisively proved the atomic mechanism of solid-state diffusion.

  1. Describe the experimental setup of the Kirkendall experiment (materials, inert markers, and diffusion heat treatment).
  2. What was the key experimental observation regarding the position of the inert markers after high-temperature annealing?
  3. Explain how this observation proved that:
    • Zinc and copper diffuse at different intrinsic rates ($D_{\text{Zn}} > D_{\text{Cu}}$).
    • Diffusion in metals occurs via the vacancy mechanism rather than direct ring/exchange mechanisms.
  4. What structural defect forms on the zinc-rich side of the couple as a result of the unequal atomic fluxes?
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): Experimental Setup of the Kirkendall Experiment

Ernest Kirkendall and Alice Smigelskas wrapped fine, chemically inert, insoluble molybdenum wires around a block of 70-30 brass ($70\text{ wt}\%\text{ Cu}, 30\text{ wt}\%\text{ Zn}$). They electroplated a thick layer of pure copper over the assembly, embedding the molybdenum wire markers directly at the original weld interface between brass and pure copper. The diffusion couple was then annealed at $785^\circ\text{C}$ for several weeks.

Part (b): Key Experimental Observation

Upon cross-sectioning and microscopic inspection, Kirkendall discovered that the inert molybdenum markers had moved inward toward the brass core. The distance between the marker wires on opposite sides had measurably decreased.

Part (c): Decisive Proof of Vacancy Mechanism and Unequal Fluxes
  1. Unequal Intrinsic Diffusion Rates ($D_{\text{Zn}} > D_{\text{Cu}}$): Zinc atoms (lower melting point, weaker bonds) diffuse out of the brass into the pure copper faster than copper atoms diffuse into the brass ($J_{\text{Zn}} > J_{\text{Cu}}$).
  2. Refutation of Direct Exchange: If diffusion occurred by direct pairwise exchange of adjacent atoms or ring rotation, every atom of Zn moving right would require exactly one atom of Cu moving left ($J_{\text{Zn}} = J_{\text{Cu}}$). Net mass flux across the interface would be zero, and markers could never move.
  3. Confirmation of Vacancy Mechanism: Because each atomic jump requires exchanging with a vacancy, the net outward atomic flux ($J_{\text{Zn}} - J_{\text{Cu}}$) must be balanced by an equal and opposite net flux of vacancies diffusing into the brass: $$J_v = -(J_{\text{Zn}} - J_{\text{Cu}})$$ As excess vacancies flow into the brass, lattice planes on the brass side are destroyed by vacancy annihilation (climb of edge dislocations). On the copper side, extra lattice planes are created by vacancy emission. The entire atomic lattice shifts rigidly relative to the inert markers at velocity $v = (D_{\text{Zn}} - D_{\text{Cu}})\frac{dX_{\text{Zn}}}{dx}$.
Part (d): Formation of Kirkendall Porosity

When the influx of vacancies into the faster-diffusing side (brass) exceeds the rate at which edge dislocations can climb and absorb them, the vacancy population becomes supersaturated. The excess vacancies condense and coalesce into macroscopic microscopic voids, termed Kirkendall voids (porosity). This causes embrittlement and delamination in microelectronic solder joints (e.g., Cu-Sn and Ni-Au interfaces in semiconductor packaging).

Kirkendall Observation: Inert markers shift toward the faster-diffusing element ($\text{Zn}$).
Scientific Proof: Proved $D_{\text{Zn}} > D_{\text{Cu}}$ and established the vacancy diffusion mechanism (refuting direct exchange).
Defect Consequence: Vacancy supersaturation produces Kirkendall porosity/voids on the faster-diffusing side.
Problem 04 Diffusion Fundamentals Advanced

Darken's Phenomenological Relations: Interdiffusion Coefficient $\tilde{D}$ and Marker Velocity

In 1948, L. S. Darken mathematically unified intrinsic diffusion coefficients with the macroscopic interdiffusion coefficient and marker shift velocity.

  1. State Darken's First Equation for the marker drift velocity $v$: $$v = (D_A - D_B) \frac{\partial X_A}{\partial x}$$ where $D_A$ and $D_B$ are the intrinsic diffusion coefficients of components $A$ and $B$, and $X_A$ is the mole fraction of $A$.
  2. State Darken's Second Equation for the chemical interdiffusion coefficient $\tilde{D}$: $$\tilde{D} = X_B D_A + X_A D_B$$
  3. In a Cu-Ni diffusion couple annealed at $1000^\circ\text{C}$, the concentration of Nickel at the marker interface is $X_{\text{Ni}} = 0.40$ ($X_{\text{Cu}} = 0.60$) and the concentration gradient is $\frac{\partial X_{\text{Ni}}}{\partial x} = 2.5 \times 10^3 \text{ m}^{-1}$. The intrinsic diffusion coefficients are $D_{\text{Cu}} = 4.0 \times 10^{-14} \text{ m}^2/\text{s}$ and $D_{\text{Ni}} = 1.0 \times 10^{-14} \text{ m}^2/\text{s}$.
    • Calculate the interdiffusion coefficient $\tilde{D}$.
    • Calculate the marker drift velocity $v$ in $\text{nm/s}$ and in $\mu\text{m/day}$.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a) & (b): Darken's Equations
  • Marker Velocity Equation: $$v = (D_A - D_B) \frac{\partial X_A}{\partial x} = -(D_A - D_B) \frac{\partial X_B}{\partial x}$$ where $v$ is the velocity of the inert marker frame relative to the fixed laboratory frame.
  • Chemical Interdiffusion Coefficient: $$\tilde{D} = X_B D_A + X_A D_B$$ For non-ideal solutions, Darken introduced the thermodynamic factor $\Phi$: $$\tilde{D} = (X_B D_A + X_A D_B) \left(1 + \frac{\partial \ln \gamma_A}{\partial \ln X_A}\right)$$
Part (c): Calculations for Cu-Ni Couple at $1000^\circ\text{C}$

Given $X_{\text{Cu}} = 0.60, X_{\text{Ni}} = 0.40$, $D_{\text{Cu}} = 4.0 \times 10^{-14} \text{ m}^2/\text{s}$, $D_{\text{Ni}} = 1.0 \times 10^{-14} \text{ m}^2/\text{s}$, and $\frac{\partial X_{\text{Ni}}}{\partial x} = 2.5 \times 10^3 \text{ m}^{-1}$ (meaning $\frac{\partial X_{\text{Cu}}}{\partial x} = -2.5 \times 10^3 \text{ m}^{-1}$):

  • Interdiffusion Coefficient $\tilde{D}$: $$\tilde{D} = X_{\text{Ni}} D_{\text{Cu}} + X_{\text{Cu}} D_{\text{Ni}} = (0.40 \times 4.0 \times 10^{-14}) + (0.60 \times 1.0 \times 10^{-14})$$ $$\tilde{D} = 1.60 \times 10^{-14} + 0.60 \times 10^{-14} = \mathbf{2.20 \times 10^{-14} \text{ m}^2/\text{s}}$$
  • Marker Velocity $v$: $$v = (D_{\text{Cu}} - D_{\text{Ni}}) \left(-\frac{\partial X_{\text{Cu}}}{\partial x}\right) = (D_{\text{Cu}} - D_{\text{Ni}}) \frac{\partial X_{\text{Ni}}}{\partial x}$$ $$v = (4.0 \times 10^{-14} - 1.0 \times 10^{-14} \text{ m}^2/\text{s}) \times (2.5 \times 10^3 \text{ m}^{-1})$$ $$v = (3.0 \times 10^{-14}) \times (2.5 \times 10^3) = \mathbf{7.50 \times 10^{-11} \text{ m/s}}$$

    Convert to $\text{nm/s}$ ($1 \text{ m} = 10^9 \text{ nm}$):

    $$v = (7.50 \times 10^{-11} \text{ m/s}) \times 10^9 \text{ nm/m} = \mathbf{0.075 \text{ nm/s}}$$

    Convert to $\mu\text{m/day}$ ($1 \text{ day} = 86,400 \text{ s}$, $1 \text{ m} = 10^6 \mu\text{m}$):

    $$v = (7.50 \times 10^{-11} \text{ m/s}) \times (86,400 \text{ s/day}) \times 10^6 \mu\text{m/m} = \mathbf{6.48 \mu\text{m/day}}$$
Darken's Relations: $\tilde{D} = X_B D_A + X_A D_B$, $v = (D_A - D_B)\frac{\partial X_A}{\partial x}$.
Interdiffusion Coefficient: $\tilde{D} = \mathbf{2.20 \times 10^{-14} \text{ m}^2/\text{s}}$.
Marker Velocity: $v = \mathbf{0.075 \text{ nm/s}} = \mathbf{6.48 \mu\text{m/day}}$ (inward toward Copper).
Problem 05 Steady-State Diffusion (Fick I) Fundamental

Steady-State Diffusion: Fick's First Law and Physical Meaning of Flux

Steady-state diffusion occurs when the concentration profile of the diffusing species does not change with time ($\frac{\partial C}{\partial t} = 0$).

  1. State Fick's First Law of diffusion mathematically: $$J = -D \frac{dC}{dx}$$ Define all four terms and state their standard SI units.
  2. Explain the physical significance of the negative sign in the equation.
  3. Distinguish between mass flux $J_m$ ($\text{kg}/(\text{m}^2\cdot\text{s})$) and atomic flux $J_{\text{at}}$ ($\text{atoms}/(\text{m}^2\cdot\text{s})$). State the conversion formula between them.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): Fick's First Law and SI Units
$$J = -D \frac{dC}{dx}$$
  • $J$: Diffusion Flux. The mass (or number of atoms) traversing perpendicular to a unit cross-sectional area per unit time. SI units: $\mathbf{\text{kg}/(\text{m}^2\cdot\text{s})}$ or $\mathbf{\text{atoms}/(\text{m}^2\cdot\text{s})}$.
  • $D$: Diffusion Coefficient (Diffusivity). The kinetic rate constant for atomic mobility in the host lattice. SI units: $\mathbf{\text{m}^2/\text{s}}$.
  • $C$: Concentration. The mass (or number of atoms) of solute per unit volume of host. SI units: $\mathbf{\text{kg/m}^3}$ or $\mathbf{\text{atoms/m}^3}$.
  • $\frac{dC}{dx}$: Concentration Gradient. The spatial rate of change of concentration along direction $x$. SI units: $\frac{\text{kg/m}^3}{\text{m}} = \mathbf{\text{kg/m}^4}$ or $\mathbf{\text{atoms/m}^4}$.
Part (b): Physical Meaning of the Negative Sign

Diffusion is a passive, spontaneous transport process driven down a concentration gradient. If concentration decreases with increasing distance ($x$), the spatial slope $\frac{dC}{dx}$ is negative. The negative sign ensures that the resultant net flux $J$ is a positive quantity pointing in the direction of lower concentration:

$$\frac{dC}{dx} < 0 \implies J = -D \left(-\left|\frac{dC}{dx}\right|\right) > 0 \quad (\text{Net mass flows from High to Low } C)$$
Part (c): Mass Flux vs. Atomic Flux Conversion

Let $M_{\text{solute}}$ be the molar mass ($\text{kg/mol}$) and $N_A = 6.022 \times 10^{23} \text{ atoms/mol}$ be Avogadro's number:

$$J_{\text{at}} = \frac{J_m \cdot N_A}{M_{\text{solute}}} \quad \left[\frac{\text{atoms}}{\text{m}^2\cdot\text{s}}\right]$$ $$J_m = \frac{J_{\text{at}} \cdot M_{\text{solute}}}{N_A} \quad \left[\frac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^2\cdot\text{s}}\right]$$
Fick's First Law: $$J = -D \frac{dC}{dx}$$ Units: $J \text{ [kg/(m}^2\cdot\text{s)]}$, $D \text{ [m}^2\text{/s]}$, $\frac{dC}{dx} \text{ [kg/m}^4\text{]}$.
Negative sign enforces transport down the gradient (from high to low concentration).
Problem 06 Steady-State Diffusion (Fick I) Intermediate

Steady-State Diffusion: Hydrogen Gas Purification Through a Palladium Foil

Palladium metal exhibits an extraordinary ability to selectively dissolve and transport hydrogen, making it the industry standard for producing ultra-pure hydrogen gas for semiconductor manufacturing.

  1. A palladium membrane of thickness $L = 0.20 \text{ mm}$ and cross-sectional area $A = 0.15 \text{ m}^2$ operates at $500^\circ\text{C}$. The diffusion coefficient of hydrogen in palladium at this temperature is $D = 1.6 \times 10^{-9} \text{ m}^2/\text{s}$. The dissolved hydrogen concentrations at the high-pressure and low-pressure surfaces are maintained constant at: $$C_A = 2.40 \text{ kg/m}^3 \quad (\text{at } x = 0), \quad C_B = 0.40 \text{ kg/m}^3 \quad (\text{at } x = L)$$ Calculate the steady-state hydrogen concentration gradient $\frac{dC}{dx}$ through the foil.
  2. Calculate the mass diffusion flux $J$ in $\text{kg}/(\text{m}^2\cdot\text{s})$ and the atomic flux $J_{\text{at}}$ in $\text{atoms}/(\text{m}^2\cdot\text{s})$ ($M_{\text{H}} = 1.008 \text{ g/mol}$).
  3. Calculate the total mass of ultra-pure hydrogen gas purified by the membrane per hour ($\text{kg/hr}$).
  4. If the foil thickness is rolled down to $L = 0.05 \text{ mm}$, calculate the new hourly purification rate.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): Steady-State Concentration Gradient

Under steady-state conditions in a flat planar membrane, the concentration profile is linear:

$$\frac{dC}{dx} = \frac{C_B - C_A}{L - 0} = \frac{0.40 \text{ kg/m}^3 - 2.40 \text{ kg/m}^3}{0.20 \times 10^{-3} \text{ m}} = \frac{-2.00 \text{ kg/m}^3}{2.0 \times 10^{-4} \text{ m}} = \mathbf{-10,000 \text{ kg/m}^4}$$
Part (b): Diffusion Flux Calculations

Using Fick's First Law ($J = -D \frac{dC}{dx}$):

$$J = -(1.6 \times 10^{-9} \text{ m}^2/\text{s}) \times (-10,000 \text{ kg/m}^4) = \mathbf{1.60 \times 10^{-5} \text{ kg}/(\text{m}^2\cdot\text{s})}$$

Calculate atomic flux using $M_{\text{H}} = 1.008 \times 10^{-3} \text{ kg/mol}$:

$$J_{\text{at}} = \frac{J \cdot N_A}{M_{\text{H}}} = \frac{(1.60 \times 10^{-5} \text{ kg/m}^2\cdot\text{s}) \times (6.022 \times 10^{23} \text{ atoms/mol})}{1.008 \times 10^{-3} \text{ kg/mol}} = \mathbf{9.56 \times 10^{21} \text{ atoms}/(\text{m}^2\cdot\text{s})}$$
Part (c): Total Purified Mass per Hour ($A = 0.15 \text{ m}^2$)

The mass flow rate $\dot{m}$ through area $A$ is:

$$\dot{m} = J \times A = (1.60 \times 10^{-5} \text{ kg/m}^2\cdot\text{s}) \times (0.15 \text{ m}^2) = 2.40 \times 10^{-6} \text{ kg/s}$$

In one hour ($t = 3600 \text{ s}$):

$$m_{\text{hr}} = \dot{m} \times 3600 \text{ s} = (2.40 \times 10^{-6} \text{ kg/s}) \times 3600 \text{ s} = \mathbf{8.64 \times 10^{-3} \text{ kg/hr} = 8.64 \text{ g/hr}}$$
Part (d): Effect of Reducing Thickness to $0.05 \text{ mm}$

Since flux is inversely proportional to thickness ($J \propto 1/L$):

$$\frac{L_1}{L_2} = \frac{0.20 \text{ mm}}{0.05 \text{ mm}} = 4.0$$ $$m_{\text{hr, new}} = 4.0 \times 8.64 \text{ g/hr} = \mathbf{34.56 \text{ g/hr}}$$

Reducing membrane thickness by a factor of 4 quadruples the purification throughput without increasing temperature.

Gradient: $\frac{dC}{dx} = \mathbf{-10,000 \text{ kg/m}^4}$
Flux: $J = \mathbf{1.60 \times 10^{-5} \text{ kg/(m}^2\cdot\text{s)}}$, $J_{\text{at}} = \mathbf{9.56 \times 10^{21} \text{ atoms/(m}^2\cdot\text{s)}}$.
Purification Rate: $\mathbf{8.64 \text{ g/hr}}$ at $0.20\text{ mm}$ • $\mathbf{34.56 \text{ g/hr}}$ at $0.05\text{ mm}$.
Problem 07 Steady-State Diffusion (Fick I) Intermediate

Steady-State Nitrogen Gas Permeation Through an Industrial Iron Plate

A chemical synthesis reactor contains ammonia gas at $700^\circ\text{C}$ separated from the atmosphere by an $\alpha$-iron plate of thickness $L = 5.0 \text{ mm}$.

Diffusion coefficient of Nitrogen in $\alpha$-iron at $700^\circ\text{C}$: $D = 4.2 \times 10^{-11} \text{ m}^2/\text{s}$
Nitrogen surface concentrations: $C_1 = 1.20 \text{ kg/m}^3$ (inside face), $C_2 = 0.10 \text{ kg/m}^3$ (outside face)
  1. Calculate the steady-state diffusion flux of nitrogen through the plate.
  2. Calculate the concentration of nitrogen at a depth of $x = 2.0 \text{ mm}$ from the inside high-pressure face.
  3. If the total surface area of the plate is $A = 0.80 \text{ m}^2$, calculate the mass of nitrogen lost to the atmosphere per 24-hour day in grams.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): Diffusion Flux Calculation

The linear concentration gradient is:

$$\frac{dC}{dx} = \frac{C_2 - C_1}{L} = \frac{0.10 - 1.20 \text{ kg/m}^3}{5.0 \times 10^{-3} \text{ m}} = \frac{-1.10}{5.0 \times 10^{-3}} = -220 \text{ kg/m}^4$$

Using Fick's First Law:

$$J = -D \frac{dC}{dx} = -(4.2 \times 10^{-11} \text{ m}^2/\text{s}) \times (-220 \text{ kg/m}^4) = \mathbf{9.24 \times 10^{-9} \text{ kg}/(\text{m}^2\cdot\text{s})}$$
Part (b): Concentration at Depth $x = 2.0 \text{ mm}$

For steady-state 1D diffusion, concentration varies linearly with position:

$$C(x) = C_1 + \left(\frac{dC}{dx}\right) x = 1.20 \text{ kg/m}^3 + (-220 \text{ kg/m}^4) \times (2.0 \times 10^{-3} \text{ m})$$ $$C(2.0 \text{ mm}) = 1.20 - 0.44 = \mathbf{0.76 \text{ kg/m}^3}$$
Part (c): Total Nitrogen Loss per Day ($A = 0.80 \text{ m}^2, t = 86,400 \text{ s}$)
$$m_{\text{day}} = J \times A \times t = (9.24 \times 10^{-9} \text{ kg/m}^2\cdot\text{s}) \times (0.80 \text{ m}^2) \times (86,400 \text{ s})$$ $$m_{\text{day}} = (7.392 \times 10^{-9} \text{ kg/s}) \times 86,400 \text{ s} = 6.387 \times 10^{-4} \text{ kg} = \mathbf{0.639 \text{ g/day}}$$
Diffusion Flux: $J = \mathbf{9.24 \times 10^{-9} \text{ kg/(m}^2\cdot\text{s)}}$.
Concentration at $2\text{ mm}$: $C = \mathbf{0.76 \text{ kg/m}^3}$.
Daily Nitrogen Loss: $m_{\text{day}} = \mathbf{0.639 \text{ g/day}}$.
Problem 08 Steady-State Diffusion (Fick I) Intermediate

Steady-State Carbon Flux in a Thin Austenitic Steel Sheet

A thin sheet of low-carbon steel of thickness $1.5 \text{ mm}$ is exposed to a carburizing atmosphere on one side and a decarburizing atmosphere on the other side at $1000^\circ\text{C}$ until steady-state diffusion is achieved.

At $x = 0$ (carburizing face): $C_1 = 1.15 \text{ wt}\%$ Carbon
At $x = 1.5 \text{ mm}$ (decarburizing face): $C_2 = 0.25 \text{ wt}\%$ Carbon
Diffusivity of Carbon in $\gamma$-Fe at $1000^\circ\text{C}$: $D = 3.0 \times 10^{-11} \text{ m}^2/\text{s}$
Density of austenite: $\rho = 7800 \text{ kg/m}^3$
  1. Convert the surface carbon concentrations from weight percent to volumetric concentrations ($C_1, C_2$ in $\text{kg/m}^3$).
  2. Calculate the carbon diffusion flux through the sheet in $\text{kg}/(\text{m}^2\cdot\text{s})$.
  3. Calculate the number of carbon atoms exiting $1.0 \text{ cm}^2$ of the decarburizing surface per second.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): Volumetric Concentration Conversion

Using $C [\text{kg/m}^3] = \left(\frac{\text{wt}\%}{100}\right) \times \rho_{\text{steel}}$:

$$C_1 = \left(\frac{1.15}{100}\right) \times 7800 \text{ kg/m}^3 = \mathbf{89.70 \text{ kg/m}^3}$$ $$C_2 = \left(\frac{0.25}{100}\right) \times 7800 \text{ kg/m}^3 = \mathbf{19.50 \text{ kg/m}^3}$$
Part (b): Diffusion Flux Calculation

Thickness $L = 1.5 \text{ mm} = 1.5 \times 10^{-3} \text{ m}$:

$$\frac{dC}{dx} = \frac{C_2 - C_1}{L} = \frac{19.50 - 89.70 \text{ kg/m}^3}{1.5 \times 10^{-3} \text{ m}} = \frac{-70.20}{1.5 \times 10^{-3}} = -46,800 \text{ kg/m}^4$$ $$J = -D \frac{dC}{dx} = -(3.0 \times 10^{-11} \text{ m}^2/\text{s}) \times (-46,800 \text{ kg/m}^4) = \mathbf{1.404 \times 10^{-6} \text{ kg}/(\text{m}^2\cdot\text{s})}$$

Radial Steady-State Diffusion: Mathematical Derivation for Cylindrical Pipe Walls

For cylindrical geometries (such as coolant pipes, fuel cladding tubes, or gas pipelines), diffusion occurs radially through an area that expands with radius $r$.

  1. Starting from Fick's first law and the condition that total mass flow rate $Q = J(r) \cdot A(r)$ must be independent of radius $r$ in steady state, derive the differential equation for radial concentration $C(r)$.
  2. Integrate the differential equation to prove that the steady-state concentration profile across a pipe wall (inner radius $r_1$, outer radius $r_2$) is logarithmic: $$C(r) = C_1 + (C_2 - C_1) \frac{\ln(r / r_1)}{\ln(r_2 / r_1)}$$
  3. Derive the expression for the total radial mass leakage rate $Q$ per unit length of pipe ($L = 1 \text{ m}$): $$\frac{Q}{L} = \frac{2\pi D (C_1 - C_2)}{\ln(r_2 / r_1)}$$
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): Differential Equation Formulation

Consider a pipe of length $L$. At radius $r$, the cylindrical surface area through which diffusion occurs is $A(r) = 2\pi r L$.

By Fick's first law, radial diffusion flux is $J(r) = -D \frac{dC}{dr}$.

The total mass flow rate $Q$ across the cylindrical shell is:

$$Q = J(r) \cdot A(r) = -D \left(\frac{dC}{dr}\right) (2\pi r L) = -2\pi D L r \frac{dC}{dr}$$

In steady state with no internal sources or sinks, conservation of mass requires $Q = \text{constant}$ (independent of $r$):

$$r \frac{dC}{dr} = -\frac{Q}{2\pi D L} = \text{constant } K$$
Part (b): Integration for Concentration Profile $C(r)$

Separating variables:

$$dC = K \frac{dr}{r} \implies C(r) = K \ln r + C_0$$

Apply boundary conditions:

  • At $r = r_1$: $C(r_1) = C_1 = K \ln r_1 + C_0$
  • At $r = r_2$: $C(r_2) = C_2 = K \ln r_2 + C_0$

Subtracting the two equations:

$$C_2 - C_1 = K (\ln r_2 - \ln r_1) = K \ln\left(\frac{r_2}{r_1}\right) \implies K = \frac{C_2 - C_1}{\ln(r_2 / r_1)}$$

Substituting $K$ and solving for $C(r)$:

$$C(r) - C_1 = K (\ln r - \ln r_1) = K \ln\left(\frac{r}{r_1}\right) = (C_2 - C_1) \frac{\ln(r / r_1)}{\ln(r_2 / r_1)}$$ $$C(r) = C_1 + (C_2 - C_1) \frac{\ln(r / r_1)}{\ln(r_2 / r_1)}$$
Part (c): Total Leakage Rate per Unit Length

From Part (a), $Q = -2\pi D L K$. Substituting $K = \frac{C_2 - C_1}{\ln(r_2 / r_1)}$:

$$Q = -2\pi D L \left[\frac{C_2 - C_1}{\ln(r_2 / r_1)}\right] = \frac{2\pi D L (C_1 - C_2)}{\ln(r_2 / r_1)}$$

Dividing by length $L$:

$$\frac{Q}{L} = \frac{2\pi D (C_1 - C_2)}{\ln(r_2 / r_1)}$$
Radial Profile: $$C(r) = C_1 + (C_2 - C_1)\frac{\ln(r/r_1)}{\ln(r_2/r_1)}$$ Total Radial Leakage per Meter: $$\frac{Q}{L} = \frac{2\pi D (C_1 - C_2)}{\ln(r_2/r_1)}$$ Unlike planar walls (linear profile), cylindrical diffusion profiles are fundamentally logarithmic.

Non-Steady-State Diffusion: Derivation of Fick's Second Law from Mass Continuity

In most real-world engineering processes (carburizing, oxidation, heat treatment), concentration varies with both position and time: $C = C(x, t)$.

  1. Consider a thin planar volume element of thickness $\Delta x$ and cross-sectional area $A$ oriented perpendicular to the diffusion direction $x$. Using the principle of conservation of mass (Rate of Accumulation = Influx - Outflux), derive the 1D continuity equation: $$\frac{\partial C}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial J}{\partial x}$$
  2. Substitute Fick's first law into the continuity equation to derive the general non-steady-state diffusion equation: $$\frac{\partial C}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(D \frac{\partial C}{\partial x}\right)$$
  3. State Fick's Second Law for the special case where diffusivity $D$ is independent of concentration and position: $$\frac{\partial C}{\partial t} = D \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial x^2}$$
  4. Under what physical conditions does diffusivity $D$ depend strongly on concentration, rendering the constant-$D$ assumption invalid?
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): Derivation from Mass Conservation

Consider an elemental volume $\Delta V = A \Delta x$ bounded by planes at $x$ and $x + \Delta x$ over time interval $\Delta t$:

  • Mass of diffusing solute entering through face at $x$: $m_{\text{in}} = J(x) \cdot A \cdot \Delta t$
  • Mass of diffusing solute leaving through face at $x + \Delta x$: $m_{\text{out}} = J(x + \Delta x) \cdot A \cdot \Delta t$
  • Net accumulation of solute inside the slice: $\Delta m = m_{\text{in}} - m_{\text{out}} = [J(x) - J(x + \Delta x)] A \Delta t$

The change in average concentration inside the volume is $\Delta C = \frac{\Delta m}{\Delta V} = \frac{\Delta m}{A \Delta x}$:

$$\Delta C = \frac{[J(x) - J(x + \Delta x)] A \Delta t}{A \Delta x} = -\frac{J(x + \Delta x) - J(x)}{\Delta x} \Delta t$$

Dividing by $\Delta t$ and taking the limit as $\Delta x \to 0$ and $\Delta t \to 0$:

$$\lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\Delta C}{\Delta t} = -\lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{J(x + \Delta x) - J(x)}{\Delta x} \implies \frac{\partial C}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial J}{\partial x}$$
Part (b) & (c): Substitution of Fick's First Law

From Fick's First Law, $J = -D \frac{\partial C}{\partial x}$. Substituting this flux expression into the continuity equation:

$$\frac{\partial C}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(-D \frac{\partial C}{\partial x}\right) = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(D \frac{\partial C}{\partial x}\right)$$

If the diffusion coefficient $D$ is constant (independent of $x$ and $C$):

$$\frac{\partial C}{\partial t} = D \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{\partial C}{\partial x}\right) = D \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial x^2}$$

This is Fick's Second Law, mathematically identical to Fourier's heat conduction equation.

Part (d): When $D = D(C)$ (Concentration-Dependent Diffusivity)

Diffusivity depends strongly on concentration when:

  1. High Solute Concentrations: In concentrated solid solutions (e.g., carbon $> 0.5 \text{ wt}\%$ in austenite, or Cu-Ni couples), dissolved solute alters the host lattice parameter and local bond stiffness, accelerating jump frequencies. In austenite, $D_{\text{C}}$ increases by a factor of 4 as carbon concentration rises from $0.1\%$ to $1.4\%$.
  2. Non-Ideal Thermodynamic Solutions: When activity coefficients vary strongly with concentration, Darken's thermodynamic factor $\left(1 + \frac{\partial \ln \gamma}{\partial \ln X}\right)$ causes $D$ to be highly concentration-dependent. (In such cases, the non-linear equation must be solved using the Boltzmann-Matano graphical method).
Continuity Equation: $$\frac{\partial C}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial J}{\partial x}$$ Fick's Second Law (Constant $D$): $$\frac{\partial C}{\partial t} = D \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial x^2}$$ $D = D(C)$ occurs in concentrated alloys where solute dilates the lattice or thermodynamics are non-ideal.

$$v = \frac{d\bar{x}}{dt} = \frac{d(2\sqrt{Dt})}{dt} = \sqrt{\frac{D}{t}} = \frac{2D}{\bar{x}}$$

$$\frac{x}{2\sqrt{Dt}} = \text{const} \implies \frac{x^2}{Dt} = \text{const}$$

  • At $T_1 = 900^\circ\text{C} = 1173 \text{ K}$: $$R T_1 = (8.314 \text{ J/(mol}\cdot\text{K)}) \times (1173 \text{ K}) = 9752.3 \text{ J/mol} = 9.7523 \text{ kJ/mol}$$ $$D_1 = (2.3 \times 10^{-5}) \exp\left(-\frac{148.0}{9.7523}\right) = (2.3 \times 10^{-5}) \exp(-15.1759)$$ $$D_1 = (2.3 \times 10^{-5}) \times (2.5656 \times 10^{-7}) = \mathbf{5.901 \times 10^{-12} \text{ m}^2/\text{s}}$$

$$\frac{D_2}{D_1} = \frac{1.9437 \times 10^{-11}}{5.901 \times 10^{-12}} = \mathbf{3.294}$$

Increasing temperature by just $100^\circ\text{C}$ speeds up carbon mobility by more than $3.3\times$!

For an identical concentration profile, the argument $z = \frac{x}{2\sqrt{Dt}}$ is constant for any depth $x$, which requires:

$$D_1 t_1 = D_2 t_2 \implies t_2 = t_1 \left(\frac{D_1}{D_2}\right)$$ $$t_2 = 10.0 \text{ hr} \times \left(\frac{1}{3.294}\right) = \mathbf{3.036 \text{ hours} = 3 \text{ hours and 2 minutes}}$$

The furnace cycle is reduced from 10 hours down to just 3 hours.

At $1000^\circ\text{C}$, the high thermal energy causes significant prior austenite grain growth (grain coarsening). (To mitigate this, microalloyed steels with fine niobium/titanium carbide precipitates are used to pin grain boundaries and prevent grain growth).

Substrate: $n$-type Silicon wafer with uniform background donor doping $C_0 = 1.0 \times 10^{15} \text{ atoms/cm}^3$

  2. Define the metallurgical junction depth $x_j$ and state the mathematical condition that defines it.
  3. Calculate the junction depth $x_j$ in micrometers ($\mu\text{m}$) after 30 minutes of pre-deposition.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): Concentration Profile for Pre-Deposition

Because the gas phase maintains a constant surface concentration $C_s$ during the entire pre-deposition step, the profile is given by the complementary error function ($\text{erfc}$):

$$C(x,t) = C_s \text{erfc}\left(\frac{x}{2\sqrt{Dt}}\right) = C_s \left[1 - \text{erf}\left(\frac{x}{2\sqrt{Dt}}\right)\right]$$

(Since $C_s = 5 \times 10^{19} \gg C_0 = 10^{15}$, the background concentration $C_0$ is completely negligible in the denominator).

Part (b): Definition of Metallurgical Junction Depth $x_j$

The metallurgical $p\text{-}n$ junction occurs at that precise depth $x_j$ where the concentration of diffused acceptor dopant atoms ($C_{\text{boron}}$) exactly equals the background donor dopant concentration ($C_0$):

$$C(x_j, t) = C_0 \implies C_s \text{erfc}\left(\frac{x_j}{2\sqrt{Dt}}\right) = C_0 \implies \text{erfc}\left(\frac{x_j}{2\sqrt{Dt}}\right) = \frac{C_0}{C_s}$$
Part (c): Junction Depth Calculation

Diffusion time: $t = 30.0 \times 60 = 1800 \text{ s}$.

$$2\sqrt{Dt} = 2 \sqrt{(1.40 \times 10^{-13} \text{ cm}^2/\text{s}) \times (1800 \text{ s})} = 2 \sqrt{2.52 \times 10^{-10} \text{ cm}^2} = 2 \times (1.5875 \times 10^{-5} \text{ cm}) = 3.175 \times 10^{-5} \text{ cm}$$

Evaluate concentration ratio:

$$\text{erfc}(z_j) = \frac{C_0}{C_s} = \frac{1.0 \times 10^{15} \text{ cm}^{-3}}{5.0 \times 10^{19} \text{ cm}^{-3}} = 2.0 \times 10^{-5}$$

For very small $\text{erfc}$ values ($\text{erfc}(z) \ll 1$), we use the asymptotic expansion $\text{erfc}(z) \approx \frac{e^{-z^2}}{z\sqrt{\pi}}$ or standard semiconductor tables:

  • For $\text{erfc}(z) = 2.0 \times 10^{-5} \implies z_j \approx \mathbf{3.045}$

Now solve for junction depth $x_j$:

$$x_j = z_j \times (2\sqrt{Dt}) = 3.045 \times (3.175 \times 10^{-5} \text{ cm}) = 9.668 \times 10^{-5} \text{ cm}$$

Convert to micrometers ($1 \text{ cm} = 10,000 \mu\text{m}$):

$$x_j = 9.668 \times 10^{-5} \times 10,000 = \mathbf{0.967 \mu\text{m}}$$
Pre-Deposition Profile: $$C(x,t) = C_s \text{erfc}\left(\frac{x}{2\sqrt{Dt}}\right)$$ Junction Condition: $C(x_j) = C_0 \implies \text{erfc}(z_j) = 2.0 \times 10^{-5} \implies z_j = 3.045$.
Junction Depth: $x_j = \mathbf{0.967 \mu\text{m}}$ ($\approx 0.97 \mu\text{m}$).

  1. State the total dopant dose per unit area $Q$ introduced during a pre-deposition step: $$Q = \int_0^\infty C(x) dx = \frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}} C_s \sqrt{D_{\text{pre}} t_{\text{pre}}}$$

    • Calculate the final junction depth $x_j$ in micrometers.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a) & (b): Pre-Deposition Dose and Gaussian Drive-In Solution

Integrating the pre-deposition complementary error function profile over all $x$ yields the total incorporated dose $Q$ ($\text{atoms/cm}^2$):

$$Q = \int_0^\infty C_s \text{erfc}\left(\frac{x}{2\sqrt{D_{\text{pre}} t_{\text{pre}}}}\right) dx = \frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}} C_s \sqrt{D_{\text{pre}} t_{\text{pre}}}$$

During drive-in, the wafer surface is sealed with an oxidizing ambient ($\text{SiO}_2$ capping layer), so no dopant enters or escapes (zero-flux boundary condition $\left.\frac{\partial C}{\partial x}\right|_{x=0} = 0$). Symmetrical reflection of the infinite Green's function yields the Gaussian half-space profile:

$$C(x, t) = \frac{Q}{\sqrt{\pi D t}} \exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{4Dt}\right)$$
Part (c): Calculations for $1100^\circ\text{C}$ Drive-In ($t = 2.0 \text{ hr} = 7200 \text{ s}$)

Given $Q = 1.80 \times 10^{15} \text{ atoms/cm}^2, D = 4.0 \times 10^{-13} \text{ cm}^2/\text{s}, t = 7200 \text{ s}$:

$$D t = (4.0 \times 10^{-13} \text{ cm}^2/\text{s}) \times (7200 \text{ s}) = 2.88 \times 10^{-9} \text{ cm}^2$$ $$\sqrt{\pi D t} = \sqrt{\pi \times (2.88 \times 10^{-9} \text{ cm}^2)} = \sqrt{9.0478 \times 10^{-9}} = 9.512 \times 10^{-5} \text{ cm}$$
  • Surface Concentration ($x = 0$): $$C(0, t) = \frac{Q}{\sqrt{\pi D t}} = \frac{1.80 \times 10^{15} \text{ cm}^{-2}}{9.512 \times 10^{-5} \text{ cm}} = \mathbf{1.892 \times 10^{19} \text{ atoms/cm}^3}$$ Notice that surface concentration has dropped from $5 \times 10^{19}$ to $1.89 \times 10^{19}$ as dopant atoms diffuse inward!
  • Final Junction Depth $x_j$ (where $C(x_j) = C_0 = 1.0 \times 10^{15} \text{ cm}^{-3}$): $$C_0 = C(0, t) \exp\left(-\frac{x_j^2}{4Dt}\right) \implies \exp\left(-\frac{x_j^2}{4Dt}\right) = \frac{C_0}{C(0, t)}$$ $$\exp\left(\frac{x_j^2}{4Dt}\right) = \frac{C(0, t)}{C_0} = \frac{1.892 \times 10^{19}}{1.0 \times 10^{15}} = 18,920$$ $$\frac{x_j^2}{4Dt} = \ln(18,920) = 9.848$$ $$x_j^2 = 9.848 \times (4Dt) = 9.848 \times (4 \times 2.88 \times 10^{-9} \text{ cm}^2) = 1.1345 \times 10^{-7} \text{ cm}^2$$ $$x_j = \sqrt{1.1345 \times 10^{-7} \text{ cm}^2} = 3.368 \times 10^{-4} \text{ cm} = \mathbf{3.37 \mu\text{m}}$$
Gaussian Drive-In: $$C(x,t) = \frac{Q}{\sqrt{\pi Dt}}\exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{4Dt}\right)$$ Surface Concentration: $C(0) = \mathbf{1.89 \times 10^{19} \text{ atoms/cm}^3}$.

Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): Analytical Solution for Infinite Diffusion Couple

For an infinite couple with initial conditions $C(x, 0) = C_1$ for $x < 0$ and $C(x, 0) = C_2$ for $x > 0$:

$$C(x,t) = \left(\frac{C_1 + C_2}{2}\right) - \left(\frac{C_1 - C_2}{2}\right) \text{erf}\left(\frac{x}{2\sqrt{Dt}}\right)$$

Equivalently written:

$$\frac{C(x,t) - C_1}{C_2 - C_1} = \frac{1}{2}\left[1 + \text{erf}\left(\frac{x}{2\sqrt{Dt}}\right)\right]$$
Part (b): Proof of Invariant Interface Concentration

Part (a): Derivation of Instantaneous Surface Flux $J_0(t)$

From the semi-infinite solution:

$$C(x,t) = C_0 + (C_s - C_0)\left[1 - \frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}} \int_0^{\frac{x}{2\sqrt{Dt}}} e^{-\eta^2} d\eta\right]$$

Differentiating with respect to $x$ using Leibniz's rule:

$$\frac{\partial C}{\partial x} = -(C_s - C_0) \frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}} \exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{4Dt}\right) \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{x}{2\sqrt{Dt}}\right) = -\frac{C_s - C_0}{\sqrt{\pi D t}} \exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{4Dt}\right)$$

Evaluating at the surface $x = 0$ ($\exp(0) = 1$):

$$\left.\frac{\partial C}{\partial x}\right|_{x=0} = -\frac{C_s - C_0}{\sqrt{\pi D t}}$$

Applying Fick's first law $J_0 = -D \left.\frac{\partial C}{\partial x}\right|_{x=0}$:

$$J_0(t) = -D \left(-\frac{C_s - C_0}{\sqrt{\pi D t}}\right) = (C_s - C_0) \frac{D}{\sqrt{\pi D t}} = \mathbf{(C_s - C_0) \sqrt{\frac{D}{\pi t}}}$$

At $t \to 0$, the concentration step is infinitely steep ($\partial C/\partial x \to -\infty$), yielding infinite initial flux. As a saturated boundary layer forms, the diffusion barrier thickens, causing flux to decay as $1/\sqrt{t}$.

Part (b): Derivation of Total Solute Mass Absorbed $M_t$

Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): The Arrhenius Equation and Units
$$D = D_0 \exp\left(-\frac{Q_d}{R T}\right)$$
  • $D$: Diffusion coefficient at absolute temperature $T$ ($\mathbf{\text{m}^2/\text{s}}$).
  • $D_0$: Temperature-independent pre-exponential factor ($\mathbf{\text{m}^2/\text{s}}$).
  • $Q_d$: Activation energy for diffusion ($\mathbf{\text{J/mol}}$ or $\mathbf{\text{kJ/mol}}$ when using gas constant $R = 8.314 \text{ J/(mol}\cdot\text{K)}$; or $\mathbf{\text{eV/atom}}$ when using Boltzmann's constant $k_B = 8.617 \times 10^{-5} \text{ eV/K}$).
  • $T$: Absolute temperature in Kelvin ($\mathbf{\text{K}}$).
Part (b): Microscopic Physical Meaning of $D_0$ and $Q_d$
  • Pre-exponential Factor $D_0$: From random walk theory, $D = \frac{1}{6} \alpha z a^2 \Gamma$, where $a$ is jump distance, $z$ is coordination number, and $\Gamma$ is successful jump frequency. Transition state theory expresses $D_0$ as: $$D_0 = \frac{1}{6} \alpha z a^2 \nu_0 \exp\left(\frac{\Delta S}{R}\right)$$ where $\nu_0$ is the Debye vibrational frequency ($\sim 10^{13} \text{ s}^{-1}$) and $\Delta S$ is the activation entropy due to lattice distortion during the atomic jump.
  • Activation Energy $Q_d$: The potential energy barrier that an atom must overcome to execute a successful jump:
    • For substitutional / self-diffusion: $Q_d = \Delta H_f + \Delta H_m$ (energy to form a vacancy plus energy to move into it).
    • For interstitial diffusion: $Q_d = \Delta H_m$ (energy to migrate through interstitial bottlenecks; vacancy formation energy is zero).
Part (c): Arrhenius Plot Interpretation

Taking the natural logarithm of the Arrhenius equation:

$$\ln D = \ln D_0 - \left(\frac{Q_d}{R}\right) \frac{1}{T}$$

This matches the standard linear form $y = m x + c$, where $y = \ln D$ and $x = \frac{1}{T}$:

  • Slope: $m = -\frac{Q_d}{R} \implies Q_d = -R \times \text{slope}$.
  • $y$-Intercept: $c = \ln D_0 \implies D_0 = e^c$.
Arrhenius Law: $$D = D_0 \exp\left(-\frac{Q_d}{RT}\right), \quad \ln D = \ln D_0 - \frac{Q_d}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T}\right)$$ Slope $= -Q_d/R$ • Intercept $= \ln D_0$.
$Q_d = \Delta H_f + \Delta H_m$ for substitutional; $Q_d = \Delta H_m$ for interstitial.

Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): Diffusivity Hierarchy (Fastest to Slowest)
$$D_{\text{surface}} > D_{\text{grain boundary}} > D_{\text{dislocation pipe}} \gg D_{\text{lattice / volume}}$$
  • External Surfaces: Atoms are bonded only to the layer below; open 2D space offers minimal geometric resistance.
  • Grain Boundaries: Disordered transition zone containing excess free volume, dilated channels, and reduced bond coordination.
  • Dislocation Cores: 1D pipe containing an extra half-plane termination with high core strain and dilated vacancy-like channels.
  • Bulk Lattice: Perfect close-packed crystal; atoms are constrained by full coordination numbers ($CN = 12$).
Part (b): Activation Energy Hierarchy (Lowest to Highest)
$$Q_{\text{surface}} < Q_{\text{grain boundary}} < Q_{\text{dislocation pipe}} < Q_{\text{lattice}}$$

Typical empirical metallurgical rules of thumb:

  • $Q_{\text{surface}} \approx 0.25\text{ to }0.35 \times Q_L$
  • $Q_{\text{grain boundary}} \approx 0.40\text{ to }0.60 \times Q_L \approx \mathbf{0.5 Q_L}$
  • $Q_{\text{dislocation pipe}} \approx 0.60\text{ to }0.70 \times Q_L$
  • $Q_{\text{lattice}} \equiv Q_L$
Part (c): Why Lattice Diffusion Dominates at High Temperature

The total mass transport is the product of diffusivity and the cross-sectional area fraction of each path:

Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): Origin of the Internal Ambipolar Electric Field

Cations are smaller than anions and generally possess much higher individual diffusion coefficients ($D_+ \gg D_-$). If cations diffused freely down a concentration gradient ahead of anions, positive charge would accumulate at the leading edge, leaving negative charge behind. To prevent macroscopic charge separation and uphold strict electroneutrality, an internal electrostatic potential gradient (electric field $E = -\nabla\phi$) is established. This field retards the faster-moving cations and accelerates the slower-moving anions, forcing both ions to migrate at a single, coupled mutual rate.

Part (b): Derivation of Ambipolar Diffusivity $D_{\text{amb}}$

The total flux of each ion under combined concentration gradient and electric field $E$ is given by the Nernst-Planck equation:

$$J_+ = -D_+ \frac{dC_+}{dx} + \frac{z_+ e D_+ C_+}{k_B T} E$$ $$J_- = -D_- \frac{dC_-}{dx} - \frac{z_- e D_- C_-}{k_B T} E$$

To prevent electrical current flow ($I = e(z_+ J_+ - z_- J_-) = 0$), electroneutrality requires $z_+ J_+ = z_- J_-$. Solving for the electric field $E$ and substituting back gives the coupled ambipolar flux:

$$J = -D_{\text{amb}} \frac{dC}{dx} \quad \text{where} \quad D_{\text{amb}} = \frac{(z_+ + z_-) D_+ D_-}{z_+ D_+ + z_- D_-}$$
Part (c): Calculation for $\text{MgO}$ ($z_+ = z_- = 2$)

For symmetric valence $z_+ = z_- = 2$:

$$D_{\text{amb}} = \frac{(2 + 2) D_+ D_-}{2 D_+ + 2 D_-} = \frac{4 D_+ D_-}{2(D_+ + D_-)} = \frac{2 D_+ D_-}{D_+ + D_-}$$

Since $D_{\text{Mg}} = 10^{-14} \text{ m}^2/\text{s} \gg D_{\text{O}} = 10^{-17} \text{ m}^2/\text{s}$:

$$D_+ + D_- \approx D_+ \implies D_{\text{amb}} \approx \frac{2 D_+ D_-}{D_+} = 2 D_-$$ $$D_{\text{amb}} = \frac{2 \times (1.0 \times 10^{-14}) \times (1.0 \times 10^{-17})}{1.0 \times 10^{-14} + 1.0 \times 10^{-17}} = \frac{2.0 \times 10^{-31}}{1.001 \times 10^{-14}} = \mathbf{1.998 \times 10^{-17} \text{ m}^2/\text{s} \approx 2 D_{\text{O}^{2-}}}$$
Part (d): Rate-Controlling Species in Sintering and Creep

Macroscopic mass transport processes (sintering, creep, grain boundary sliding) require stoichiometric formula units ($\text{Mg}^{2+} + \text{O}^{2-}$) to move together to build or remove lattice planes. You cannot sinter $\text{MgO}$ by moving only magnesium; oxygen ions must move as well. Because the ambipolar diffusion coefficient is pinned to the slower species ($D_{\text{amb}} \approx 2 D_{\text{O}}$), the slower-diffusing oxygen ion ($\text{O}^{2-}$) is the strict rate-controlling bottleneck for all high-temperature deformation and sintering of $\text{MgO}$.

Part (b) & (c): Flux and Black's Equation

The net effective charge is defined as $Z^* = Z_{\text{direct}} + Z_{\text{wind}}$ (typically negative, $Z^* \approx -1\text{ to }-10$):

$$J_{\text{EM}} = \frac{C D}{k_B T} Z^* e E = \frac{C D}{k_B T} Z^* e (\rho_e j)$$

In 1969, J. R. Black formulated the standard reliability lifetime equation:

$$\text{MTTF} = \frac{A}{j^n} \exp\left(\frac{E_a}{k_B T}\right) \quad (n \approx 1.5\text{--}2.0)$$
Part (d): Overclocking Lifetime Calculation

Given $\text{MTTF}_1 = 10 \text{ years}$, $j_1 = 1.0 \times 10^6 \text{ A/cm}^2, T_1 = 378 \text{ K}$, $j_2 = 1.5 \times 10^6 \text{ A/cm}^2, T_2 = 398 \text{ K}$, $E_a = 0.90 \text{ eV}$, $n = 2$:

$$\frac{\text{MTTF}_2}{\text{MTTF}_1} = \left(\frac{j_1}{j_2}\right)^n \exp\left[\frac{E_a}{k_B}\left(\frac{1}{T_2} - \frac{1}{T_1}\right)\right]$$
  • Current density factor: $$\left(\frac{j_1}{j_2}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{1.0}{1.5}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^2 = \mathbf{0.4444}$$
  • Thermal factor: $$\frac{1}{T_2} - \frac{1}{T_1} = \frac{1}{398} - \frac{1}{378} = 2.5126 \times 10^{-3} - 2.6455 \times 10^{-3} = -1.329 \times 10^{-4} \text{ K}^{-1}$$ $$\frac{E_a}{k_B} \Delta(1/T) = \frac{0.90 \text{ eV}}{8.617 \times 10^{-5} \text{ eV/K}} \times (-1.329 \times 10^{-4} \text{ K}^{-1}) = 10,444.5 \times (-1.329 \times 10^{-4}) = -1.388$$ $$\exp(-1.388) = \mathbf{0.2496}$$

Combined lifetime ratio:

$$\frac{\text{MTTF}_2}{\text{MTTF}_1} = 0.4444 \times 0.2496 = \mathbf{0.1109}$$ $$\text{MTTF}_2 = 10 \text{ years} \times 0.1109 = \mathbf{1.11 \text{ years}}$$

A $50\%$ increase in current density combined with a $20^\circ\text{C}$ temperature rise slashes the microprocessor lifetime from 10 years down to just 1.1 years (an $89\%$ reduction in reliability!).

Problem 49 Short-Circuit Paths & Microstructure Intermediate

Thermomigration (Soret Effect): Thermal Gradient Driven Atomic Transport

Thermomigration (the Soret effect) is directional atomic mass transport driven by steep temperature gradients ($\nabla T$).

  1. State the atomic flux equation for thermomigration in a temperature gradient $\frac{dT}{dx}$: $$J_{\text{TM}} = -\frac{C D Q^*}{R T^2} \frac{dT}{dx}$$ Define the heat of transport $Q^*$ and explain what determines whether solute atoms migrate toward the hotter region ($Q^* < 0$) or the colder region ($Q^* > 0$).
  2. In nuclear reactor uranium dioxide fuel pellets ($\text{UO}_2$), an extreme temperature gradient of $\nabla T = 5000^\circ\text{C/cm}$ ($5.0 \times 10^5 \text{ K/m}$) exists between the centerline ($2000^\circ\text{C}$) and the cooled surface ($800^\circ\text{C}$). Explain how thermomigration drives oxygen redistribution and the formation of a central void.
  3. In microelectronic flip-chip solder bumps operating at high power densities, temperature gradients can exceed $1000^\circ\text{C/cm}$. How does thermomigration cause voiding and open-circuit failures?
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): Thermomigration Flux and Heat of Transport $Q^*$
$$J_{\text{TM}} = -\frac{C D Q^*}{R T^2} \frac{dT}{dx}$$

The heat of transport $Q^*$ represents the net thermal energy carried by an atom per mole during an atomic jump, minus the enthalpy of the atom on its lattice site:

  • If $Q^* > 0$ (Positive Heat of Transport): The diffusing atom transports excess heat forward. Energetically, jumping toward the colder side lowers free energy: atoms migrate down the thermal gradient from HOT to COLD.
  • If $Q^* < 0$ (Negative Heat of Transport): Atoms migrate up the thermal gradient from COLD to HOT (common for interstitial carbon and nitrogen in certain refractory metals).
Part (b): Centerline Void Formation in Nuclear Fuel Pins ($\text{UO}_2$)

In operating $\text{UO}_2$ nuclear reactor fuel rods, the thermal gradient is immense ($\sim 5 \times 10^5 \text{ K/m}$). Pores and bubbles migrate rapidly up the thermal gradient toward the hot center via an evaporation-condensation mechanism (vaporizing at the hot side of the pore and condensing on the cold side). As millions of pores migrate inward, they coalesce at the cylinder axis, forming a hollow, continuous central void tunnel surrounded by columnar grain structures.

Part (c): Thermomigration in Flip-Chip Solder Bumps

In high-power semiconductor packaging, Joule heating concentrates at tiny contact corners where current enters the solder bump, generating steep localized temperature gradients ($\nabla T > 1000^\circ\text{C/cm}$ over a $50 \mu\text{m}$ bump). Under this driving force, tin and lead atoms migrate rapidly from the hot silicon chip side toward the colder organic substrate side. This leaves behind a dense band of coalesced vacancy voids at the hot interface, culminating in sudden electrical open-circuit failure.

Thermomigration Flux: $$J_{\text{TM}} = -\frac{CDQ^*}{RT^2}\frac{dT}{dx}$$ $Q^* > 0 \implies$ transport to COLD • $Q^* < 0 \implies$ transport to HOT.
Drives central hole formation in nuclear fuel pellets and voiding in microelectronic solder bumps.
Problem 50 Short-Circuit Paths & Microstructure Advanced

Comprehensive Diffusion Synthesis: Multi-Stage Industrial Carburizing and Microstructure Control

An automotive powertrain manufacturer produces heavy-duty truck drive pinions from AISI 8620 steel ($C_0 = 0.20 \text{ wt}\%$). The specifications require a surface carbon content of $0.85 \text{ wt}\%$ (to prevent brittle retained austenite) and an effective case depth of $1.00 \text{ mm}$ (where $C = 0.40 \text{ wt}\%$).

Two-Stage "Boost-Diffuse" Industrial Carburizing Cycle:
  • Stage 1 (Boost Stage at $950^\circ\text{C}$): A rich carburizing gas holds surface carbon at the austenite solubility limit $C_s = 1.20 \text{ wt}\%$ for $t_1 = 4.0 \text{ hours}$. ($D_1 = 1.28 \times 10^{-11} \text{ m}^2/\text{s}$).
  • Stage 2 (Diffuse Stage at $950^\circ\text{C}$): The gas atmosphere carbon potential is lowered to hold the surface at $C_s = 0.85 \text{ wt}\%$ while carbon continues diffusing deeper into the core for duration $t_2$.
  1. Calculate the carbon content at depth $x = 1.00 \text{ mm}$ at the conclusion of Stage 1 ($t_1 = 4.0 \text{ hr}$). Is the required specification met by Stage 1 alone?
  2. Calculate the total mass of carbon absorbed per square meter of gear surface ($M_t$ in $\text{g/m}^2$) during the 4-hour Boost stage.
  3. Explain the metallurgical necessity of the two-stage "Boost-Diffuse" process: Why would quenching directly from a single-stage carburization at $1.20 \text{ wt}\%$ carbon cause excessive retained austenite and microcracking?
  4. Synthesize how short-circuit grain boundary diffusion during the final oil quench influences the depth of martensite transformation.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): Carbon Content after Stage 1 (Boost Stage, $t_1 = 4.0 \text{ hr} = 14,400 \text{ s}$)

Given $C_0 = 0.20 \text{ wt}\%, C_s = 1.20 \text{ wt}\%, D_1 = 1.28 \times 10^{-11} \text{ m}^2/\text{s}, x = 1.00 \text{ mm} = 1.0 \times 10^{-3} \text{ m}$:

$$2\sqrt{D_1 t_1} = 2 \sqrt{(1.28 \times 10^{-11} \text{ m}^2/\text{s}) \times (14,400 \text{ s})} = 2 \sqrt{1.8432 \times 10^{-7} \text{ m}^2} = 2 \times (4.2932 \times 10^{-4} \text{ m}) = 8.5865 \times 10^{-4} \text{ m}$$ $$z = \frac{x}{2\sqrt{D_1 t_1}} = \frac{1.0 \times 10^{-3} \text{ m}}{8.5865 \times 10^{-4} \text{ m}} = \mathbf{1.1646}$$

Using standard error function values: $\text{erf}(1.10) = 0.8802, \text{erf}(1.20) = 0.9103$:

$$\text{erf}(1.1646) = 0.8802 + \left(\frac{1.1646 - 1.10}{0.10}\right) \times (0.9103 - 0.8802) = 0.8802 + 0.646 \times (0.0301) = 0.8802 + 0.0194 = \mathbf{0.8996}$$ $$\frac{C(1.00 \text{ mm}) - 0.20}{1.20 - 0.20} = 1 - \text{erf}(z) = 1 - 0.8996 = 0.1004$$ $$C(1.00 \text{ mm}) = 0.20 + 0.1004(1.00) = \mathbf{0.300 \text{ wt}\%}$$

Specification Check: At $1.00 \text{ mm}$, the carbon content is only $0.30 \text{ wt}\%$, which falls short of the required $0.40 \text{ wt}\%$. The specification is not met by Stage 1 alone; additional diffuse time is mandatory.

Part (b): Total Carbon Absorbed during Boost Stage ($M_t$)

Using the integrated mass uptake formula from Problem 25:

$$\Delta C = (1.20 - 0.20 \text{ wt}\%) \times 7800 \text{ kg/m}^3 = 78.0 \text{ kg/m}^3$$ $$M_t = 2 \Delta C \sqrt{\frac{D_1 t_1}{\pi}} = 2 \times (78.0) \times \sqrt{\frac{1.8432 \times 10^{-7} \text{ m}^2}{\pi}} = 156.0 \times \sqrt{5.867 \times 10^{-8}} = 156.0 \times (2.422 \times 10^{-4} \text{ m})$$ $$M_t = 0.03779 \text{ kg/m}^2 = \mathbf{37.79 \text{ g/m}^2}$$
Part (c): Metallurgical Necessity of Boost-Diffuse Cycle

If the gear were quenched directly with $1.20 \text{ wt}\%$ surface carbon:

  1. High dissolved carbon depresses the martensite finish temperature ($M_f$) far below room temperature ($M_f \approx -50^\circ\text{C}$). Upon oil quenching to $25^\circ\text{C}$, the transformation cannot complete, leaving $30\text{--}50\%$ soft, ductile retained austenite at the surface. Retained austenite degrades surface hardness from 62 HRC down to $< 52 \text{ HRC}$ and causes catastrophic contact fatigue spalling.
  2. Excessive carbon causes continuous proeutectoid cementite ($\text{Fe}_3\text{C}$) networks to precipitate along prior austenite grain boundaries during cooling, leading to severe embrittlement.
  3. The Diffuse stage lowers surface carbon to the eutectoid composition ($0.80\text{--}0.85\text{ wt}\%$), raising $M_s/M_f$ to ensure $100\%$ transformation to hard plate martensite while driving carbon deeper to satisfy the $1.00 \text{ mm}$ case depth specification.
Part (d): Microstructure Synthesis During Quenching

During the rapid quench, carbon diffusion is kinetically frozen in the bulk lattice, trapping carbon into octahedral interstitial sites to force the diffusionless FCC $\to$ BCT martensite transformation. However, in the lower-carbon core ($0.20 \text{ wt}\%$, high $M_s \approx 420^\circ\text{C}$), the transformation begins earlier, forming high-toughness lath martensite and bainite. The resulting composite component possesses a wear-resistant, high-hardness case ($62 \text{ HRC}$) under compressive residual stress, supported by a ductile, fracture-resistant core ($32 \text{ HRC}$).

Stage 1 Result: $C(1.0\text{ mm}) = \mathbf{0.30 \text{ wt}\%}$ (spec not met; diffuse stage required).
Carbon Uptake: $M_t = \mathbf{37.79 \text{ g/m}^2}$ absorbed in Boost stage.
Boost-Diffuse Rationale: Lowers surface carbon to $0.85\text{ wt}\%$ to eliminate soft retained austenite and grain-boundary carbide networks while driving carbon to $1.0\text{ mm}$ depth.
