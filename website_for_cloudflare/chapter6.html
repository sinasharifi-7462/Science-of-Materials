Engineering Stress and Strain: Uniaxial Tension Definitions and Units

The standard uniaxial tensile test is the primary mechanical characterization method utilized to assess the structural load-bearing capacity of engineering materials.

  1. State the definitions and mathematical formulas for:
    • Engineering Stress ($\sigma$).
    • Engineering Strain ($\varepsilon$).
    Define original cross-sectional area $A_0$, instantaneous applied tensile load $F$, original gauge length $l_0$, and instantaneous gauge length $l$. State standard SI and Imperial units.
  2. A cylindrical tensile specimen of AISI 1045 steel has an initial gauge diameter $d_0 = 12.80 \text{ mm}$ and initial gauge length $l_0 = 50.00 \text{ mm}$. Under a tensile load of $F = 45.0 \text{ kN}$, the gauge length elongates to $l = 50.085 \text{ mm}$. Calculate the engineering stress $\sigma$ in megapascals ($\text{MPa}$) and the engineering strain $\varepsilon$ (dimensionless and in percent).
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): Mathematical Definitions and Units
  • Engineering Stress ($\sigma$): The instantaneous tensile load $F$ divided by the original, undeformed cross-sectional area $A_0$: $$\sigma = \frac{F}{A_0} \quad \left[\frac{\text{N}}{\text{m}^2} = \text{Pa}, \text{ commonly reported in MPa } (1 \text{ MPa} = 10^6 \text{ N/m}^2 = 1 \text{ N/mm}^2) \text{ or psi / ksi}\right]$$
  • Engineering Strain ($\varepsilon$): The change in gauge length $\Delta l = l - l_0$ divided by the original gauge length $l_0$: $$\varepsilon = \frac{\Delta l}{l_0} = \frac{l - l_0}{l_0} \quad [\text{dimensionless, or m/m, in/in, or percentage } \varepsilon \times 100\%]$$
Part (b): Calculations for AISI 1045 Steel Specimen

Initial cross-sectional area ($d_0 = 12.80 \text{ mm} = 12.80 \times 10^{-3} \text{ m}$):

$$A_0 = \frac{\pi d_0^2}{4} = \frac{\pi (12.80 \text{ mm})^2}{4} = \frac{\pi \times 163.84}{4} = 128.68 \text{ mm}^2 = 1.2868 \times 10^{-4} \text{ m}^2$$

Engineering stress ($F = 45.0 \text{ kN} = 45,000 \text{ N}$):

$$\sigma = \frac{F}{A_0} = \frac{45,000 \text{ N}}{1.2868 \times 10^{-4} \text{ m}^2} = 3.497 \times 10^8 \text{ Pa} = \mathbf{349.7 \text{ MPa}}$$

Engineering strain ($l_0 = 50.00 \text{ mm}, l = 50.085 \text{ mm}$):

$$\Delta l = 50.085 - 50.000 = 0.085 \text{ mm}$$ $$\varepsilon = \frac{\Delta l}{l_0} = \frac{0.085 \text{ mm}}{50.00 \text{ mm}} = \mathbf{1.70 \times 10^{-3} = 0.170\%}$$
Engineering Stress: $\sigma = \frac{F}{A_0} = \mathbf{349.7 \text{ MPa}}$.
Engineering Strain: $\varepsilon = \frac{\Delta l}{l_0} = \mathbf{1.70 \times 10^{-3}} = \mathbf{0.170\%}$.
Problem 02 Elastic Deformation & Moduli Fundamental

Hooke's Law in Tension: Young's Modulus of Elasticity and Atomic Bonding

For most engineering metals subjected to low stresses, deformation is linear and completely reversible (elastic).

  1. State Hooke's Law in 1D tension: $$\sigma = E \varepsilon$$ Define the Modulus of Elasticity (Young's Modulus $E$) and state its standard units.
  2. Explain the microscopic physical origin of Young's modulus in terms of the interatomic potential energy well $E_N(r)$ and interatomic force curve $F(r)$. Show that $E \propto \left.\frac{d^2 E_N}{dr^2}\right|_{r_0}$.
  3. Rank metals, ceramics, and polymers in order of decreasing Young's modulus, and explain why heating a metal causes its modulus of elasticity to decrease.
  4. Using the data from Problem 1 ($\sigma = 349.7 \text{ MPa}$ at $\varepsilon = 1.70 \times 10^{-3}$ in the linear elastic regime), calculate Young's modulus $E$ for the steel specimen in gigapascals ($\text{GPa}$).
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): Hooke's Law and Young's Modulus
$$\sigma = E \varepsilon \implies E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}$$

Young's Modulus $E$ represents the mechanical stiffness of a material—its resistance to elastic elongation under applied tensile stress. Standard units: $\text{Pa}$ or $\mathbf{\text{GPa}}$ ($1 \text{ GPa} = 10^9 \text{ Pa} = 10^3 \text{ MPa}$).

Part (b): Atomic Origin of Elastic Modulus

Under small elastic tensile strains, external stress slightly displaces atoms from their equilibrium separation distance $r_0$ against attractive and repulsive interatomic forces. The restoring force per unit displacement is the interatomic force constant $k_0$:

$$k_0 = \left.\frac{dF}{dr}\right|_{r_0} = \left.\frac{d^2 E_N}{dr^2}\right|_{r_0}$$

Because macroscopic stress scales as force per atomic area ($\sigma \sim F/r_0^2$) and strain scales as fractional displacement ($\varepsilon \sim \Delta r / r_0$):

$$E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon} \sim \frac{F / r_0^2}{\Delta r / r_0} = \frac{1}{r_0} \left(\frac{F}{\Delta r}\right) = \frac{k_0}{r_0} = \frac{1}{r_0} \left.\frac{d^2 E_N}{dr^2}\right|_{r_0}$$

Thus, Young's modulus is directly proportional to the curvature (steepness) of the interatomic potential energy well at the equilibrium separation $r_0$.

Part (c): Materials Ranking and Temperature Effect
  • Ranking: $\mathbf{\text{Ceramics} > \text{Metals} > \text{Polymers}}$
    • Ceramics (e.g., Diamond $1000\text{ GPa}$, SiC $450\text{ GPa}$, Alumina $380\text{ GPa}$): Ultra-strong directional covalent and ionic bonds produce extremely steep, deep potential wells.
    • Metals (e.g., Tungsten $410\text{ GPa}$, Steel $207\text{ GPa}$, Copper $110\text{ GPa}$, Aluminum $70\text{ GPa}$): Strong non-directional metallic bonds.
    • Polymers (e.g., Nylon $2\text{--}4\text{ GPa}$, Polyethylene $0.2\text{--}1.0\text{ GPa}$): Intermolecular bonding is governed by weak secondary van der Waals forces, yielding shallow potential wells.
  • Temperature Dependence: As temperature rises, increased thermal vibration amplitudes expand the average interatomic separation ($r > r_0$). At larger separation, the curvature of the potential energy well $\frac{d^2 E_N}{dr^2}$ decreases, causing Young's modulus to decline monotonically with temperature ($dE/dT < 0$).
Part (d): Calculation for Steel Specimen
$$E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon} = \frac{349.7 \times 10^6 \text{ Pa}}{1.70 \times 10^{-3}} = 2.057 \times 10^{11} \text{ Pa} = \mathbf{205.7 \text{ GPa}}$$

This matches the standard canonical textbook value for structural carbon steels ($E \approx 207 \text{ GPa}$).

Hooke's Law: $\sigma = E \varepsilon \implies E = \frac{1}{r_0}\left.\frac{d^2E_N}{dr^2}\right|_{r_0}$.
Stiffness Ranking: $\text{Ceramics} > \text{Metals} \gg \text{Polymers}$.
Steel Modulus: $E = \mathbf{205.7 \text{ GPa}}$.
Problem 03 Elastic Deformation & Moduli Fundamental

Lateral Elastic Contraction and Poisson's Ratio: Theoretical Bounds and Values

When a solid rod is pulled in tension, its longitudinal elongation is accompanied by a simultaneous lateral contraction.

  1. Define Poisson's Ratio ($\nu$) mathematically for uniaxial loading along the $z$-axis: $$\nu = -\frac{\varepsilon_{\text{lateral}}}{\varepsilon_{\text{axial}}} = -\frac{\varepsilon_x}{\varepsilon_z} = -\frac{\varepsilon_y}{\varepsilon_z}$$ Why is the negative sign included in the definition?
  2. State the theoretical thermodynamic bounds for Poisson's ratio in isotropic solids ($-1 \le \nu \le 0.5$). Explain the physical significance of $\nu = 0.5$ (incompressibility) and $\nu = 0$ (cork).
  3. What are auxetic materials ($\nu < 0$), and what unusual geometric behavior do they exhibit when pulled in tension?
  4. A cylindrical copper bar ($E = 110 \text{ GPa}, \nu = 0.34$) with initial diameter $d_0 = 10.000 \text{ mm}$ is loaded in tension by $F = 15.0 \text{ kN}$ within its elastic limit. Calculate the change in diameter $\Delta d$ in micrometers.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): Definition of Poisson's Ratio $\nu$
$$\nu = -\frac{\varepsilon_{\text{lateral}}}{\varepsilon_{\text{axial}}}$$

Under uniaxial tension, the axial strain is positive ($\varepsilon_{\text{axial}} > 0$), while the lateral dimensions contract, making lateral strain negative ($\varepsilon_{\text{lateral}} < 0$). The negative sign is explicitly inserted so that Poisson's ratio is a positive dimensionless number for conventional engineering materials.

Part (b): Theoretical Bounds for Isotropic Solids ($-1 \le \nu \le 0.5$)

From continuum elasticity, positive strain energy requires that both shear modulus $G = \frac{E}{2(1+\nu)} > 0$ and bulk modulus $K = \frac{E}{3(1-2\nu)} > 0$. This imposes the strict mathematical bounds:

$$-1.0 \le \nu \le 0.5$$
  • $\nu = 0.5$ (Perfect Incompressibility): The material deforms with zero change in volume ($\Delta V / V = 0$). Typical of rubber, elastomers, and plastically deforming metals.
  • $\nu = 0$ (Zero Lateral Strain): Uniaxial tension causes elongation with zero lateral contraction. Typical of cellular cork (allowing wine corks to be pushed into bottles without expanding radially).
  • Common Metals: Most engineering metals exhibit $\nu \approx 0.28\text{ to }0.35$ (e.g., steel $\sim 0.30$, aluminum $\sim 0.33$, copper $\sim 0.34$).
Part (c): Auxetic Materials ($\nu < 0$)

Auxetic materials possess negative Poisson's ratios ($\nu < 0$). When pulled in longitudinal tension, their lateral dimensions expand perpendicularly rather than contracting (due to re-entrant re-folding cellular microstructures). Used in blast-resistant armor, gore-tex vascular stents, and high-energy absorption footwear.

Part (d): Calculation for Copper Bar ($d_0 = 10.00 \text{ mm}, F = 15.0 \text{ kN}$)
$$A_0 = \frac{\pi (10.00 \times 10^{-3} \text{ m})^2}{4} = 7.854 \times 10^{-5} \text{ m}^2$$ $$\sigma_z = \frac{F}{A_0} = \frac{15,000 \text{ N}}{7.854 \times 10^{-5} \text{ m}^2} = 1.9099 \times 10^8 \text{ Pa} = 191.0 \text{ MPa}$$

Axial strain along $z$:

$$\varepsilon_z = \frac{\sigma_z}{E} = \frac{1.9099 \times 10^8 \text{ Pa}}{110 \times 10^9 \text{ Pa}} = 1.7363 \times 10^{-3}$$

Lateral strain along diameter:

$$\varepsilon_{\text{lat}} = -\nu \varepsilon_z = -(0.34) \times (1.7363 \times 10^{-3}) = -5.903 \times 10^{-4}$$

Change in diameter $\Delta d$:

$$\Delta d = d_0 \cdot \varepsilon_{\text{lat}} = (10.00 \text{ mm}) \times (-5.903 \times 10^{-4}) = -5.903 \times 10^{-3} \text{ mm} = \mathbf{-5.90 \mu\text{m}}$$

The diameter contracts by $5.90$ micrometers.

Poisson's Ratio: $\nu = -\frac{\varepsilon_{\text{lat}}}{\varepsilon_{\text{axial}}}$ (Bounds: $-1.0 \le \nu \le 0.5$).
Physical Limits: $\nu = 0.5$ (incompressible rubber) • $\nu = 0$ (cork) • $\nu < 0$ (auxetic expansion).
Lateral Contraction: $\Delta d = \mathbf{-5.90 \mu\text{m}}$.
Problem 04 Elastic Deformation & Moduli Intermediate

Elastic Constants: Shear Modulus $G$, Bulk Modulus $K$, and Volumetric Strain

For an isotropic linear elastic solid, all elastic behaviors are completely defined by any two independent elastic constants.

  1. State the mathematical definitions of:
    • Shear Modulus ($G$) relating shear stress $\tau$ to shear strain $\gamma$.
    • Bulk Modulus ($K$) relating hydrostatic pressure $P$ to fractional volume change $\Delta V / V_0$.
  2. State the isotropic elasticity relationships linking $G$ and $K$ to Young's modulus $E$ and Poisson's ratio $\nu$: $$G = \frac{E}{2(1 + \nu)}, \quad K = \frac{E}{3(1 - 2\nu)}$$
  3. Prove that the volumetric strain (dilatation $e = \Delta V / V_0$) under uniaxial tensile stress $\sigma_z$ is given by: $$e = \frac{\Delta V}{V_0} = \varepsilon_z (1 - 2\nu) = \frac{\sigma_z}{E}(1 - 2\nu)$$ Confirm that for $\nu = 0.5$, volumetric strain is identically zero.
  4. Titanium has $E = 107 \text{ GPa}$ and $\nu = 0.34$. Calculate its Shear Modulus $G$ and Bulk Modulus $K$ in gigapascals.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): Definitions of $G$ and $K$
  • Shear Modulus ($G$): The ratio of shear stress $\tau$ to engineering shear strain $\gamma$: $$\tau = G \gamma \implies G = \frac{\tau}{\gamma} \quad [\text{GPa}]$$
  • Bulk Modulus ($K$): The ratio of uniform hydrostatic pressure $P = -\sigma_h$ to the resulting fractional volumetric compression: $$P = -K \left(\frac{\Delta V}{V_0}\right) \implies K = -\frac{P}{\Delta V / V_0} \quad [\text{GPa}]$$
Part (b): Interrelationships in Isotropic Elasticity
$$G = \frac{E}{2(1 + \nu)}, \quad K = \frac{E}{3(1 - 2\nu)}, \quad E = \frac{9KG}{3K + G}$$
Part (c): Proof of Volumetric Strain Equation

Consider an infinitesimal cube of dimensions $L_0 \times L_0 \times L_0$ subjected to uniaxial tensile stress along $z$. The principal strains are:

$$\varepsilon_z = \frac{\sigma_z}{E}, \quad \varepsilon_x = -\nu\varepsilon_z, \quad \varepsilon_y = -\nu\varepsilon_z$$

The deformed volume is:

$$V = L_0(1 + \varepsilon_x) \cdot L_0(1 + \varepsilon_y) \cdot L_0(1 + \varepsilon_z) = V_0 (1 - \nu\varepsilon_z)(1 - \nu\varepsilon_z)(1 + \varepsilon_z)$$

Expanding and neglecting higher-order strain products ($\varepsilon^2 \approx 0$ for small elastic deformations):

$$\frac{V}{V_0} \approx 1 + \varepsilon_x + \varepsilon_y + \varepsilon_z = 1 - \nu\varepsilon_z - \nu\varepsilon_z + \varepsilon_z = 1 + \varepsilon_z(1 - 2\nu)$$ $$\frac{\Delta V}{V_0} = \frac{V - V_0}{V_0} = \mathbf{\varepsilon_z (1 - 2\nu) = \frac{\sigma_z}{E}(1 - 2\nu)}$$

If $\nu = 0.5$, then $(1 - 2\nu) = 1 - 2(0.5) = 0 \implies \Delta V / V_0 \equiv 0$ (perfectly constant volume).

Part (d): Calculations for Titanium ($E = 107 \text{ GPa}, \nu = 0.34$)
  • Shear Modulus ($G$): $$G = \frac{E}{2(1 + \nu)} = \frac{107 \text{ GPa}}{2(1 + 0.34)} = \frac{107}{2.68} = \mathbf{39.93 \text{ GPa}}$$
  • Bulk Modulus ($K$): $$K = \frac{E}{3(1 - 2\nu)} = \frac{107 \text{ GPa}}{3(1 - 2 \times 0.34)} = \frac{107}{3(1 - 0.68)} = \frac{107}{3(0.32)} = \frac{107}{0.96} = \mathbf{111.46 \text{ GPa}}$$
Isotropic Relations: $G = \frac{E}{2(1+\nu)}$, $K = \frac{E}{3(1-2\nu)}$, $\frac{\Delta V}{V_0} = \varepsilon_z(1-2\nu)$.
Titanium Moduli: $G = \mathbf{39.9 \text{ GPa}}$, $K = \mathbf{111.5 \text{ GPa}}$.
Problem 05 Yielding & Tensile Parameters Fundamental

Plastic Yielding: The 0.2% Strain Offset Yield Strength Construction

Because most engineering metals exhibit a gradual transition from elastic to plastic deformation without an abrupt kink, yield strength is standardized using the 0.2% offset method.

  1. Explain the detailed geometric procedure for determining the 0.2% Offset Yield Strength ($\sigma_y$ or $\sigma_{0.2}$) from an engineering stress-strain curve according to ASTM E8.
  2. Why is a plastic offset of $\varepsilon_{\text{offset}} = 0.002$ ($0.2\%$) chosen rather than $0.000$ or $0.05$?
  3. The linear elastic portion of a tensile curve for an aluminum alloy ($E = 70.0 \text{ GPa}$) passes through the origin. Plastic yielding begins gradually. The measured total engineering strain at a stress of $\sigma = 310 \text{ MPa}$ is $\varepsilon_{\text{total}} = 0.00643$. Determine whether the material has reached, exceeded, or not yet reached its 0.2% offset yield point at this stress.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): Geometric Construction of the 0.2% Offset Yield Strength
  1. Plot the experimental engineering stress-strain curve ($\sigma$ vs. $\varepsilon$).
  2. Determine the slope of the initial linear elastic region, which defines Young's modulus $E$.
  3. On the strain axis ($\sigma = 0$), locate the point corresponding to a permanent offset strain of $\varepsilon = 0.002$ ($0.2\%$).
  4. Draw a straight construction line starting from $(0.002, 0)$ directed parallel to the linear elastic line (slope $= E$).
  5. The intersection point where this parallel offset line crosses the stress-strain curve defines the 0.2% offset yield strength ($\sigma_y$ or $\sigma_{0.2}$).
Part (b): Why $\varepsilon_{\text{offset}} = 0.002$ ($0.2\%$) Was Chosen

True microscopic plastic deformation begins at the proportional limit ($\sigma_{pl}$), where the curve first deviates from linearity. However, measuring $\sigma_{pl}$ is highly subjective and depends strongly on extensometer sensitivity and noise. A small offset of $0.002$ ($0.2\%$) represents a tiny, acceptable permanent plastic deformation that can be reproduced with high precision across different laboratories and testing machines, providing an unambiguous engineering design criterion.

Part (c): Evaluation for Aluminum Alloy ($\sigma = 310 \text{ MPa}, \varepsilon_{\text{total}} = 0.00643$)

Total strain is the sum of recoverable elastic strain and permanent plastic strain:

$$\varepsilon_{\text{total}} = \varepsilon_{\text{elastic}} + \varepsilon_{\text{plastic}}$$

By Hooke's law, the elastic strain at $\sigma = 310 \text{ MPa}$ ($E = 70.0 \text{ GPa} = 70,000 \text{ MPa}$) is:

$$\varepsilon_{\text{elastic}} = \frac{\sigma}{E} = \frac{310 \text{ MPa}}{70,000 \text{ MPa}} = 0.004429$$

Calculate the plastic strain:

$$\varepsilon_{\text{plastic}} = \varepsilon_{\text{total}} - \varepsilon_{\text{elastic}} = 0.00643 - 0.004429 = \mathbf{0.002001 \approx 0.00200}$$

Because the plastic strain is precisely $\varepsilon_{\text{plastic}} = 0.00200$ ($0.200\%$), this stress point lies exactly on the offset line! Therefore, the 0.2% offset yield strength of this alloy is precisely $\mathbf{\sigma_y = 310 \text{ MPa}}$.

0.2% Offset Method: Construct line from $\varepsilon = 0.002$ parallel to elastic slope $E$; intersection defines $\sigma_y$.
Aluminum Specimen: $\varepsilon_{\text{plastic}} = 0.00643 - 0.00443 = \mathbf{0.00200} \implies \sigma_y = \mathbf{310 \text{ MPa}}$.
Problem 06 Yielding & Tensile Parameters Intermediate

The Yield Point Phenomenon: Upper/Lower Yield Points, Lüders Bands, and Cottrell Pinning

Certain structural alloys, most notably low-carbon mild steels, exhibit an anomalous yield point phenomenon with distinct upper and lower yield points.

  1. Differentiate between:
    • Upper Yield Point ($\sigma_{\text{uy}}$)
    • Lower Yield Point ($\sigma_{\text{ly}}$)
    • Yield Point Elongation (YPE)
  2. Explain the microscopic mechanism responsible for the upper yield point using Cottrell atmosphere dislocation pinning by interstitial carbon and nitrogen atoms.
  3. Describe what happens microstructurally during the flat yield point elongation region. What are Lüders bands (stretcher strains)?
  4. In design engineering, which yield point ($\sigma_{\text{uy}}$ or $\sigma_{\text{ly}}$) is used as the design parameter, and why?
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): Yield Point Phenomenon Definitions
  • Upper Yield Point ($\sigma_{\text{uy}}$): The temporary peak stress where plastic deformation first initiates. After this peak, the stress drops abruptly without increasing load. Highly sensitive to testing machine stiffness and specimen alignment.
  • Lower Yield Point ($\sigma_{\text{ly}}$): The stable, relatively constant plateau stress at which plastic deformation propagates across the specimen gauge length.
  • Yield Point Elongation (YPE): The heterogeneous plastic strain that accumulates at constant lower yield stress before continuous strain hardening commences (typically $1\text{--}4\%$ strain).
Part (b): Dislocation Pinning Mechanism (Cottrell Atmospheres)

In low-carbon steel, interstitial carbon and nitrogen atoms segregate to the dilated tensile regions beneath edge dislocation cores, forming dense solute clouds known as Cottrell atmospheres. These solute atmospheres anchor (pin) dislocations firmly in place. To initiate plastic flow, an exceptionally high stress ($\sigma_{\text{uy}}$) must be applied to tear dislocations away from their pinning solute atmospheres. Once torn free, these unpinned dislocations multiply catastrophically and glide with extreme ease at a much lower glide resistance, causing the stress to drop abruptly to the lower yield point ($\sigma_{\text{ly}}$).

Part (c): Lüders Bands Propagation (Yield Point Elongation)

During the yield point elongation, deformation does not occur uniformly throughout the specimen. Instead, one or more localized bands of plastic shear (oriented at approximately $45^\circ\text{--}50^\circ$ to the tensile axis) nucleate at stress concentrators (such as specimen shoulders) and propagate steadily along the gauge section at constant lower yield stress. These visible surface markings are termed Lüders bands or stretcher-strain marks. Once the entire gauge length has been swept by Lüders bands, uniform strain hardening begins.

Part (d): Design Engineering Practice

Structural engineering design codes universally specify the lower yield strength ($\sigma_{\text{ly}}$) as the design allowable. The upper yield point is unreliable because minor vibrations, slight specimen misalignment, or tiny surface scratches can prematurely trigger dislocation unpinning, dropping the load-bearing capacity immediately to the lower yield stress.

Upper Yield: Dislocation breakout from Cottrell atmospheres • Lower Yield: Mobile dislocation glide.
Lüders Bands: Heterogeneous shear bands propagating along gauge section during yield point elongation.
Design Standard: Lower yield strength $\sigma_{\text{ly}}$ is strictly used for engineering design safety.
Problem 07 Yielding & Tensile Parameters Intermediate

Elimination of Stretcher Strains: Skin-Pass Temper Rolling and Dynamic Strain Aging

In the automotive stamping of outer body panels (hoods, fenders, doors), the formation of visible Lüders bands during deep drawing results in unacceptable surface roughness (orange-peel effect).

  1. Explain how skin-pass (temper) rolling (a light cold rolling pass of $0.5\text{--}1.5\%$ reduction) eliminates the yield point phenomenon and suppresses stretcher strains in deep-drawing steels.
  2. What is strain aging (static strain aging), and why must temper-rolled steel sheets be stamped into automotive body panels within a few weeks of rolling?
  3. How does the addition of small amounts of Titanium or Aluminum (Interstitial-Free / IF steels) prevent strain aging permanently?
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): Mechanism of Skin-Pass (Temper) Rolling

Skin-pass rolling applies a tiny cold reduction of $0.5\text{--}1.5\%$ between smooth steel rolls. This slight plastic deformation breaks all pinned dislocations free from their Cottrell atmospheres throughout the sheet and introduces a small population of fresh, mobile dislocations ($\sim 10^9 \text{ cm}^{-2}$). When the temper-rolled sheet is subsequently stamped in a press, these pre-existing mobile dislocations immediately glide uniformly across the entire part. The stress-strain curve shows a smooth, continuous elastic-to-plastic transition with zero yield point drop and zero Lüders bands, producing flawless, mirror-smooth automotive outer panels.

Part (b): Static Strain Aging During Storage

If temper-rolled steel coils sit in a warehouse at room temperature ($25^\circ\text{C}$) for several weeks or months, dissolved interstitial carbon and nitrogen atoms diffuse slowly through the ferrite lattice ($D_{\text{N}} \sim 10^{-20} \text{ m}^2/\text{s}$). Driven by elastic strain relief, they migrate to the newly created dislocations and re-form Cottrell atmospheres, pinning them once again. As a consequence, the sharp yield point and yield point elongation spontaneously return, accompanied by an increase in yield strength and a loss of ductility. If stamped after aging, the sheet develops severe stretcher-strain wrinkles and may tear during deep drawing.

Part (c): Interstitial-Free (IF) Steels

In modern Interstitial-Free (IF) steels, tiny microalloying additions of Titanium and Niobium (e.g., $0.02\text{ wt}\%\text{ Ti/Nb}$) are added during vacuum degassing. Titanium and Niobium have an immense chemical affinity for carbon and nitrogen, precipitating all interstitial solutes as ultra-stable microscopic carbo-nitrides ($\text{TiN}, \text{TiC}, \text{NbC}$). This purges the ferrite matrix of free interstitial carbon and nitrogen ($C_{\text{free}} < 0.001\text{ wt}\%$). Without dissolved interstitials, Cottrell atmospheres cannot form, rendering the steel completely immune to strain aging indefinitely.

Skin-Pass Rolling ($0.5\text{--}1.5\%$): Unpins dislocations, eliminating yield drop and stretcher strains.
Strain Aging: Interstitial diffusion re-pins dislocations after weeks of storage, restoring Lüders bands.
IF Steels: Microalloying with $\text{Ti/Nb}$ precipitates all free interstitials, preventing strain aging permanently.
Problem 08 Yielding & Tensile Parameters Fundamental

Ultimate Tensile Strength (UTS): Maximum Load Capacity and the Onset of Necking

The Ultimate Tensile Strength (UTS) represents the peak engineering stress sustained by a material during a uniaxial tensile test.

  1. Define Tensile Strength (UTS / $\sigma_u$) mathematically in terms of maximum applied load $F_{\max}$ and original area $A_0$.
  2. Explain the physical competition between strain hardening and geometric cross-sectional area reduction that produces the maximum on the engineering stress-strain curve.
  3. Prove that the mathematical condition defining the peak of the engineering stress-strain curve is: $$\left.\frac{d\sigma}{d\varepsilon}\right|_u = 0 \iff \left.\frac{dF}{dl}\right|_{\max} = 0$$ and that this point marks the transition from uniform elongation to localized necking.
  4. A cylindrical aluminum tensile bar ($d_0 = 12.8 \text{ mm}$) sustains a maximum tensile load of $F_{\max} = 54.2 \text{ kN}$ before necking begins. Calculate the Ultimate Tensile Strength in megapascals.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): Definition of Ultimate Tensile Strength
$$\sigma_{\text{UTS}} = \sigma_u = \frac{F_{\max}}{A_0} \quad [\text{MPa}]$$

where $F_{\max}$ is the absolute maximum tensile load registered by the load cell, and $A_0$ is the original, undeformed cross-sectional area of the gauge section.

Part (b): Physical Competition Mechanism

During plastic deformation, two opposing physical effects govern the load-bearing capacity $F = \sigma_T A$:

  1. Strain Hardening: Dislocation multiplication and interaction increase the intrinsic flow stress $\sigma_T$ of the metal, enabling it to support greater load.
  2. Cross-Sectional Area Reduction: As the specimen elongates in tension, its instantaneous cross-sectional area $A$ continuously shrinks (Poisson contraction and volume conservation), reducing the physical amount of material carrying the load.

Prior to UTS, strain hardening outpaces area reduction ($d\sigma_T / \sigma_T > |dA| / A$), so the required load $F$ increases, and deformation remains perfectly uniform along the gauge length. At UTS, the rate of area reduction exactly balances the rate of strain hardening ($dF = 0$). Beyond UTS, geometric area reduction dominates, and deformation concentrates into a localized zone (necking).

Part (c): Mathematical Condition at Maximum Load

Since engineering stress is $\sigma = F/A_0$ and original area $A_0$ is constant:

$$\frac{d\sigma}{d\varepsilon} = \frac{1}{A_0} \frac{dF}{d\varepsilon} = \frac{l_0}{A_0} \frac{dF}{dl}$$

At the maximum load point $F = F_{\max}$, the load derivative is zero: $\left.\frac{dF}{dl}\right|_{\max} = 0$. Therefore:

$$\left.\frac{d\sigma}{d\varepsilon}\right|_u = 0$$

Before this point, plastic deformation is uniformly distributed over the entire gauge length ($l_0$). At and beyond this point, plastic strain localizes within a narrow necking zone; the engineering stress drops because the load cell registers falling load, even though the true local stress in the neck continues to rise until fracture.

Part (d): Calculation for Aluminum Bar ($d_0 = 12.8 \text{ mm}, F_{\max} = 54.2 \text{ kN}$)

True Stress and True Strain: Physical Distinction from Engineering Parameters

Engineering stress and strain are based on initial undeformed dimensions, obscuring the true physical state of the metal during large plastic deformation.

  1. State the mathematical definitions of:
    • True Stress ($\sigma_T$) in terms of instantaneous load $F$ and instantaneous cross-sectional area $A_i$.
    • True Strain ($\varepsilon_T$) defined as the integral of incremental strain increments $dl/l$ from original length $l_0$ to instantaneous length $l_i$: $$\varepsilon_T = \int_{l_0}^{l_i} \frac{dl}{l} = \ln\left(\frac{l_i}{l_0}\right)$$
  2. Explain why the engineering stress-strain curve exhibits an artificial downward drop after necking, whereas the true stress-strain curve continues to rise monotonically until fracture.
  3. Explain why true strains are additive ($\varepsilon_{T, \text{total}} = \varepsilon_{T, 1} + \varepsilon_{T, 2}$), whereas engineering strains are not.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): Mathematical Definitions of True Stress and Strain
  • True Stress ($\sigma_T$): The instantaneous tensile load $F$ divided by the actual instantaneous cross-sectional area $A_i$ carrying that load: $$\sigma_T = \frac{F}{A_i} \quad [\text{MPa}]$$
  • True Strain ($\varepsilon_T$, Hencky / Logarithmic Strain): The sum of all incremental strain changes referenced to the instantaneous length $l$ at each step of deformation: $$\varepsilon_T = \int_{l_0}^{l_i} \frac{dl}{l} = \left[\ln l\right]_{l_0}^{l_i} = \ln\left(\frac{l_i}{l_0}\right)$$
Part (b): Why Engineering Stress Drops While True Stress Rises

Engineering stress is calculated as $\sigma = F / A_0$, dividing instantaneous load $F$ by the fixed original area $A_0$. Once localized necking begins at UTS, the cross-sectional area at the neck drops much faster than the rate of strain hardening. Consequently, the total load $F$ required to continue deforming the shrinking neck decreases, causing the engineering stress curve to curve downward.

True stress $\sigma_T = F / A_{\text{neck}}$ divides by the true local area of the neck, which is shrinking rapidly. Because dislocations continue to multiply and tangle within the neck, the metal continues to work-harden right up to the final microsecond of fracture. Thus, the true stress $\sigma_T$ rises monotonically throughout the entire test.

Part (c): Additivity of True Strain

Suppose a metal bar is deformed from length $l_0$ to $l_1$, and then subsequently deformed from $l_1$ to $l_2$:

$$\varepsilon_{T, \text{step 1}} = \ln\left(\frac{l_1}{l_0}\right), \quad \varepsilon_{T, \text{step 2}} = \ln\left(\frac{l_2}{l_1}\right)$$ $$\varepsilon_{T, 1} + \varepsilon_{T, 2} = \ln\left(\frac{l_1}{l_0}\right) + \ln\left(\frac{l_2}{l_1}\right) = \ln\left(\frac{l_1}{l_0} \cdot \frac{l_2}{l_1}\right) = \ln\left(\frac{l_2}{l_0}\right) = \mathbf{\varepsilon_{T, \text{total}}}$$

For engineering strain: $\varepsilon_1 = \frac{l_1 - l_0}{l_0}, \varepsilon_2 = \frac{l_2 - l_1}{l_1}$. The sum $\varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2 \neq \frac{l_2 - l_0}{l_0}$ because the reference baseline changes from $l_0$ to $l_1$. The logarithmic nature of true strain makes it mathematically rigorous and additive across multi-step metal forming operations (rolling, drawing, forging).

True Stress & Strain: $$\sigma_T = \frac{F}{A_i}, \quad \varepsilon_T = \ln\left(\frac{l_i}{l_0}\right)$$ Engineering stress drop after necking is an artifact of dividing by fixed $A_0$; true stress rises continuously.
True strains are strictly additive: $\varepsilon_{T, \text{total}} = \sum \varepsilon_{T, i}$.

Post-Necking Plasticity: Triaxial Stress States and the Bridgman Correction Factor

Once localized necking forms in a cylindrical tensile bar, the geometry of the neck alters the internal stress field from simple uniaxial tension to a complex triaxial state.

  1. Explain why the geometric curvature of the neck profile induces radial ($\sigma_r$) and circumferential ($\sigma_\theta$) tensile stresses in the interior of the neck, creating a hydrostatic tensile stress state ($\sigma_h > 0$) along the specimen centerline.
  2. Why does the apparent average true stress $\sigma_{T, \text{avg}} = F / A_{\text{neck}}$ overestimate the true equivalent flow stress $\bar{\sigma}$ required for plastic dislocation slip?
  3. State Bridgman's Correction Formula for calculating the true equivalent flow stress $\bar{\sigma}$: $$\bar{\sigma} = \frac{\sigma_{T, \text{avg}}}{\left(1 + \frac{2R}{a}\right) \ln\left(1 + \frac{a}{2R}\right)}$$ defining the neck radius $a$ and profile radius of curvature $R$.
  4. At fracture, a steel tensile bar has a neck radius $a = 4.0 \text{ mm}$ and neck curvature radius $R = 8.0 \text{ mm}$. The fracture load is $F_f = 60.0 \text{ kN}$. Calculate the uncorrected average true fracture stress $\sigma_{T, \text{avg}}$ and the true Bridgman-corrected flow stress $\bar{\sigma}$.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): Origin of Triaxial Stresses in the Neck

Before necking, the specimen surface is straight and parallel; outer surface elements are traction-free, ensuring a strictly 1D uniaxial stress state ($\sigma_z = \sigma, \sigma_r = \sigma_\theta = 0$). When a curved neck forms, the longitudinal stress trajectories bend inward following the concave profile. To satisfy differential equilibrium of forces in cylindrical coordinates ($\frac{\partial\sigma_r}{\partial r} + \frac{\sigma_r - \sigma_\theta}{r} + \frac{\partial\tau_{rz}}{\partial z} = 0$), radial and hoop tensile stresses ($\sigma_r = \sigma_\theta > 0$) must develop inside the neck. These lateral tensile stresses reach their maximum at the center axis ($r = 0$), generating an intense triaxial hydrostatic tensile stress state ($\sigma_h = \frac{\sigma_z + 2\sigma_r}{3} > 0$) that drives ductile microvoid cavitation and initiates cup-and-cone fracture at the center.

Part (b): Overestimation by Average True Stress

According to the von Mises yield criterion, plastic dislocation glide is driven purely by deviatoric shear stresses ($\sigma_{\text{eff}} = \sigma_z - \sigma_r$), completely unaffected by hydrostatic tension $\sigma_h$. Because the lateral stresses $\sigma_r$ and $\sigma_\theta$ are positive (tensile), a higher longitudinal stress $\sigma_z$ is required to achieve the same effective shear stress $(\sigma_z - \sigma_r = \bar{\sigma})$. The simple average $\sigma_{T, \text{avg}} = F / A$ measures total longitudinal load, thus significantly overestimating the true material yield flow stress.

Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): The Hollomon Power-Law Equation
$$\sigma_T = K \varepsilon_T^n$$
  • $\sigma_T$: True flow stress ($\mathbf{\text{MPa}}$).
  • $\varepsilon_T$: True plastic strain (dimensionless).
  • $K$: Strength Coefficient. Represents the true stress required to sustain a true plastic strain of $\varepsilon_T = 1.0$ (since $1.0^n = 1$). Units: $\mathbf{\text{MPa}}$.
  • $n$: Strain-Hardening Exponent (dimensionless, typically $0.10 \le n \le 0.50$). It measures the ability of a metal to work-harden and resist strain localization under increasing plastic strain: $$n = \frac{d(\ln\sigma_T)}{d(\ln\varepsilon_T)} = \frac{\varepsilon_T}{\sigma_T} \frac{d\sigma_T}{d\varepsilon_T}$$
Part (b): Physical Limiting Cases of $n$
  • $n = 0$ (Rigid-Perfectly Plastic): The flow stress is independent of strain ($\sigma_T = K = \text{const}$). The material exhibits zero work hardening; once yield is reached, deformation localizes instantly into a neck without uniform elongation (typical of lead at room temperature or metals undergoing superplastic flow).
  • $n = 1$ (Linear Elastic / Hardening): The flow stress increases linearly with strain ($\sigma_T = K \varepsilon_T$), behaving analogously to Hooke's law.
Part (c): Typical Magnitudes of $n$
  • Low-SFE FCC Metals (Brass, 304 Stainless): $n \approx 0.40\text{ to }0.55$ (exceptionally high strain-hardening rate due to wide partial dislocation ribbons blocking cross-slip).
  • BCC Metals (Low-Carbon Steels): $n \approx 0.15\text{ to }0.25$ (moderate strain hardening; cross-slip occurs readily).
  • High-SFE FCC Metals (Aluminum, Copper): $n \approx 0.20\text{ to }0.35$.
  • Heavily Cold-Worked Metals: $n \approx 0.02\text{ to }0.05$ (dislocation density is already saturated near $\sim 10^{12} \text{ cm}^{-2}$, leaving virtually zero capacity for further work hardening).
Part (d): Importance in Sheet Metal Deep Drawing

In sheet metal press stamping, the punch exerts intense tensile stretching over punch radii. If a local region thins slightly, a high-$n$ material rapidly work-hardens in that thinned area, raising its local flow stress. This forces subsequent deformation to shift to adjacent, thicker unhardened regions of the sheet. Thus, high $n$ spreads plastic deformation uniformly across the entire panel, suppressing necking, preventing tearing, and maximizing the maximum draw depth (Limiting Draw Ratio).

Considère's Construction: Mathematical Proof that $\varepsilon_{T, u} = n$ at Maximum Load

In 1885, Armand Considère derived the rigorous mathematical condition governing the onset of plastic instability (necking) in uniaxial tension.

  1. Starting from the condition of maximum tensile load ($dF = 0$), derive Considère's Criterion in terms of true stress $\sigma_T$ and true strain $\varepsilon_T$: $$\frac{d\sigma_T}{d\varepsilon_T} = \sigma_T$$ Explain the geometric interpretation of this condition on a plot of $\sigma_T$ versus $\varepsilon_T$.
  2. Assuming the material follows the Hollomon power law $\sigma_T = K \varepsilon_T^n$, differentiate the equation and substitute into Considère's criterion to prove the famous theorem: $$\varepsilon_{T, u} = n$$ stating that the true uniform strain at the onset of necking is identically equal to the strain-hardening exponent.
  3. Calculate the maximum engineering strain $\varepsilon_u$ at necking for a brass alloy with $n = 0.45$.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): Derivation of Considère's Criterion

The instantaneous tensile load supported by the specimen is $F = \sigma_T A$.

At the maximum load point (UTS), the differential of load is zero:

$$dF = 0 \implies d(\sigma_T A) = \sigma_T dA + A d\sigma_T = 0 \implies A d\sigma_T = -\sigma_T dA \implies \frac{d\sigma_T}{\sigma_T} = -\frac{dA}{A}$$

During plastic deformation, volume is strictly conserved ($V = A l = \text{const}$):

$$dV = A dl + l dA = 0 \implies -\frac{dA}{A} = \frac{dl}{l} \equiv d\varepsilon_T$$

Substituting $-\frac{dA}{A} = d\varepsilon_T$ into the load equation:

$$\frac{d\sigma_T}{\sigma_T} = d\varepsilon_T \implies \mathbf{\frac{d\sigma_T}{d\varepsilon_T} = \sigma_T}$$

Geometric Interpretation: Necking begins at the exact point on the true stress-strain curve where the slope (the rate of strain hardening $\frac{d\sigma_T}{d\varepsilon_T}$) becomes numerically equal to the stress itself ($\sigma_T$). In Considère's geometric construction, drawing a tangent from $(-1, 0)$ on the true strain axis to the true stress-strain curve identifies the exact necking point.

Part (b): Proof that $\varepsilon_{T, u} = n$ for Hollomon Power Law

Given the Hollomon equation: $\sigma_T = K \varepsilon_T^n$. Differentiating with respect to $\varepsilon_T$:

$$\frac{d\sigma_T}{d\varepsilon_T} = \frac{d}{d\varepsilon_T}(K \varepsilon_T^n) = n K \varepsilon_T^{n-1} = n \left(\frac{K \varepsilon_T^n}{\varepsilon_T}\right) = \frac{n \sigma_T}{\varepsilon_T}$$

Equating this derivative to Considère's necking criterion ($\frac{d\sigma_T}{d\varepsilon_T} = \sigma_T$):

$$\frac{n \sigma_T}{\varepsilon_{T, u}} = \sigma_T \implies \frac{n}{\varepsilon_{T, u}} = 1 \implies \mathbf{\varepsilon_{T, u} = n}$$

This elegant theorem proves that a material's capacity for uniform elongation prior to necking is dictated strictly by its strain-hardening exponent $n$.

Part (c): Maximum Engineering Strain for Brass ($n = 0.45$)

From Considère's theorem, true strain at necking is $\varepsilon_{T, u} = n = 0.45$.

Using the conversion formula $\varepsilon_T = \ln(1 + \varepsilon)$:

$$\ln(1 + \varepsilon_u) = 0.45 \implies 1 + \varepsilon_u = \exp(0.45) = 1.5683$$ $$\varepsilon_u = 1.5683 - 1 = \mathbf{0.5683 = 56.8\%}$$

Cartridge brass can sustain an astonishing $56.8\%$ engineering strain uniformly across its entire length before localized necking begins!

  2. Define the Schmid Factor $m = \cos\phi \cos\lambda$.
  3. State the geometric restriction between angles $\phi$ and $\lambda$. What is the theoretical maximum value of the Schmid factor ($m_{\max}$), and at what specific angles does it occur?
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): Geometric Derivation of Schmid's Law

Consider a slip plane slicing through the cylindrical crystal at angle $\phi$ to the cross-sectional plane:

  1. Area of the Slip Plane ($A_s$): The normal to the slip plane makes angle $\phi$ with the tensile axis. The projected area on the horizontal plane is $A_0 = A_s \cos\phi$. Therefore, the actual area of the inclined slip plane is: $$A_s = \frac{A_0}{\cos\phi}$$
  2. Resolved Force Component along Slip Direction ($F_s$): The tensile force $F$ acts along the specimen cylinder axis. The slip direction vector lies within the slip plane and makes angle $\lambda$ with the tensile force vector. The component of tensile force directed parallel to the slip direction is: $$F_s = F \cos\lambda$$
  3. Resolved Shear Stress ($\tau_R$): By definition, shear stress is resolved shear force divided by slip plane area: $$\tau_R = \frac{F_s}{A_s} = \frac{F \cos\lambda}{\frac{A_0}{\cos\phi}} = \left(\frac{F}{A_0}\right) \cos\phi \cos\lambda = \mathbf{\sigma \cos\phi \cos\lambda}$$
Part (b): The Schmid Factor $m$
$$m = \cos\phi \cos\lambda \implies \tau_R = m \cdot \sigma$$

The Schmid factor $m$ is a purely geometric orientation factor (dimensionless, $0 \le m \le 0.5$) that quantifies the fraction of applied tensile stress converted into shear stress driving dislocation motion on a specific slip system.

Part (c): Geometric Bounds and Maximum Schmid Factor

Because the slip direction $\mathbf{s}$ lies entirely within the slip plane, it is strictly perpendicular to the slip plane normal $\mathbf{n}$ ($\mathbf{s} \cdot \mathbf{n} = 0$). By spherical trigonometry, the sum of angles satisfies:

$$\phi + \lambda \ge 90^\circ$$

To maximize $m = \cos\phi \cos\lambda$, the tensile axis must lie in the plane containing both $\mathbf{n}$ and $\mathbf{s}$, giving $\phi + \lambda = 90^\circ \implies \lambda = 90^\circ - \phi$.

$$m = \cos\phi \cos(90^\circ - \phi) = \cos\phi \sin\phi = \frac{1}{2} \sin(2\phi)$$

The maximum value of $\sin(2\phi)$ is $1.0$, occurring when $2\phi = 90^\circ \implies \phi = 45^\circ$:

$$\mathbf{m_{\max} = 0.500 \quad \text{at } \phi = 45^\circ \text{ and } \lambda = 45^\circ}$$

  3.     • Estimate the Ultimate Tensile Strength in megapascals ($\text{MPa}$).
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Part (a): The Vickers Hardness Test (HV)

The Vickers test uses a square-based diamond pyramid indenter with an apex angle between opposite faces of $\alpha = 136^\circ$.

The surface area of the four triangular pyramid faces is $A = \frac{d^2}{2\sin(136^\circ/2)} = \frac{d^2}{2\sin(68^\circ)} = \frac{d^2}{1.85437}$.

$$\text{HV} = \frac{P}{A} = \frac{2P \sin(68^\circ)}{d^2} = \mathbf{\frac{1.8544 P}{d^2}}$$

where $P$ is applied load in $\text{kgf}$ (typically $10\text{--}1000 \text{ gf}$ for microhardness, up to $50 \text{ kgf}$ for macrohardness) and $d$ is the average length of the two indentation diagonals measured under a microscope in millimeters.

Part (b): The Knoop Microhardness Test (HK)

The Knoop test uses an elongated pyramidal diamond indenter that produces a narrow rhombic impression with a length-to-width ratio of approximately $7.11 : 1$. Because the indentation depth is only about one-third of a Vickers impression for the same load, elastic recovery occurs primarily along the short diagonal while the long diagonal remains unaffected. This ultra-shallow penetration minimizes tensile hoop stresses around the impression, allowing microhardness measurements on brittle ceramics, glass, enamel, and sub-micron coatings without inducing fracture or chipping.

Part (c): Empirical Hardness-Strength Correlation for Steels

Because plastic indentation is constrained by surrounding elastic material, slip line field theory (Tabor's rule) proves that indentation pressure is roughly three times the tensile yield stress ($H \approx 3 \sigma_y$). For work-hardening steels, empirical calibration yields:

$$\sigma_{\text{UTS}} \text{ [MPa]} \approx \mathbf{3.45 \times \text{HB}}$$ $$\sigma_{\text{UTS}} \text{ [psi]} \approx \mathbf{500 \times \text{HB}}$$
Part (d): Calculations for Crankshaft Steel ($D = 10.0 \text{ mm}, P = 3000 \text{ kgf}, d = 3.90 \text{ mm}$)
$$\sqrt{D^2 - d^2} = \sqrt{10.0^2 - 3.90^2} = \sqrt{100.0 - 15.21} = \sqrt{84.79} = 9.20815 \text{ mm}$$ $$D - \sqrt{D^2 - d^2} = 10.0 - 9.20815 = 0.79185 \text{ mm}$$ $$\text{HBW} = \frac{2 \times 3000}{\pi \times 10.0 \times 0.79185} = \frac{6000}{24.8767} = \mathbf{241.2 \text{ HBW} \approx 241 \text{ HB}}$$

Tresca Yield Criterion (Maximum Shear Stress Theory)

A pressure hull made of high-strength alloy steel with a uniaxial yield strength of $\sigma_y = 650\text{ MPa}$ is subjected to a triaxial stress state: $\sigma_1 = 450\text{ MPa}$, $\sigma_2 = 200\text{ MPa}$, and $\sigma_3 = -280\text{ MPa}$.

Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Statement of the Tresca criterion
The Tresca (Maximum Shear Stress) criterion postulates that yielding initiates when the absolute maximum shear stress in a multiaxial stress state reaches the maximum shear stress at yielding in a uniaxial tension test: $$\tau_{\text{max}} = \frac{\sigma_1 - \sigma_3}{2} \ge \tau_y = \frac{\sigma_y}{2} \iff \sigma_1 - \sigma_3 \ge \sigma_y$$ where $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \sigma_3$.
Step 2: Maximum shear stress calculation
Given $\sigma_1 = 450\text{ MPa}$, $\sigma_2 = 200\text{ MPa}$, $\sigma_3 = -280\text{ MPa}$: $$\tau_{\text{max}} = \frac{450 - (-280)}{2} = \frac{730}{2} = 365\text{ MPa}$$ The equivalent Tresca stress is: $$\sigma_{\text{Tresca}} = \sigma_1 - \sigma_3 = 730\text{ MPa}$$
Step 3: Yield assessment and factor of safety
Comparing the equivalent stress to the uniaxial yield strength: $$\sigma_{\text{Tresca}} = 730\text{ MPa} > \sigma_y = 650\text{ MPa}$$ $$\tau_{\text{max}} = 365\text{ MPa} > \tau_y = \frac{650}{2} = 325\text{ MPa}$$ The material will undergo plastic yielding because the stress exceeds the yield threshold.
The factor of safety is: $$N_{\text{Tresca}} = \frac{\sigma_y}{\sigma_1 - \sigma_3} = \frac{650}{730} \approx 0.890 \quad (< 1.0 \implies \text{failed/yielded})$$
Key Results:
$\tau_{\text{max}} = 365\text{ MPa}$, $\sigma_{\text{Tresca}} = 730\text{ MPa}$
$\sigma_{\text{Tresca}} > \sigma_y$ ($730\text{ MPa} > 650\text{ MPa}$) $\implies$ Yielding will occur ($N = 0.89$)
Problem 29 Yield Criteria Medium

von Mises Yield Criterion (Distortion Energy Theory)

For the same stress state as in the previous problem ($\sigma_1 = 450\text{ MPa}$, $\sigma_2 = 200\text{ MPa}$, $\sigma_3 = -280\text{ MPa}$, and $\sigma_y = 650\text{ MPa}$):

1. Calculate the von Mises equivalent stress $\sigma_{\text{vM}}$ (effective stress).
2. Determine whether yielding is predicted according to the von Mises criterion.
3. Calculate the factor of safety $N_{\text{vM}}$ and compare it with the Tresca factor of safety $N_{\text{Tresca}} = 0.890$. Explain why the von Mises criterion predicts a higher safety margin than Tresca.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Calculate the von Mises equivalent stress
The von Mises equivalent stress is defined as: $$\sigma_{\text{vM}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{(\sigma_1 - \sigma_2)^2 + (\sigma_2 - \sigma_3)^2 + (\sigma_3 - \sigma_1)^2}$$ Substitute the given principal stresses: $$\sigma_1 - \sigma_2 = 450 - 200 = 250\text{ MPa} \implies (250)^2 = 62,500\text{ MPa}^2$$ $$\sigma_2 - \sigma_3 = 200 - (-280) = 480\text{ MPa} \implies (480)^2 = 230,400\text{ MPa}^2$$ $$\sigma_3 - \sigma_1 = -280 - 450 = -730\text{ MPa} \implies (-730)^2 = 532,900\text{ MPa}^2$$ Sum of squared differences: $$\sum (\Delta \sigma)^2 = 62,500 + 230,400 + 532,900 = 825,800\text{ MPa}^2$$ $$\sigma_{\text{vM}} = \sqrt{\frac{825,800}{2}} = \sqrt{412,900} \approx 642.57\text{ MPa}$$
Step 2: Yield assessment
Comparing $\sigma_{\text{vM}}$ to $\sigma_y$: $$\sigma_{\text{vM}} = 642.6\text{ MPa} < \sigma_y = 650\text{ MPa}$$ Remarkably, according to the von Mises criterion, the material remains elastic and does NOT yield!
Step 3: Factor of safety and comparison
$$N_{\text{vM}} = \frac{\sigma_y}{\sigma_{\text{vM}}} = \frac{650}{642.57} \approx 1.012$$ Physical explanation: The Tresca hexagon is entirely inscribed within the smooth von Mises ellipse on the $\pi$-plane (deviatoric plane). Tresca depends solely on the extreme principal stresses $(\sigma_1 - \sigma_3)$ and completely ignores the intermediate principal stress $\sigma_2$. In contrast, von Mises accounts for shear distortions along all octahedral planes, where an intermediate stress $\sigma_2$ between $\sigma_1$ and $\sigma_3$ provides constraint that lowers the distortion energy relative to the maximum shear stress. Thus, Tresca is always more conservative than von Mises by up to $\sim 15.5\%$.
Key Results:
$\sigma_{\text{vM}} = 642.6\text{ MPa}$
Since $\sigma_{\text{vM}} < \sigma_y$ ($642.6 < 650\text{ MPa}$), yielding does NOT occur under von Mises ($N_{\text{vM}} = 1.01$).
Tresca predicts yielding ($N = 0.89$) while von Mises predicts safety ($N = 1.01$).

4. Find the percentage difference between the two predictions.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Principal stresses in pure shear
In pure shear on the surface of a torsion bar: $$\sigma_x = 0, \quad \sigma_y = 0, \quad \tau_{xy} = \tau$$ The principal stresses are: $$\sigma_1 = \tau, \quad \sigma_2 = 0, \quad \sigma_3 = -\tau$$
Step 2: Tresca shear yield strength
According to Tresca: $$\sigma_1 - \sigma_3 = \tau - (-\tau) = 2\tau = \sigma_y \implies k_{\text{Tresca}} = \frac{\sigma_y}{2} = 0.500\,\sigma_y$$ Numerical value: $$k_{\text{Tresca}} = 0.500 \times 300\text{ MPa} = 150.0\text{ MPa}$$
Step 3: von Mises shear yield strength

Step 3: Yield strength at $T = 450\text{ K}$
Since $T = 450\text{ K} > T_0 = 360\text{ K}$, thermal energy completely overcomes the lattice friction barrier ($\sigma^* = 0$): $$\sigma_y(450) = 0 + \sigma_\mu = 120.0\text{ MPa}$$
Step 4: BCC vs FCC lattice friction
In BCC crystals, screw dislocations possess a non-planar core spread over three intersecting $\{110\}$ planes. Constricting this non-planar core so the dislocation can glide requires a substantial activation energy (high Peierls-Nabarro lattice friction barrier). At low temperatures, thermal vibrations are insufficient to assist constriction, causing the yield stress to skyrocket. In contrast, FCC dislocations have planar cores with very low intrinsic Peierls barriers ($\tau_{\text{PN}} \approx 10^{-5} G$ vs $10^{-2} G$ for BCC); thus, FCC yield strength depends only weakly on temperature.

Adiabatic Shear Banding and Thermomechanical Instability

In high-speed deformation (e.g., ballistic penetration or high-speed machining), almost all plastic work is converted into heat adiabatically. An instability known as an adiabatic shear band (ASB) initiates when thermal softening overtakes work hardening: $$\frac{d\sigma}{d\varepsilon} = \left(\frac{\partial \sigma}{\partial \varepsilon}\right)_T + \left(\frac{\partial \sigma}{\partial T}\right)_\varepsilon \frac{dT}{d\varepsilon} \le 0$$ Assuming the adiabatic temperature rise is given by $\frac{dT}{d\varepsilon} = \frac{\beta \sigma}{\rho c_p}$, where Taylor-Quinney factor $\beta = 0.90$, density $\rho = 7800\text{ kg/m}^3$, and specific heat capacity $c_p = 460\text{ J}/(\text{kg}\cdot\text{K})$.

For a steel alloy, the constitutive law is $\sigma = K \varepsilon^n [1 - \gamma(T - T_0)]$, with $K = 800\text{ MPa}$, $n = 0.15$, and thermal softening coefficient $\gamma = 0.0012\text{ K}^{-1}$.

1. Calculate the temperature rise per unit plastic strain $dT/d\varepsilon$ at $\sigma = 750\text{ MPa}$.
2. Formulate the instability condition $d\sigma/d\varepsilon = 0$ and estimate the critical true strain $\varepsilon_{\text{crit}}$ at which shear localization triggers.
3. Explain why adiabatic shear bands are catastrophic in armor plates and turbine rotor bursts.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Calculate adiabatic temperature rise per unit strain
$$\rho c_p = 7800\text{ kg/m}^3 \times 460\text{ J}/(\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}) = 3.588 \times 10^6\text{ J}/(\text{m}^3\cdot\text{K}) = 3.588\text{ MPa/K}$$ $$\frac{dT}{d\varepsilon} = \frac{\beta \sigma}{\rho c_p} = \frac{0.90 \times 750\text{ MPa}}{3.588\text{ MPa/K}} \approx 188.13\text{ K/unit strain}$$ Every $10\%$ of plastic strain under adiabatic conditions generates roughly $\sim 18.8\text{ K}$ of localized temperature increase!
Step 2: Formulate instability condition and find $\varepsilon_{\text{crit}}$
From $\sigma = K \varepsilon^n [1 - \gamma(T - T_0)]$: $$\left(\frac{\partial \sigma}{\partial \varepsilon}\right)_T = n \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}$$ $$\left(\frac{\partial \sigma}{\partial T}\right)_\varepsilon = -\gamma K \varepsilon^n \approx -\gamma \sigma$$ The total derivative becomes: $$\frac{d\sigma}{d\varepsilon} = n \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon} - \gamma \sigma \frac{dT}{d\varepsilon} = \sigma \left[\frac{n}{\varepsilon} - \gamma \frac{\beta \sigma}{\rho c_p}\right] = 0$$ Setting the bracket to zero: $$\frac{n}{\varepsilon_{\text{crit}}} = \frac{\gamma \beta \sigma}{\rho c_p} \implies \varepsilon_{\text{crit}} = \frac{n \rho c_p}{\gamma \beta \sigma}$$ Substitute the given values: $$\varepsilon_{\text{crit}} = \frac{0.15 \times 3.588 \times 10^6}{0.0012 \times 0.90 \times (750 \times 10^6)} = \frac{538,200}{810,000} \approx 0.664$$
Step 3: Catastrophic nature of adiabatic shear bands
Once strain reaches $\varepsilon_{\text{crit}}$, further deformation softens the material locally ($d\sigma/d\varepsilon < 0$). This causes severe strain localization into narrow bands (only $10\text{ to }50\text{ }\mu\text{m}$ wide). Within the band, temperatures can reach $80\%\text{ to }100\%$ of the melting point, destroying the material's shear strength and leading to rapid, catastrophic shear plugging or fragmentation without macroscale warning.
Key Results:
$dT/d\varepsilon = 188.1\text{ K/unit strain}$ at $750\text{ MPa}$
Instability condition: $\frac{n}{\varepsilon} - \frac{\gamma \beta \sigma}{\rho c_p} \le 0$
Critical strain $\varepsilon_{\text{crit}} \approx 0.66$ ($66\%$ local strain)
ASBs cause intense thermal softening concentrated in $10\text{--}50\text{ }\mu\text{m}$ zones, causing sudden shear failure.

2. Predict the compressive reverse yield stress $|\sigma_{y,\text{rev}}|$ assuming: (a) Pure isotropic hardening.
(b) Pure kinematic hardening.
3. If experimental measurement reveals reverse yielding begins at $|\sigma_{y,\text{rev}}| = 160\text{ MPa}$, calculate the Bauschinger stress parameter $\beta_B = \frac{\sigma_{\text{fwd}} - |\sigma_{y,\text{rev}}|}{2 \sigma_{\text{fwd}}}$. What does this value indicate?
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Definition and microscopic origin
The Bauschinger effect is the phenomenon whereby plastic deformation in one direction (e.g., forward tension) lowers the yield strength upon load reversal in the opposite direction (reverse compression).
Microscopic origin: During forward loading, dislocations glide and pile up against impenetrable obstacles (grain boundaries, precipitates). These pile-ups create a long-range microscopic back stress ($\tau_b$) that opposes further forward glide. When the load is reversed, this internal back stress aids dislocation glide in the reverse direction, triggering plastic flow at a significantly reduced reverse external stress.
Step 2: Predictions under hardening models
(a) Pure Isotropic Hardening: The yield surface expands uniformly in all directions while keeping its center fixed at the origin. $$|\sigma_{y,\text{rev}}| = \sigma_{\text{fwd}} = 480\text{ MPa}$$
(b) Pure Kinematic Hardening: The yield surface maintains a constant size (elastic range $= 2\sigma_{y0}$) and translates rigidly in stress space by back stress $\alpha$: $$\alpha = \sigma_{\text{fwd}} - \sigma_{y0} = 480 - 240 = 240\text{ MPa}$$ Reverse yield stress: $$|\sigma_{y,\text{rev}}| = \sigma_{\text{fwd}} - 2\sigma_{y0} = 480 - 2(240) = 0\text{ MPa}$$ (yielding in reverse begins immediately upon reaching zero stress!)
Step 3: Experimental assessment and Bauschinger parameter
Given measured $|\sigma_{y,\text{rev}}| = 160\text{ MPa}$: $$\beta_B = \frac{\sigma_{\text{fwd}} - |\sigma_{y,\text{rev}}|}{2\sigma_{\text{fwd}}} = \frac{480 - 160}{2(480)} = \frac{320}{960} = \frac{1}{3} \approx 0.333$$ Alternatively, the ratio of reverse yield to forward stress is: $$\frac{|\sigma_{y,\text{rev}}|}{\sigma_{\text{fwd}}} = \frac{160}{480} = 0.333$$ This proves real metals exhibit mixed isotropic-kinematic hardening: isotropic hardening expands the elastic range, while kinematic back stresses shift the yield center.
Key Results:
Isotropic model prediction: $|\sigma_{y,\text{rev}}| = 480\text{ MPa}$
Kinematic model prediction: $|\sigma_{y,\text{rev}}| = 0\text{ MPa}$
Measured: $|\sigma_{y,\text{rev}}| = 160\text{ MPa}$ $\implies$ $\beta_B = 0.333$ (mixed hardening behavior).

The springback ratio $K_s$ relates the initial loaded bend radius $r_i$ to the final unloaded radius $r_f$: $$K_s = \frac{r_i + t/2}{r_f + t/2} \approx 4\left(\frac{r_i \sigma_y}{E t}\right)^3 - 3\left(\frac{r_i \sigma_y}{E t}\right) + 1$$ where $t$ is sheet thickness, $\sigma_y$ is yield strength, and $E$ is Young's modulus.

2. Calculate the springback ratio $K_s$.
3. If the desired final bend angle is $\alpha_f = 90.0^\circ$, determine the required tooling bend angle $\alpha_i$ to compensate for springback ($K_s = \alpha_f / \alpha_i$).
4. Repeat the calculation for an aluminum alloy sheet of identical dimensions with $E = 70\text{ GPa}$ and $\sigma_y = 350\text{ MPa}$. Which material exhibits greater springback?
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Calculate dimensionless parameter $\xi$ for high-strength steel
$$\xi_{\text{steel}} = \frac{r_i \sigma_y}{E t} = \frac{(10.0 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}) \times (600 \times 10^6\text{ Pa})}{(205 \times 10^9\text{ Pa}) \times (2.0 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m})} = \frac{6000}{410,000} \approx 0.014634$$
Step 2: Calculate springback ratio $K_s$ for steel
$$K_s \approx 4\xi^3 - 3\xi + 1$$ Since $\xi^3 = (0.014634)^3 \approx 3.13 \times 10^{-6}$ is negligible: $$K_{s,\text{steel}} \approx 1 - 3(0.014634) = 1 - 0.04390 = 0.9561$$
Step 3: Tooling punch angle $\alpha_i$ for steel
$$K_s = \frac{\alpha_f}{\alpha_i} \implies \alpha_i = \frac{\alpha_f}{K_s} = \frac{90.0^\circ}{0.9561} \approx 94.13^\circ$$ The tool must overbend the part by $\Delta \alpha = 94.13^\circ - 90^\circ = 4.13^\circ$.
Step 4: Aluminum alloy calculation and comparison
$$\xi_{\text{Al}} = \frac{r_i \sigma_y}{E t} = \frac{(10.0 \times 10^{-3}) \times (350 \times 10^6)}{(70 \times 10^9) \times (2.0 \times 10^{-3})} = \frac{3500}{140,000} = 0.0250$$ $$K_{s,\text{Al}} \approx 1 - 3(0.0250) = 1 - 0.0750 = 0.9250$$ $$\alpha_{i,\text{Al}} = \frac{90.0^\circ}{0.9250} \approx 97.30^\circ \implies \Delta \alpha_{\text{Al}} = 7.30^\circ$$ Aluminum exhibits significantly greater springback ($7.30^\circ$ vs $4.13^\circ$). Springback is directly proportional to $\sigma_y / E$. Because aluminum has one-third the modulus of steel ($70\text{ GPa}$ vs $205\text{ GPa}$), its ratio $\sigma_y / E = 350 / 70,000 = 0.0050$ is larger than steel's $600 / 205,000 = 0.00293$, resulting in far greater elastic recovery upon die release.
Key Results:
Steel: $K_s = 0.9561$, required tool angle $\alpha_i = 94.13^\circ$ (overbend $= 4.13^\circ$)
Aluminum: $K_s = 0.9250$, required tool angle $\alpha_i = 97.30^\circ$ (overbend $= 7.30^\circ$)
Aluminum undergoes $77\%$ more springback due to its higher $\sigma_y / E$ ratio.

Step 1: Calculate the elastoplastic bending moment $M$
For a rectangular cross-section with elastic core height $2y_0$: $$M = \sigma_y b \left(c^2 - \frac{y_0^2}{3}\right)$$ Given $b = 0.020\text{ m}$, $c = 0.020\text{ m}$, $y_0 = 0.010\text{ m}$: $$c^2 - \frac{y_0^2}{3} = (0.020)^2 - \frac{(0.010)^2}{3} = 0.00040 - 0.00003333 = 0.00036667\text{ m}^2$$ $$M = (300 \times 10^6\text{ N/m}^2) \times (0.020\text{ m}) \times (0.00036667\text{ m}^2) = 2200.0\text{ N}\cdot\text{m} = 2.20\text{ kN}\cdot\text{m}$$

Unloading is purely elastic. By antisymmetry across $y = 0$, the resultant axial force $\int \sigma_{\text{res}} dA = 0$ and the net residual internal moment $\int \sigma_{\text{res}} y dA = 0$, confirming perfect self-equilibrium.

$$\varepsilon_0 = \frac{d_0(\psi=0) - d_0}{d_0} = \frac{1.08240 - 1.08200}{1.08200} = \frac{0.00040}{1.08200} \approx +3.6969 \times 10^{-4}$$ $$\varepsilon_{45} = \frac{d_{45} - d_0}{d_0} = \frac{1.08092 - 1.08200}{1.08200} = \frac{-0.00108}{1.08200} \approx -9.9815 \times 10^{-4}$$
Step 2: Slope of the $\sin^2\psi$ plot
$$M = \frac{\varepsilon_{45} - \varepsilon_0}{\sin^2(45^\circ) - \sin^2(0^\circ)} = \frac{-9.9815 \times 10^{-4} - 3.6969 \times 10^{-4}}{0.50 - 0.0} = \frac{-13.6784 \times 10^{-4}}{0.50} = -2.7357 \times 10^{-3}$$
Step 3: Calculate surface residual stress $\sigma_\phi$
From the governing equation: $$M = \left(\frac{1+\nu}{E}\right) \sigma_\phi \implies \sigma_\phi = M \left(\frac{E}{1+\nu}\right)$$ $$\frac{E}{1+\nu} = \frac{210 \times 10^3\text{ MPa}}{1 + 0.30} = \frac{210,000}{1.30} \approx 161,538.5\text{ MPa}$$ $$\sigma_\phi = (-2.7357 \times 10^{-3}) \times (161,538.5\text{ MPa}) \approx -441.9\text{ MPa}$$ The negative sign confirms that the surface possesses a compressive residual stress of $441.9\text{ MPa}$.
Step 4: Alternative destructive measurement technique
The Hole-Drilling Strain Gage Method (ASTM E837): A high-speed milling cutter drills a small blind hole ($1.6\text{ to }2.0\text{ mm}$ diameter) in the center of a specialized 3-element strain gage rosette. Removing the stressed material relaxes the surrounding elastic stress field; the relieved strains measured by the rosette are converted into in-plane residual stresses as a function of depth.
Key Results:
Slope $M = -2.736 \times 10^{-3}$
Surface residual stress $\sigma_\phi = -441.9\text{ MPa}$ (compressive)
Destructive alternative: Blind hole-drilling strain gage method (ASTM E837) or contour method.
Problem 48 Sheet Metal Formability Medium

Plastic Strain Ratio (Lankford R-Value) and Normal Anisotropy

The plastic strain ratio (Lankford $R$-value) characterizes the resistance of sheet metal to thinning during plastic forming: $$R = \frac{\varepsilon_w}{\varepsilon_t} = \frac{\ln(w / w_0)}{\ln(t / t_0)}$$ Assuming constancy of volume during plastic deformation ($\varepsilon_l + \varepsilon_w + \varepsilon_t = 0$).

Tensile specimens stamped from a cold-rolled IF (interstitial-free) steel sheet are elongated to a uniform longitudinal plastic strain $\varepsilon_l = 0.180$. The width strains measured at angles to the rolling direction are: - Longitudinal ($0^\circ$): $\varepsilon_w = -0.120$ - Diagonal ($45^\circ$): $\varepsilon_w = -0.110$ - Transverse ($90^\circ$): $\varepsilon_w = -0.128$
1. Calculate the thickness strain $\varepsilon_t$ and the $R$-value for each orientation ($R_0, R_{45}, R_{90}$).
2. Calculate the average normal anisotropy parameter $\bar{R} = \frac{R_0 + 2R_{45} + R_{90}}{4}$.
3. Explain why sheets with $\bar{R} > 1.8$ are highly prized in automotive body deep-drawing applications.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Calculate thickness strains and $R$-values
From volume conservation: $\varepsilon_t = -(\varepsilon_l + \varepsilon_w) = -(0.180 + \varepsilon_w)$.
- For $0^\circ$ (Rolling direction): $$\varepsilon_{t, 0} = -(0.180 - 0.120) = -0.060$$ $$R_0 = \frac{\varepsilon_w}{\varepsilon_t} = \frac{-0.120}{-0.060} = 2.000$$
- For $45^\circ$ (Diagonal): $$\varepsilon_{t, 45} = -(0.180 - 0.110) = -0.070$$ $$R_{45} = \frac{\varepsilon_w}{\varepsilon_t} = \frac{-0.110}{-0.070} \approx 1.571$$
- For $90^\circ$ (Transverse): $$\varepsilon_{t, 90} = -(0.180 - 0.128) = -0.052$$ $$R_{90} = \frac{\varepsilon_w}{\varepsilon_t} = \frac{-0.128}{-0.052} \approx 2.462$$
Step 2: Calculate average normal anisotropy $\bar{R}$
$$\bar{R} = \frac{R_0 + 2R_{45} + R_{90}}{4} = \frac{2.000 + 2(1.571) + 2.462}{4} = \frac{2.000 + 3.142 + 2.462}{4} = \frac{7.604}{4} \approx 1.901$$
Step 3: Importance of high $\bar{R}$ in deep drawing
The $R$-value is the ratio of width contraction to thickness thinning. When $R > 1$, the sheet resists thinning in the thickness direction far more than it resists contraction in the width direction. In a deep-drawing process (e.g., drawing a beverage can or car fender), the material drawn over the die radius undergoes high tensile drawing stresses. A high $\bar{R}$ value ($\bar{R} \approx 1.9$) prevents localized necking and premature tearing in the cup wall, allowing much higher Limiting Drawing Ratios (LDR) and enabling deeper, defect-free stampings.
Key Results:
$R_0 = 2.00$, $R_{45} = 1.57$, $R_{90} = 2.46$
Average normal anisotropy $\bar{R} = 1.90$
High $\bar{R}$ prevents wall thinning and premature tearing during deep drawing.
Problem 49 Sheet Metal Formability Medium

Planar Anisotropy and Earing Behavior in Deep Drawing

Planar anisotropy $\Delta R$ quantifies the variation of plastic properties within the sheet plane: $$\Delta R = \frac{R_0 - 2R_{45} + R_{90}}{2}$$ During deep drawing of a circular blank into a cylindrical cup, non-zero planar anisotropy causes non-uniform cup rim heights, forming wave-like projections called ears.

Using the data from Problem 48 ($R_0 = 2.000$, $R_{45} = 1.571$, $R_{90} = 2.462$):
1. Calculate the planar anisotropy parameter $\Delta R$.
2. Predict the number and angular positions of the ears formed along the rim of a deep-drawn cup.
3. If another alloy has $\Delta R < 0$ (e.g., cold-rolled and annealed aluminum), predict where the ears will form.
4. Explain how crystallographic preferred orientation (texture) generated during cold rolling creates planar anisotropy.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Calculate planar anisotropy $\Delta R$
$$\Delta R = \frac{R_0 - 2R_{45} + R_{90}}{2} = \frac{2.000 - 2(1.571) + 2.462}{2} = \frac{2.000 - 3.142 + 2.462}{2} = \frac{1.320}{2} = +0.660$$
Step 2: Predict ear locations for $\Delta R > 0$
When $\Delta R > 0$, the $R$-values along $0^\circ$ and $90^\circ$ are significantly higher than along $45^\circ$. Because higher $R$ resists thinning and promotes material flow into the rim height: - Four ears will form located at $0^\circ, 90^\circ, 180^\circ,$ and $270^\circ$ relative to the rolling direction (coinciding with the rolling and transverse directions). - Troughs (depressions) will occur at $45^\circ, 135^\circ, 225^\circ,$ and $315^\circ$.
Step 3: Ear locations for $\Delta R < 0$
When $\Delta R < 0$, $R_{45} > \frac{R_0 + R_{90}}{2}$. Consequently, the four ears will shift by $45^\circ$ and form at $45^\circ, 135^\circ, 225^\circ,$ and $315^\circ$, while troughs form at $0^\circ$ and $90^\circ$.
Step 4: Physical origin in crystallographic texture
During cold rolling, individual crystal grains rotate toward specific crystallographic orientations (e.g., $\{111\}\langle 112\rangle$ or $\{001\}\langle 110\rangle$ textures). Because close-packed slip planes $(\{111\}$ in FCC, $\{110\}$ in BCC) and slip directions are non-randomly oriented relative to the sheet axes, the Schmid factors and yield resistance vary systematically with in-plane testing direction. This directional bias directly causes anisotropic yielding and ear formation.
Key Results:
$\Delta R = +0.660$
Since $\Delta R > 0$, 4 ears form at $0^\circ, 90^\circ, 180^\circ,$ and $270^\circ$.
For $\Delta R < 0$, ears form at $45^\circ, 135^\circ, 225^\circ,$ and $315^\circ$.
Earing is caused by crystallographic deformation texture aligning slip systems anisotropically.
Problem 50 Material Selection & Design Hard

Comprehensive Material Selection: Aerospace Pressure Cylinder

A cylindrical aerospace cabin pressure vessel with diameter $D = 2.0\text{ m}$ (radius $R = 1.0\text{ m}$) and length $L = 6.0\text{ m}$ must withstand an internal operating gauge pressure $p = 120\text{ kPa}$ ($1.2\text{ bar}$) with a design factor of safety $N = 2.0$. To minimize launch mass, the structural engineer must evaluate three competing candidate alloys:

Candidate AlloyDensity $\rho$ ($\text{g/cm}^3$)Modulus $E$ ($\text{GPa}$)Yield Strength $\sigma_y$ ($\text{MPa}$)Elongation %EL
Alloy A: Al 2024-T32.787334518%
Alloy B: Ti-6Al-4V4.4311488014%
Alloy C: Maraging Steel 2508.00210172011%

1. Using the von Mises yield criterion with safety factor $N = 2.0$, derive the formula for the minimum wall thickness $t$ of the cylinder.
2. Calculate the required minimum wall thickness $t$ for all three alloys.
3. Calculate the total structural mass $M = \rho \pi D L t$ for each alloy option.
4. Which alloy achieves the lightest overall structure? Calculate the percentage mass saving achieved by the winning alloy over the other two candidates.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Formula for minimum wall thickness under von Mises
In a thin-walled cylinder: $\sigma_\theta = p R / t$, $\sigma_z = p R / (2t) = 0.5 \sigma_\theta$, $\sigma_r \approx 0$. The von Mises stress was derived in Problem 31: $$\sigma_{\text{vM}} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \sigma_\theta = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \frac{p R}{t}$$ For safety factor $N$: $$\sigma_{\text{vM}} \le \frac{\sigma_y}{N} \implies \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \frac{p R}{t} = \frac{\sigma_y}{N} \implies t_{\text{min}} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \frac{N p R}{\sigma_y}$$ Given $N = 2.0$, $p = 120 \times 10^3\text{ Pa}$, $R = 1.0\text{ m}$: $$N p R = 2.0 \times 120,000 \times 1.0 = 240,000\text{ N/m}$$ $$t_{\text{min}} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \times \frac{240,000}{\sigma_y} = \frac{207,846}{\sigma_y}\text{ m} = \frac{207.85}{\sigma_y\text{ (in MPa)}}\text{ mm}$$
Step 2: Required thickness for each alloy
- Alloy A (Al 2024-T3, $\sigma_y = 345\text{ MPa}$): $$t_A = \frac{207.846}{345} \approx 0.6025\text{ mm} = 6.025 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}$$ - Alloy B (Ti-6Al-4V, $\sigma_y = 880\text{ MPa}$): $$t_B = \frac{207.846}{880} \approx 0.2362\text{ mm} = 2.362 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}$$ - Alloy C (Maraging Steel, $\sigma_y = 1720\text{ MPa}$): $$t_C = \frac{207.846}{1720} \approx 0.1208\text{ mm} = 1.208 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}$$
Step 3: Calculate structural shell mass $M = \rho \pi D L t$
Surface area of cylinder: $A_{\text{cyl}} = \pi D L = \pi \times 2.0 \times 6.0 \approx 37.699\text{ m}^2$.
- Alloy A (Al 2024): $$\rho_A = 2780\text{ kg/m}^3 \implies M_A = 2780 \times 37.699 \times (6.025 \times 10^{-4}) \approx 63.15\text{ kg}$$ - Alloy B (Ti-6Al-4V): $$\rho_B = 4430\text{ kg/m}^3 \implies M_B = 4430 \times 37.699 \times (2.362 \times 10^{-4}) \approx 39.45\text{ kg}$$ - Alloy C (Maraging Steel): $$\rho_C = 8000\text{ kg/m}^3 \implies M_C = 8000 \times 37.699 \times (1.208 \times 10^{-4}) \approx 36.43\text{ kg}$$
Step 4: Comparison and selection
The structural mass ranks: 1. Maraging Steel 250 ($36.4\text{ kg}$) — Lightest 2. Ti-6Al-4V ($39.5\text{ kg}$) — $+8.3\%$ heavier 3. Al 2024-T3 ($63.2\text{ kg}$) — $+73.4\%$ heavier
Ashby index rationale: For a minimum-weight internally pressurized thin shell, the mass minimization index is to maximize specific strength $\sigma_y / \rho$: - $\sigma_y / \rho (\text{Steel}) = 1720 / 8.00 = 215.0\text{ MPa}\cdot\text{cm}^3/\text{g}$ - $\sigma_y / \rho (\text{Ti}) = 880 / 4.43 = 198.6\text{ MPa}\cdot\text{cm}^3/\text{g}$ - $\sigma_y / \rho (\text{Al}) = 345 / 2.78 = 124.1\text{ MPa}\cdot\text{cm}^3/\text{g}$
Maraging steel yields the lightest vessel ($36.4\text{ kg}$), saving $42.3\%$ mass compared to aluminum 2024-T3. (In practical aerospace design, titanium might still be preferred due to corrosion resistance, damage tolerance, and minimum manufacturing thickness gage limits of $0.12\text{ mm}$ for ultra-thin steel foil).
Key Results:
Thicknesses: $t_A = 0.603\text{ mm}$, $t_B = 0.236\text{ mm}$, $t_C = 0.121\text{ mm}$
Vessel Mass: Maraging Steel $= 36.4\text{ kg}$ (Lightest), Ti-6Al-4V $= 39.5\text{ kg}$, Al 2024-T3 $= 63.2\text{ kg}$
Maraging Steel saves $42.3\%$ mass over aluminum and $8.3\%$ mass over titanium due to its superior specific yield strength ($\sigma_y/\rho = 215$).
