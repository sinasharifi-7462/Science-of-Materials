Problem 01 Fracture Fundamentals Easy

Ductile vs. Brittle Fracture: Macroscopic and Microscopic Characteristics

A mechanical components inspector must examine two failed alloy steel drive shafts that fractured in service. One failed by ductile overload, while the other failed by catastrophic brittle cleavage.

1. Complete a comparative analysis between ductile and brittle fracture across the following 5 criteria: (a) Macroscopic plastic deformation and cross-sectional necking.
(b) Crack propagation velocity and energy absorption.
(c) Macroscopic fracture surface morphology (including orientation relative to tensile axis).
(d) Warning signs prior to final fracture.
(e) Microscopic fractographic appearance under a Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM).
2. Explain why engineers strongly prefer ductile fracture over brittle fracture in structural design.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Comparative Analysis Table
FeatureDuctile FractureBrittle Fracture
Macroscopic Plastic DeformationExtensive plastic deformation; visible cross-sectional reduction (necking) and elongationNegligible or zero measurable plastic deformation; dimensions unchanged
Crack Propagation & EnergySlow, stable crack growth (crack advances only if applied stress increases); high energy absorptionExtremely rapid, unstable catastrophic crack propagation (near the speed of sound, $\sim 1000\text{ m/s}$); very low energy absorption
Macroscopic Fracture SurfaceDull, fibrous gray appearance; classic cup-and-cone morphology with flat tensile fibrous core and $45^\circ$ shear lipsBright, flat, reflective crystalline/granular facets; perpendicular ($90^\circ$) to maximum tensile stress
Warning Prior to FailureClear advance warning (visible sagging, bulging, paint flaking, deformation)Sudden, catastrophic failure without warning
Microscopic SEM AppearanceDense population of microscopic spherical or parabolic dimples (microvoid coalescence)Flat transgranular cleavage facets with river patterns, or intergranular "rock candy" boundaries
Step 2: Why ductile fracture is strongly preferred
1. Damage Tolerance and Advance Warning: Ductile materials deform noticeably before fracturing, allowing maintenance crews, sensors, or visual inspections to detect excessive strain, take the machine offline, and repair the part before catastrophic collapse. 2. Enormous Energy Absorption: The large plastic deformation zone dissipates vast amounts of mechanical energy ($\int \sigma d\varepsilon$), preventing sudden fragmentation, blast wave releases, and secondary collapse under unexpected dynamic impact or overloads.
Key Results:
Ductile: Large plastic necking, slow stable crack growth, cup-and-cone with $45^\circ$ shear lips, SEM microvoid dimples, high energy absorption.
Brittle: Zero plastic necking, sound-speed unstable crack, flat reflective surface at $90^\circ$, SEM cleavage river patterns, zero advance warning.
Ductile is preferred for safety because it provides visual warning and absorbs massive mechanical energy.
Problem 02 Fracture Fundamentals Medium

Microscopic Mechanisms: Microvoid Coalescence vs. Cleavage and Intergranular Fracture

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) fractography is the definitive forensic tool for diagnosing fracture mechanisms in failed mechanical components.

1. Describe the 3 sequential stages of Microvoid Coalescence (MVC) in ductile fracture: (a) Void nucleation.
(b) Void growth.
(c) Void coalescence (sheet thinning).
2. Explain how equiaxed dimples form under pure uniaxial tension versus elongated (parabolic) dimples under in-plane shear or tear loading.
3. What is transgranular cleavage? Describe the physical origin of river patterns on cleavage facets and explain which direction the "rivers" flow.
4. Describe intergranular fracture ("rock candy" appearance) and identify two common metallurgical causes.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: The three stages of Microvoid Coalescence (MVC)
(a) Void Nucleation: When plastic strain accumulates, the severe elastic incompatibility between the ductile metallic matrix and hard, non-deformable second-phase inclusions (e.g., $\text{MnS}$ inclusions, $\text{Fe}_3\text{C}$ carbides, oxide particles) creates high interface tensile tractions. Microvoids nucleate either by decohesion of the inclusion/matrix interface or by cracking of the brittle particle itself.
(b) Void Growth: Driven by plastic slip and triaxial tensile hydrostatic stress ($\sigma_m$), the nucleated microvoids expand in volume and stretch in the direction of the principal tensile stress.
(c) Void Coalescence: As voids grow, the metallic ligaments between adjacent voids progressively thin down until localized plastic shear instability (internal necking) shears the intervening ligaments, joining the voids into a continuous macroscopic crack front.
Step 2: Equiaxed vs. Elongated (parabolic) dimples
- Equiaxed dimples: Under uniform uniaxial tension, voids grow symmetrically in all radial directions, resulting in circular, equiaxed cups (dimples) when viewed face-on.
- Parabolic/elongated dimples: Under shear or tearing (Mode II/III), plastic deformation tilts and stretches the voids along the shear direction. The dimples on the two mating fracture surfaces point in opposite directions under shear, or in the same direction toward the crack origin under tear loading.
Step 3: Transgranular cleavage and river patterns
Cleavage is rapid separation along low-index, weakly bonded crystallographic planes (principally $\{100\}$ in BCC iron). As a cleavage crack advances across a grain, it encounters screw dislocation steps or small tilt misorientations. The crack is forced to split onto parallel cleavage planes at slightly different elevations, creating vertical cleavage steps. To minimize surface energy, adjacent steps converge and merge as the crack advances, forming a branching tributary network known as a river pattern. Crucial rule: River branches merge into larger "rivers" in the direction of crack propagation; therefore, following river patterns upstream points directly back to the microscopic fracture initiation site!
Step 4: Intergranular fracture
Intergranular fracture occurs when the crack propagates along prior austenitic or ferritic grain boundaries, revealing faceted, 3D polyhedral grains resembling chunks of rock candy. Metallurgical causes: 1. Temper Embrittlement: Segregation of tramp impurity elements (phosphorus, sulfur, antimony, tin, arsenic) to grain boundaries during slow cooling through $375^\circ\text{C}\text{--}575^\circ\text{C}$, severely lowering grain boundary cohesion. 2. Sensitization in Stainless Steels: Precipitation of continuous chromium carbides ($\text{Cr}_{23}\text{C}_6$) along grain boundaries, depleting chromium in adjacent channels and causing intergranular stress corrosion. 3. Hydrogen Embrittlement: Trapping of dissolved atomic hydrogen at grain boundaries, lowering local boundary cohesion.
Key Results:
MVC: Nucleation at particles $\to$ growth under triaxiality $\to$ coalescence by ligament shearing.
Equiaxed dimples indicate pure tension; parabolic dimples indicate shear/tearing.
Cleavage river patterns branch in the direction of crack travel; following them upstream locates the crack origin.
Intergranular fracture ("rock candy") results from impurity segregation (P, S, Sb) or grain boundary carbide precipitation.
Problem 03 Stress Concentrations Medium

Inglis Stress Concentration Factor at an Elliptical Flaw

In 1913, C. E. Inglis solved the elastic stress distribution around an elliptical hole with major axis $2a$ and minor axis $2b$ oriented perpendicular to a remote uniform tensile stress $\sigma_0$ in an infinite plate. The maximum localized tensile stress $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ occurring at the sharp tips of the ellipse is: $$\sigma_{\text{max}} = \sigma_0 \left(1 + 2\frac{a}{b}\right) = \sigma_0 \left(1 + 2\sqrt{\frac{a}{\rho_t}}\right)$$ where $\rho_t = b^2 / a$ is the tip radius of curvature. For a sharp flaw ($\rho_t \ll a$), the stress concentration factor $K_t$ simplifies to: $$K_t = \frac{\sigma_{\text{max}}}{\sigma_0} \approx 2\sqrt{\frac{a}{\rho_t}}$$
1. A wide steel plate is subjected to a remote tensile stress $\sigma_0 = 180\text{ MPa}$. An elliptical flaw of total length $2a = 10.0\text{ mm}$ (crack half-length $a = 5.0\text{ mm}$) has a root tip radius $\rho_t = 0.050\text{ mm}$ ($50\text{ }\mu\text{m}$). Calculate $K_t$ and the peak localized tip stress $\sigma_{\text{max}}$.
2. If the flaw is sharpened by fatigue until $\rho_t = 0.50\text{ }\mu\text{m}$ ($5.0 \times 10^{-7}\text{ m}$), calculate the new $K_t$ and $\sigma_{\text{max}}$.
3. What is $K_t$ for a perfectly circular hole ($a = b$)?
4. Why does an atomistically sharp crack ($\rho_t \to 0$) produce an infinite stress singularity in linear elasticity, and how does real material plasticity resolve this paradox?
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Calculate $K_t$ and peak stress for $\rho_t = 0.050\text{ mm}$
Given: $a = 5.0\text{ mm}$, $\rho_t = 0.050\text{ mm}$: $$\frac{a}{\rho_t} = \frac{5.0}{0.050} = 100$$ $$K_t = 1 + 2\sqrt{\frac{a}{\rho_t}} = 1 + 2\sqrt{100} = 1 + 2(10) = 21.0$$ Peak localized stress: $$\sigma_{\text{max}} = K_t \sigma_0 = 21.0 \times 180\text{ MPa} = 3,780\text{ MPa} = 3.78\text{ GPa}$$ The nominal stress of $180\text{ MPa}$ is amplified to nearly $3.8\text{ GPa}$ at the notch tip!
Step 2: Calculate for sharp fatigue flaw ($\rho_t = 0.50\text{ }\mu\text{m} = 0.00050\text{ mm}$)
$$\frac{a}{\rho_t} = \frac{5.0\text{ mm}}{0.00050\text{ mm}} = 10,000$$ $$K_t = 1 + 2\sqrt{10,000} = 1 + 2(100) = 201.0$$ $$\sigma_{\text{max}} = 201.0 \times 180\text{ MPa} = 36,180\text{ MPa} \approx 36.2\text{ GPa}$$
Step 3: Circular hole ($a = b$)
When $a = b$, the ellipse becomes a circle with $\rho_t = a^2/a = a$: $$K_t = 1 + 2\left(\frac{a}{a}\right) = 1 + 2(1) = 3.0$$ A circular hole produces a classic stress concentration factor of exactly 3.0 regardless of hole size.
Step 4: The sharp crack singularity and plastic relief
As the tip radius approaches atomic dimensions ($\rho_t \to 0$), the mathematical formula predicts $\sigma_{\text{max}} \propto 1/\sqrt{\rho_t} \to \infty$ (a stress singularity). In real ductile metals, stresses can never reach infinity because once localized stress exceeds the yield strength $\sigma_y$, plastic deformation blunts the crack tip. Dislocation nucleation and slip round off the sharp tip (increasing effective $\rho_t$) and redistribute the peak stresses over a finite plastic zone $r_p$, shielding the material from catastrophic fracture. In brittle materials (glass, ceramics), plastic blunting cannot occur, leading to instantaneous fracture at tiny loads.
Key Results:
At $\rho_t = 50\text{ }\mu\text{m}$: $K_t = 21.0 \implies \sigma_{\text{max}} = 3,780\text{ MPa}$
At $\rho_t = 0.5\text{ }\mu\text{m}$: $K_t = 201.0 \implies \sigma_{\text{max}} = 36.2\text{ GPa}$
Circular hole: $K_t = 3.0$
Plastic yielding at the crack tip blunts the notch root in metals, mitigating the theoretical elastic singularity.
Problem 04 Fracture Mechanics Hard

Griffith Energy Balance Criterion for Brittle Fracture

A. A. Griffith (1921) formulated the fundamental criterion for brittle fracture based on an energy balance. In an infinite plate of unit thickness containing an internal crack of length $2a$ subjected to a uniform tensile stress $\sigma$, the total change in potential energy $\Delta U$ upon crack formation is: $$\Delta U = U_{\text{surface}} - U_{\text{strain}} = 4 a \gamma_s - \frac{\pi \sigma^2 a^2}{E}$$ where $\gamma_s$ is the specific surface free energy of the material and $E$ is Young's modulus.

1. Using the equilibrium thermodynamic condition $\frac{d(\Delta U)}{da} = 0$, derive the classical Griffith formula for the critical fracture stress $\sigma_c$ for plane stress.
2. Show that the second derivative $\frac{d^2(\Delta U)}{da^2} < 0$ at $a = a_c$, and explain why this proves crack propagation is spontaneously unstable (explosive) once the critical stress is reached.
3. For a soda-lime silicate glass ($E = 68\text{ GPa}$, $\gamma_s = 0.35\text{ J/m}^2$), calculate the critical fracture stress $\sigma_c$ for an internal crack of length $2a = 4.0\text{ }\mu\text{m}$ (crack half-length $a = 2.0\text{ }\mu\text{m}$).
4. Calculate the critical crack size $a_c$ if the glass is loaded to a tensile stress $\sigma = 50.0\text{ MPa}$.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Derivation of the Griffith equation
The total potential energy is: $$\Delta U = 4 a \gamma_s - \frac{\pi \sigma^2 a^2}{E}$$ (Note: $4 a \gamma_s$ represents the surface energy of two crack faces of length $2a$, so area $= 2 \times 2a \times 1 = 4a$). Differentiating with respect to crack half-length $a$: $$\frac{d(\Delta U)}{da} = 4 \gamma_s - \frac{2\pi \sigma^2 a}{E} = 0$$ Solving for $\sigma^2$: $$\frac{2\pi \sigma^2 a}{E} = 4 \gamma_s \implies \sigma^2 = \frac{4 \gamma_s E}{2\pi a} = \frac{2 E \gamma_s}{\pi a}$$ Taking the square root yields Griffith's equation: $$\sigma_c = \sqrt{\frac{2 E \gamma_s}{\pi a}}$$
Step 2: Second derivative and instability
$$\frac{d^2(\Delta U)}{da^2} = -\frac{2\pi \sigma^2}{E}$$ Because $E > 0$ and $\sigma > 0$, the second derivative is strictly negative ($\frac{d^2(\Delta U)}{da^2} < 0$). This means the stationary point represents an energy maximum (an unstable equilibrium barrier). If a crack reaches length $a \ge a_c$, any incremental advance releases more elastic strain energy than is required to create new surface area ($dU_{\text{strain}}/da > dU_{\text{surface}}/da$). The net energy drops continuously ($\Delta U \to -\infty$), providing excess kinetic energy that accelerates the crack tip up to Rayleigh wave speeds ($\sim 1500\text{ m/s}$), causing sudden explosive fragmentation.
Step 3: Calculate $\sigma_c$ for glass with $a = 2.0\text{ }\mu\text{m}$
Given: $E = 68 \times 10^9\text{ Pa}$, $\gamma_s = 0.35\text{ J/m}^2$, $a = 2.0 \times 10^{-6}\text{ m}$: $$2 E \gamma_s = 2 \times (68 \times 10^9) \times 0.35 = 4.76 \times 10^{10}\text{ N}^2/\text{m}^3$$ $$\pi a = \pi \times (2.0 \times 10^{-6}\text{ m}) \approx 6.28318 \times 10^{-6}\text{ m}$$ $$\sigma_c = \sqrt{\frac{4.76 \times 10^{10}}{6.28318 \times 10^{-6}}} = \sqrt{7.5758 \times 10^{15}} \approx 87,039,000\text{ Pa} \approx 87.04\text{ MPa}$$
Step 4: Critical crack size $a_c$ for $\sigma = 50.0\text{ MPa}$
$$\sigma_c = \sqrt{\frac{2 E \gamma_s}{\pi a_c}} \implies a_c = \frac{2 E \gamma_s}{\pi \sigma^2}$$ $$a_c = \frac{4.76 \times 10^{10}}{\pi \times (50.0 \times 10^6)^2} = \frac{4.76 \times 10^{10}}{\pi \times 2.50 \times 10^{15}} = \frac{4.76 \times 10^{10}}{7.854 \times 10^{15}} \approx 6.06 \times 10^{-6}\text{ m} = 6.06\text{ }\mu\text{m}$$ An microscopic scratch of depth just $6\text{ }\mu\text{m}$ is sufficient to shatter the glass at $50\text{ MPa}$!
Key Results:
Derived: $\sigma_c = \sqrt{\frac{2E\gamma_s}{\pi a}}$
$d^2(\Delta U)/da^2 < 0$ proves crack growth is unstably accelerated once $a \ge a_c$.
Critical fracture stress at $a = 2\text{ }\mu\text{m}$: $\sigma_c = 87.0\text{ MPa}$
At $\sigma = 50\text{ MPa}$: critical crack size $a_c = 6.06\text{ }\mu\text{m}$.
Problem 05 Fracture Mechanics Medium

Orowan-Irwin Plastic Work Modification of Griffith Theory

When Griffith's formula $\sigma_c = \sqrt{\frac{2E\gamma_s}{\pi a}}$ was tested on ductile and semi-brittle metals (such as structural steel), experimental fracture stresses were found to be $100\times$ to $1000\times$ higher than predicted by Griffith using the true surface energy $\gamma_s \approx 1.0\text{ J/m}^2$. E. Orowan and G. Irwin recognized that in metals, dislocation motion around the advancing crack tip dissipates plastic work energy $\gamma_p$ that dwarfs the thermodynamic surface energy: $$\sigma_c = \sqrt{\frac{2E(\gamma_s + \gamma_p)}{\pi a}} \approx \sqrt{\frac{2E\gamma_p}{\pi a}} \quad (\text{since } \gamma_p \gg \gamma_s)$$
1. A structural low-carbon steel has $E = 210\text{ GPa}$ and surface energy $\gamma_s = 1.2\text{ J/m}^2$. If Griffith's original formula (with $\gamma_s$ alone) were used, calculate the predicted fracture stress $\sigma_c$ for an internal crack of half-length $a = 2.0\text{ mm}$.
2. Experimental fracture testing of this steel yields an actual fracture stress $\sigma_c = 360\text{ MPa}$ for $a = 2.0\text{ mm}$. Calculate the actual effective plastic work of fracture $\gamma_p$ (in $\text{kJ/m}^2$ or $\text{J/m}^2$).
3. Calculate the ratio $\gamma_p / \gamma_s$ and comment on the physical significance of plastic work dissipation in preventing brittle fracture in metals.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Griffith prediction using $\gamma_s$ alone
Given: $E = 210 \times 10^9\text{ Pa}$, $\gamma_s = 1.2\text{ J/m}^2$, $a = 2.0 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}$: $$\sigma_{c, \text{Griffith}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 E \gamma_s}{\pi a}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times (210 \times 10^9) \times 1.2}{\pi \times (2.0 \times 10^{-3})}} = \sqrt{\frac{5.04 \times 10^{11}}{0.00628318}} = \sqrt{8.0214 \times 10^{13}} \approx 8.956 \times 10^6\text{ Pa} \approx 8.96\text{ MPa}$$ Griffith's equation predicts that the steel would fracture at a pathetic tensile stress of only $8.96\text{ MPa}$!
Step 2: Calculate actual plastic work of fracture $\gamma_p$
From $\sigma_c = \sqrt{\frac{2E(\gamma_s + \gamma_p)}{\pi a}}$: $$\sigma_c^2 = \frac{2E(\gamma_s + \gamma_p)}{\pi a} \implies \gamma_s + \gamma_p = \frac{\pi a \sigma_c^2}{2E}$$ Given $\sigma_c = 360\text{ MPa} = 360 \times 10^6\text{ Pa}$: $$\sigma_c^2 = (360 \times 10^6)^2 = 1.296 \times 10^{17}\text{ Pa}^2$$ $$\pi a \sigma_c^2 = \pi \times (2.0 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}) \times (1.296 \times 10^{17}\text{ Pa}^2) \approx 8.143 \times 10^{14}\text{ N}^2/\text{m}$$ $$\gamma_s + \gamma_p = \frac{8.143 \times 10^{14}}{2 \times (210 \times 10^9)} = \frac{8.143 \times 10^{14}}{4.20 \times 10^{11}} \approx 1,938.8\text{ J/m}^2 \approx 1.94\text{ kJ/m}^2$$ Since $\gamma_s = 1.2\text{ J/m}^2$: $$\gamma_p = 1,938.8 - 1.2 \approx 1,937.6\text{ J/m}^2 \approx 1.94\text{ kJ/m}^2$$
Step 3: Ratio $\gamma_p / \gamma_s$ and physical significance
$$\frac{\gamma_p}{\gamma_s} = \frac{1,937.6\text{ J/m}^2}{1.2\text{ J/m}^2} \approx 1,615$$ The energy absorbed by plastic work in the plastic zone ahead of the crack tip ($\gamma_p$) is more than $1600\times$ larger than the thermodynamic surface energy ($\gamma_s$) required to sever atomic bonds. In metals, crack extension is physically impossible without dragging along a cloud of billions of gliding and multiplying dislocations. This plastic deformation acts as a massive energy sink that absorbs incoming strain energy, raising fracture resistance by thousands of times compared to glass or ceramics.
Key Results:
Pure Griffith prediction: $\sigma_c = 8.96\text{ MPa}$ (gross underestimation).
Plastic work of fracture: $\gamma_p \approx 1,938\text{ J/m}^2$ ($1.94\text{ kJ/m}^2$).
$\gamma_p / \gamma_s \approx 1,615\times$ (plastic dislocation dissipation dominates fracture resistance in metals).
Problem 06 Linear Elastic Fracture Mechanics Medium

Strain Energy Release Rate $G$ and Critical Driving Force $G_c$

Irwin defined the Strain Energy Release Rate ($G$) as the elastic energy released per unit of newly created crack area $A$: $$G = -\frac{dU_{\text{el}}}{dA} = \frac{P^2}{2B} \frac{dC}{da}$$ where $P$ is the applied force, $B$ is specimen thickness, $a$ is crack length, and $C = \delta / P$ is the load-line compliance. Fracture instability occurs when the driving force $G$ reaches the material's critical fracture resistance $G_c$: $G \ge G_c$.

In a double-cantilever beam (DCB) fracture specimen of width $B = 25.0\text{ mm}$ and beam half-height $h = 15.0\text{ mm}$ made of an epoxy adhesive ($E = 3.5\text{ GPa}$): The compliance of the DCB specimen is given by beam theory: $$C = \frac{8 a^3}{E B h^3}$$
1. Derive the expression for the strain energy release rate $G$ as a function of load $P$, crack length $a$, $E, B$, and $h$.
2. A critical fracture load $P_c = 420.0\text{ N}$ is measured when the crack length is $a = 60.0\text{ mm}$. Calculate the critical strain energy release rate $G_{Ic}$ of the epoxy.
3. For plane strain, the Mode I stress intensity factor is related to $G_{Ic}$ by $K_{Ic} = \sqrt{\frac{E G_{Ic}}{1 - \nu^2}}$. Calculate $K_{Ic}$ for this epoxy ($\nu = 0.35$).
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Derivation of $G$ for DCB specimen
From compliance $C(a) = \frac{8 a^3}{E B h^3}$: Differentiating with respect to crack length $a$: $$\frac{dC}{da} = \frac{d}{da}\left(\frac{8 a^3}{E B h^3}\right) = \frac{24 a^2}{E B h^3}$$ Substitute into Irwin's compliance formula $G = \frac{P^2}{2B} \frac{dC}{da}$: $$G = \frac{P^2}{2B} \left(\frac{24 a^2}{E B h^3}\right) = \frac{12 P^2 a^2}{E B^2 h^3}$$
Step 2: Calculate critical release rate $G_{Ic}$
Given: $P_c = 420.0\text{ N}$, $a = 0.060\text{ m}$, $B = 0.025\text{ m}$, $h = 0.015\text{ m}$, $E = 3.5 \times 10^9\text{ Pa}$: $$P_c^2 = (420)^2 = 176,400\text{ N}^2$$ $$a^2 = (0.060)^2 = 0.0036\text{ m}^2$$ $$\text{Numerator} = 12 \times 176,400 \times 0.0036 = 7,620.48\text{ N}^2\cdot\text{m}^2$$ $$\text{Denominator} = E B^2 h^3 = (3.5 \times 10^9) \times (0.025)^2 \times (0.015)^3$$ $$B^2 = 0.000625\text{ m}^2, \quad h^3 = 3.375 \times 10^{-6}\text{ m}^3$$ $$\text{Denom} = (3.5 \times 10^9) \times (6.25 \times 10^{-4}) \times (3.375 \times 10^{-6}) = 7,382.81\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$$ $$G_{Ic} = \frac{7,620.48}{7,382.81} \approx 1.0322\text{ N/m} = 1.032\text{ kJ/m}^2 = 1,032\text{ J/m}^2$$
Step 3: Calculate fracture toughness $K_{Ic}$
$$K_{Ic} = \sqrt{\frac{E G_{Ic}}{1 - \nu^2}}$$ $$1 - \nu^2 = 1 - (0.35)^2 = 1 - 0.1225 = 0.8775$$ $$\frac{E G_{Ic}}{1 - \nu^2} = \frac{(3.5 \times 10^9\text{ Pa}) \times (1,032.2\text{ J/m}^2)}{0.8775} = \frac{3.6127 \times 10^{12}}{0.8775} \approx 4.117 \times 10^{12}\text{ Pa}^2\cdot\text{m}$$ $$K_{Ic} = \sqrt{4.117 \times 10^{12}} \approx 2,029,000\text{ Pa}\sqrt{\text{m}} \approx 2.03\text{ MPa}\sqrt{\text{m}}$$
Key Results:
Formula: $G = \frac{12 P^2 a^2}{E B^2 h^3}$
Critical energy release rate: $G_{Ic} = 1,032\text{ J/m}^2$ ($1.03\text{ kJ/m}^2$)
Plane strain fracture toughness: $K_{Ic} = 2.03\text{ MPa}\sqrt{\text{m}}$
Problem 07 Linear Elastic Fracture Mechanics Easy

The Three Fracture Modes and Crack-Tip Stress Singularity

Linear Elastic Fracture Mechanics (LEFM) categorizes relative crack face displacements into three fundamental kinematic modes.

1. Identify and describe the physical loading and displacement kinematics for: (a) Mode I (Opening Mode).
(b) Mode II (In-plane Shear / Sliding Mode).
(c) Mode III (Out-of-plane Shear / Tearing Mode).
2. Why is Mode I by far the most critical and widely tested mode in engineering structural design?
3. The asymptotic elastic stress tensor near a sharp Mode I crack tip is given by the Westergaard equations: $$\sigma_{ij}(r, \theta) = \frac{K_I}{\sqrt{2\pi r}} f_{ij}(\theta)$$ What is the mathematical nature of the stress singularity as the distance from the crack tip $r \to 0$?
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: The Three Fracture Modes
(a) Mode I (Opening Mode): Tensile forces act perpendicular to the crack plane ($\sigma_{yy}$). The two opposing crack faces pull apart symmetrically in the $+y$ and $-y$ directions without sliding relative to each other.
(b) Mode II (In-Plane Shear / Sliding Mode): Shear forces act parallel to the crack plane and perpendicular to the crack front line ($\tau_{xy}$). The two crack faces slide over one another in opposite directions within the crack plane.
(c) Mode III (Out-of-Plane Shear / Tearing Mode): Shear forces act parallel to the crack plane and parallel to the crack front line ($\tau_{yz}$). The crack faces tear apart by sliding past each other in the antiplane $z$-direction (like tearing a sheet of paper with two hands moving in and out of the page).
Step 2: Why Mode I dominates engineering design
1. Lowest Fracture Toughness: For almost all engineering materials, the Mode I fracture toughness $K_{Ic}$ is significantly lower than the shear toughness values ($K_{IIc}$ and $K_{IIIc}$). Materials are much weaker in tensile bond separation than in shear.
2. Crack Path Deflection: Under mixed-mode loading (combined Mode I and Mode II), a propagating crack naturally curls and deflects until it aligns itself perpendicular to the maximum principal tensile stress, converting any shear loading back into a pure Mode I local opening crack.
Step 3: Nature of the $1/\sqrt{r}$ singularity
As the distance from the tip $r \to 0$, the stresses scale as: $$\sigma_{ij} \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{r}} = r^{-1/2}$$ This is the famous inverse square-root singularity ($r^{-1/2}$) characteristic of all linear elastic sharp crack problems. The stress intensity factor $K_I$ uniquely defines the amplitude (intensity) of this singularity field, so knowing $K_I$ completely characterizes the local stress and strain state surrounding the crack tip regardless of global geometry.
Key Results:
Mode I: Tensile opening ($y$-displacement).
Mode II: In-plane shear sliding ($x$-displacement).
Mode III: Out-of-plane tearing ($z$-displacement).
Mode I dominates because materials have the lowest toughness in tension and cracks naturally deflect into pure Mode I.
Crack-tip stresses exhibit an inverse square-root singularity: $\sigma \propto K_I / \sqrt{2\pi r}$.
Problem 08 Linear Elastic Fracture Mechanics Medium

Mode I Stress Intensity Factor and Critical Crack Size

The Mode I stress intensity factor $K_I$ is expressed as: $$K_I = Y \sigma \sqrt{\pi a}$$ where $\sigma$ is the nominal applied tensile stress, $a$ is the crack length (or half-length for an internal crack), and $Y$ is a dimensionless geometric calibration factor.

An aircraft wing skin panel fabricated from 2024-T3 aluminum alloy has a yield strength $\sigma_y = 345\text{ MPa}$ and plane strain fracture toughness $K_{Ic} = 34.0\text{ MPa}\sqrt{\text{m}}$. The panel is subjected to a maximum in-flight tensile stress $\sigma = 230\text{ MPa}$ (two-thirds of $\sigma_y$).

1. Calculate the critical crack size $a_c$ (crack half-length) for an internal through-thickness crack located far from plate boundaries ($Y = 1.00$).
2. Calculate the critical crack size $a_c'$ for an edge crack growing inward from the outer sheet edge ($Y = 1.12$).
3. By what percentage does the edge crack configuration reduce the allowable critical crack size compared to an internal crack?
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Critical crack size for internal through crack ($Y = 1.00$)
Fracture occurs when $K_I = K_{Ic}$: $$K_{Ic} = Y \sigma \sqrt{\pi a_c} \implies \sqrt{\pi a_c} = \frac{K_{Ic}}{Y \sigma} \implies a_c = \frac{1}{\pi}\left(\frac{K_{Ic}}{Y \sigma}\right)^2$$ Given: $K_{Ic} = 34.0\text{ MPa}\sqrt{\text{m}}$, $\sigma = 230\text{ MPa}$, $Y = 1.00$: $$\frac{K_{Ic}}{Y \sigma} = \frac{34.0}{1.00 \times 230} = \frac{34.0}{230} \approx 0.147826\text{ m}^{1/2}$$ $$a_c = \frac{1}{\pi} (0.147826)^2 = \frac{0.0218525}{\pi} \approx 0.006956\text{ m} \approx 6.96\text{ mm}$$ The critical crack half-length is $a_c \approx 6.96\text{ mm}$ (total crack length $2a_c \approx 13.9\text{ mm}$).
Step 2: Critical crack size for edge crack ($Y = 1.12$)

Plane Stress vs. Plane Strain Fracture Toughness ($K_c$ vs. $K_{Ic}$)

When measuring the apparent fracture toughness $K_c$ of a metal plate containing a through-crack as a function of plate thickness $B$: - Very thin sheets exhibit an elevated apparent toughness $K_c \gg K_{Ic}$ with $100\%$ slanted $45^\circ$ shear lips. - As thickness $B$ increases, $K_c$ decreases and asymptotically levels off to a lower-bound plateau value $K_{Ic}$ with flat, square fracture surfaces.

1. Explain the stress state transformation: why do thin sheets experience plane stress ($\sigma_z = 0$) while thick plates experience plane strain ($\varepsilon_z = 0, \sigma_z = \nu(\sigma_x + \sigma_y) > 0$)?
2. Explain how out-of-plane constraint in thick plates suppresses the plastic zone size and lowers apparent fracture toughness.
3. Why is $K_{Ic}$ (plane strain fracture toughness) considered a true, geometry-independent material property, while $K_c$ is not?
4. State the ASTM E399 minimum specimen thickness criterion required to guarantee a valid $K_{Ic}$ measurement.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Stress state transformation
- Thin sheets: Both top and bottom surfaces are traction-free free surfaces ($\sigma_z = 0$). Because the plate is extremely thin, the condition $\sigma_z \approx 0$ extends throughout the entire thickness. The material can contract freely in the thickness direction ($\varepsilon_z = -\frac{\nu}{E}(\sigma_x + \sigma_y)$), creating a plane stress condition.
- Thick plates: Near the plate mid-plane, the surrounding bulk elastic material acts as a rigid clamp preventing lateral contraction in the $z$-direction ($\varepsilon_z = 0$). By Hooke's law, this kinematic constraint generates a large out-of-plane tensile stress: $$\sigma_z = \nu(\sigma_x + \sigma_y) > 0$$ creating a severe triaxial tensile state known as plane strain.
Step 2: Suppression of plastic zone and toughness
Plastic yielding is driven by shear stress (von Mises or Tresca). In plane stress ($\sigma_1 > \sigma_2 > 0, \sigma_3 = 0$), the maximum shear stress is large ($\tau_{\text{max}} = \sigma_1 / 2$). Dislocation slip operates freely on $45^\circ$ planes through the thickness, creating a large plastic zone that absorbs immense plastic work energy. In plane strain, the tensile constraint $\sigma_3 = \sigma_z \approx 2\nu \sigma_1 \approx 0.6\text{--}0.7\sigma_1$ raises the mean hydrostatic stress and compresses the shear stress difference ($\sigma_1 - \sigma_3$). This shrinks the crack-tip plastic zone volume by a factor of 3 to 6: $$r_{p,\text{plane strain}} \approx \frac{1}{3} r_{p,\text{plane stress}}$$ With a severely constricted plastic dissipation zone, the material fractures at a much lower energy release rate.
Step 3: Why $K_{Ic}$ is a true material property
Once thickness $B$ is large enough that plane strain prevails across $> 95\%$ of the crack front, further increases in thickness produce zero change in constraint or plastic zone size. The fracture toughness reaches a reproducible, geometry-independent lower-bound plateau ($K_{Ic}$) that depends only on composition and microstructure, functioning as a true material constant (like Young's modulus).
Step 4: ASTM E399 validity criterion
To ensure full plane strain constraint, ASTM E399 mandates: $$B \ge 2.5 \left(\frac{K_{Ic}}{\sigma_y}\right)^2, \quad a \ge 2.5 \left(\frac{K_{Ic}}{\sigma_y}\right)^2, \quad W - a \ge 2.5 \left(\frac{K_{Ic}}{\sigma_y}\right)^2$$
Key Results:
Thin sheets: Plane stress ($\sigma_z = 0$), free lateral contraction, large plastic zone, high $K_c$, $45^\circ$ shear lips.
Thick plates: Plane strain ($\varepsilon_z = 0$), high tensile triaxiality ($\sigma_z > 0$), plastic zone shrinks by $\sim 3\times$, flat cleavage.
$K_{Ic}$ is a lower-bound geometry-independent material property.
ASTM E399 criterion: $B, a, (W-a) \ge 2.5 (K_{Ic}/\sigma_y)^2$.

Problem 11 Fracture Fundamentals Medium

Theoretical Cleavage Strength of Brittle Solids

In 1949, E. Orowan derived the theoretical cleavage strength $\sigma_{\text{th}}$ required to pull adjacent atomic planes apart simultaneously in a defect-free crystal. Assuming the cohesive restoring stress $\sigma(x)$ varies sinusoidally with displacement $x$: $$\sigma(x) = \sigma_{\text{th}} \sin\left(\frac{2\pi x}{\lambda}\right) \approx \sigma_{\text{th}} \left(\frac{2\pi x}{\lambda}\right) \quad (\text{for small } x)$$ where $\lambda$ is the interaction wavelength. Equating the work of fracture to the surface energy of two newly created cleavage surfaces ($2\gamma_s$): $$\int_0^{\lambda/2} \sigma(x) dx = 2\gamma_s$$
1. Using Hooke's law $\sigma = E (x / a_0)$, show that $\lambda = \frac{2\pi a_0 \sigma_{\text{th}}}{E}$, where $a_0$ is the equilibrium lattice plane spacing.
2. Substitute $\lambda$ into the surface energy integral and derive Orowan's formula: $$\sigma_{\text{th}} = \sqrt{\frac{E \gamma_s}{a_0}} \approx \frac{E}{10}$$ 3. For alumina ceramic ($\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$ sapphire: $E = 380\text{ GPa}$, $\gamma_s = 1.0\text{ J/m}^2$, $a_0 = 0.22\text{ nm}$), calculate $\sigma_{\text{th}}$.
4. The measured tensile fracture strength of bulk polycrystalline alumina is typically only $\sigma_f \approx 300\text{ MPa}$. Calculate the discrepancy ratio $\sigma_{\text{th}} / \sigma_f$ and explain why it exists.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Relate $\lambda$ to Hooke's law
For small displacements ($x \ll \lambda$): $$\sigma(x) \approx \sigma_{\text{th}} \left(\frac{2\pi x}{\lambda}\right)$$ From Hooke's law with strain $\varepsilon = x / a_0$: $$\sigma = E \varepsilon = E \left(\frac{x}{a_0}\right)$$ Equating the slopes: $$\sigma_{\text{th}} \left(\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\right) = \frac{E}{a_0} \implies \lambda = \frac{2\pi a_0 \sigma_{\text{th}}}{E}$$
Step 2: Integration and derivation of $\sigma_{\text{th}}$
The work required to separate the planes to infinity equals the surface energy of two faces ($2\gamma_s$): $$\int_0^{\lambda/2} \sigma_{\text{th}} \sin\left(\frac{2\pi x}{\lambda}\right) dx = \sigma_{\text{th}} \left[-\frac{\lambda}{2\pi}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi x}{\lambda}\right)\right]_0^{\lambda/2} = \sigma_{\text{th}} \frac{\lambda}{2\pi} [-\cos\pi + \cos 0] = \sigma_{\text{th}} \frac{\lambda}{\pi} = 2\gamma_s$$ Substitute $\lambda = \frac{2\pi a_0 \sigma_{\text{th}}}{E}$: $$\sigma_{\text{th}} \frac{1}{\pi} \left(\frac{2\pi a_0 \sigma_{\text{th}}}{E}\right) = 2\gamma_s \implies \frac{2 a_0 \sigma_{\text{th}}^2}{E} = 2\gamma_s \implies \sigma_{\text{th}}^2 = \frac{E \gamma_s}{a_0}$$ $$\sigma_{\text{th}} = \sqrt{\frac{E \gamma_s}{a_0}}$$ Assuming $\gamma_s \approx E a_0 / 100$: $\sigma_{\text{th}} \approx \sqrt{E^2 / 100} = \frac{E}{10}$.
Step 3: Calculate $\sigma_{\text{th}}$ for alumina
Given: $E = 380 \times 10^9\text{ Pa}$, $\gamma_s = 1.0\text{ J/m}^2$, $a_0 = 0.22 \times 10^{-9}\text{ m}$: $$\sigma_{\text{th}} = \sqrt{\frac{(380 \times 10^9) \times 1.0}{0.22 \times 10^{-9}}} = \sqrt{\frac{3.80 \times 10^{11}}{0.22 \times 10^{-9}}} = \sqrt{1.7273 \times 10^{21}} \approx 4.156 \times 10^{10}\text{ Pa} \approx 41.56\text{ GPa}$$ Using the approximation $E/10$: $\sigma_{\text{th}} \approx 38\text{ GPa}$.
Step 4: Ratio and explanation of discrepancy
$$\frac{\sigma_{\text{th}}}{\sigma_f} = \frac{41,560\text{ MPa}}{300\text{ MPa}} \approx 138.5$$ Physical explanation: Bulk ceramics invariably contain microscopic manufacturing defects: sintering pores, microcracks, grain boundary flaws, and surface scratches ($a \sim 10\text{--}50\text{ }\mu\text{m}$). As proven by Inglis and Griffith, these pre-existing flaws create intense stress concentrations that amplify applied loads by $>100\times$, nucleating cleavage at a macroscopic stress of just $300\text{ MPa}$. When tested as defect-free single-crystal nanoneedles or whiskers, alumina approaches its full theoretical strength ($\sim 40\text{ GPa}$).
Key Results:
Derived: $\sigma_{\text{th}} = \sqrt{\frac{E\gamma_s}{a_0}} \approx \frac{E}{10}$
Theoretical cleavage strength of alumina: $\sigma_{\text{th}} \approx 41.6\text{ GPa}$
Discrepancy ratio: $\sigma_{\text{th}}/\sigma_f \approx 139\times$ due to stress-concentrating microflaws and sintering pores.
Problem 12 Ceramic Fracture Medium

Thermal Shock Resistance Parameter and Critical Temperature Drop

When a hot brittle component is suddenly quenched into cold water, rapid surface contraction is constrained by the hotter interior, generating intense biaxial tensile surface stresses: $$\sigma_{\text{thermal}} = \frac{E \alpha_L \Delta T}{1 - \nu}$$ Fracture initiates when $\sigma_{\text{thermal}}$ reaches the fracture strength $\sigma_f$. The maximum quench temperature drop that the material can survive without cracking is the Thermal Shock Resistance Parameter ($R$): $$R = \Delta T_{\text{max}} = \frac{\sigma_f (1 - \nu)}{E \alpha_L}$$
Consider two engineering ceramics: - Ceramic A: Standard Alumina ($\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$): $\sigma_f = 350\text{ MPa}$, $E = 380\text{ GPa}$, $\nu = 0.25$, $\alpha_L = 8.5 \times 10^{-6}\text{ K}^{-1}$. - Ceramic B: Fused Silica Glass ($\text{SiO}_2$): $\sigma_f = 70\text{ MPa}$, $E = 72\text{ GPa}$, $\nu = 0.17$, $\alpha_L = 0.50 \times 10^{-6}\text{ K}^{-1}$.
1. Calculate the thermal shock resistance parameter $R = \Delta T_{\text{max}}$ for both ceramics.
2. Explain why a red-hot crucible ($1000^\circ\text{C}$) made of fused silica can be dropped directly into ice water without cracking, while alumina shatters instantly.
3. Identify the material property that gives fused silica its legendary thermal shock immunity.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Calculate $R = \Delta T_{\text{max}}$ for both ceramics
- Ceramic A (Alumina): $$\text{Numerator} = \sigma_f (1 - \nu) = (350 \times 10^6\text{ Pa}) \times (1 - 0.25) = (350 \times 10^6) \times 0.75 = 2.625 \times 10^8\text{ Pa}$$ $$\text{Denominator} = E \alpha_L = (380 \times 10^9\text{ Pa}) \times (8.5 \times 10^{-6}\text{ K}^{-1}) = 3.230 \times 10^6\text{ Pa/K}$$ $$R_{\text{Alumina}} = \frac{2.625 \times 10^8}{3.230 \times 10^6} \approx 81.27\text{ K} \approx 81^\circ\text{C}$$ Alumina cannot survive a temperature quench exceeding $\Delta T \approx 81^\circ\text{C}$!
- Ceramic B (Fused Silica Glass): $$\text{Numerator} = \sigma_f (1 - \nu) = (70 \times 10^6\text{ Pa}) \times (1 - 0.17) = (70 \times 10^6) \times 0.83 = 5.810 \times 10^7\text{ Pa}$$ $$\text{Denominator} = E \alpha_L = (72 \times 10^9\text{ Pa}) \times (0.50 \times 10^{-6}\text{ K}^{-1}) = 3.600 \times 10^4\text{ Pa/K}$$ $$R_{\text{Silica}} = \frac{5.810 \times 10^7}{3.600 \times 10^4} \approx 1,613.9\text{ K} \approx 1,614^\circ\text{C}$$
Step 2: Comparison and quenching behavior
Quenching from $1000^\circ\text{C}$ into ice water ($0^\circ\text{C}$) imposes a temperature shock $\Delta T = 1000^\circ\text{C}$. - For alumina: $\Delta T = 1000^\circ\text{C} \gg R = 81^\circ\text{C}$. The induced thermal stress exceeds $\sigma_f$ by more than $12\times$, causing immediate catastrophic shattering. - For fused silica: $\Delta T = 1000^\circ\text{C} < R = 1,614^\circ\text{C}$. The thermal stresses remain well below fracture strength, so the silica crucible emerges completely intact without a scratch.
Step 3: Key material property behind silica's immunity
The secret lies entirely in fused silica's exceptionally low coefficient of thermal expansion ($\alpha_L = 0.50 \times 10^{-6}\text{ K}^{-1}$), which is $17\times$ smaller than alumina's. Because the 3D covalent silica network expands and contracts minimally upon temperature change, thermal strains ($\varepsilon_{\text{th}} = \alpha_L \Delta T$) are virtually zero, generating negligible thermal stresses.
Key Results:
Alumina: $R = \Delta T_{\text{max}} \approx 81^\circ\text{C}$
Fused Silica: $R = \Delta T_{\text{max}} \approx 1,614^\circ\text{C}$ ($20\times$ higher resistance)
Fused silica survives extreme thermal shock due to its near-zero thermal expansion coefficient ($\alpha_L = 0.5 \times 10^{-6}\text{ K}^{-1}$).
Problem 13 Linear Elastic Fracture Mechanics Medium

Crack-Tip Plastic Zone Size: Plane Stress vs. Plane Strain

Near the tip of a sharp crack, the elastic stress singularity exceeds the yield strength $\sigma_y$. Irwin showed that plastic yielding redistributes load, creating a circular plastic zone of radius $r_y$: - For Plane Stress: $$r_y = \frac{1}{2\pi} \left(\frac{K_I}{\sigma_y}\right)^2 \quad (\text{total plastic zone diameter } 2r_y = d_p = \frac{1}{\pi}\left(\frac{K_I}{\sigma_y}\right)^2)$$ - For Plane Strain (where triaxial tensile constraint raises effective yield resistance to $\approx 3\sigma_y$): $$r_y = \frac{1}{6\pi} \left(\frac{K_I}{\sigma_y}\right)^2 \quad (\text{diameter } d_p = \frac{1}{3\pi}\left(\frac{K_I}{\sigma_y}\right)^2)$$
A structural steel beam with yield strength $\sigma_y = 450\text{ MPa}$ operates at a Mode I stress intensity factor $K_I = 45.0\text{ MPa}\sqrt{\text{m}}$.

1. Calculate the plastic zone radius $r_y$ and diameter $d_p$ under plane stress conditions.
2. Calculate $r_y$ and $d_p$ under plane strain conditions.
3. What is the ratio of plane stress to plane strain plastic zone volume? Explain how this confirms why thin sheets are tougher than thick plates.
4. State Irwin's effective crack length correction: $a_{\text{eff}} = a + r_y$.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Calculate plastic zone size for Plane Stress
Given: $K_I = 45.0\text{ MPa}\sqrt{\text{m}}$, $\sigma_y = 450\text{ MPa}$: $$\frac{K_I}{\sigma_y} = \frac{45.0}{450} = 0.100\text{ m}^{1/2}$$ $$\left(\frac{K_I}{\sigma_y}\right)^2 = (0.100)^2 = 0.0100\text{ m} = 10.0\text{ mm}$$ Radius: $$r_{y,\text{stress}} = \frac{1}{2\pi} \left(\frac{K_I}{\sigma_y}\right)^2 = \frac{10.0\text{ mm}}{2\pi} \approx 1.5915\text{ mm} \approx 1.59\text{ mm}$$ Diameter: $$d_{p,\text{stress}} = 2 r_{y,\text{stress}} = \frac{10.0\text{ mm}}{\pi} \approx 3.183\text{ mm} \approx 3.18\text{ mm}$$
Step 2: Calculate plastic zone size for Plane Strain
Radius: $$r_{y,\text{strain}} = \frac{1}{6\pi} \left(\frac{K_I}{\sigma_y}\right)^2 = \frac{10.0\text{ mm}}{6\pi} \approx 0.5305\text{ mm} \approx 0.53\text{ mm}$$ Diameter: $$d_{p,\text{strain}} = 2 r_{y,\text{strain}} = \frac{10.0\text{ mm}}{3\pi} \approx 1.061\text{ mm} \approx 1.06\text{ mm}$$
Step 3: Ratio and toughness explanation
$$\frac{r_{y,\text{stress}}}{r_{y,\text{strain}}} = \frac{1 / (2\pi)}{1 / (6\pi)} = 3.0$$ The plastic zone radius is $3\times$ larger in plane stress than in plane strain. Because plastic energy dissipation is proportional to the volume of the plastic zone ($\propto r_y^2 \times B$), the plane stress plastic zone absorbs roughly $3^2 = 9\times$ more energy per unit thickness. This massive energy dissipation explains why thin sheets have vastly higher apparent fracture toughness ($K_c$) than thick plates.
Step 4: Irwin's effective crack length correction
Because the plastic zone yields and cannot carry stresses above $\sigma_y$, load sheds into the surrounding elastic field. The elastic stress field behaves as if it surrounded an imaginary, slightly longer crack extending to the center of the plastic zone: $$a_{\text{eff}} = a + r_y$$ Using $a_{\text{eff}}$ in $K_I = Y \sigma \sqrt{\pi a_{\text{eff}}}$ accounts for small-scale crack tip yielding within linear elasticity.
Key Results:
Plane stress: $r_y = 1.59\text{ mm}$, diameter $d_p = 3.18\text{ mm}$
Plane strain: $r_y = 0.53\text{ mm}$, diameter $d_p = 1.06\text{ mm}$
Plastic zone shrinks by $3\times$ in radius ($9\times$ in area) under plane strain.
Irwin effective crack length: $a_{\text{eff}} = a + r_y$.

Step 1: Calculate hoop stress $\sigma_h$
$$\sigma_h = \frac{p r}{t} = \frac{(4.0\text{ MPa}) \times (600\text{ mm})}{12.0\text{ mm}} = \frac{2400}{12.0} = 200.0\text{ MPa}$$

Crack Tip Opening Displacement (CTOD) and Blunting

When linear elastic fracture mechanics (LEFM) ceases to be valid due to extensive crack-tip plasticity, the Crack Tip Opening Displacement (CTOD, $\delta_t$) serves as a fundamental fracture criterion. Under small-scale yielding, Irwin's and Wells' relationship connects CTOD to the stress intensity factor $K_I$: $$\delta_t = \frac{K_I^2}{m \sigma_y E}$$ where $m \approx 1.0$ for plane stress and $m \approx 2.0$ for plane strain.

A pressure vessel steel ($E = 205\text{ GPa}$, $\nu = 0.30$, yield strength $\sigma_y = 480\text{ MPa}$) operates under plane strain conditions ($m = 2.0$).

1. Calculate the critical crack-tip opening displacement $\delta_{tc}$ at the moment of fracture if the plane strain fracture toughness is $K_{Ic} = 85.0\text{ MPa}\sqrt{\text{m}}$.
2. Calculate the corresponding plastic zone diameter $d_p = \frac{1}{3\pi}\left(\frac{K_{Ic}}{\sigma_y}\right)^2$.
3. Compare $\delta_{tc}$ with $d_p$ and explain the physical meaning of crack-tip blunting prior to unstable tearing.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Calculate critical CTOD $\delta_{tc}$
Given: $K_{Ic} = 85.0\text{ MPa}\sqrt{\text{m}} = 85.0 \times 10^6\text{ Pa}\sqrt{\text{m}}$, $\sigma_y = 480 \times 10^6\text{ Pa}$, $E = 205 \times 10^9\text{ Pa}$, $m = 2.0$: $$K_{Ic}^2 = (85.0 \times 10^6)^2 = 7.225 \times 10^{15}\text{ Pa}^2\cdot\text{m}$$ $$m \sigma_y E = 2.0 \times (480 \times 10^6\text{ Pa}) \times (205 \times 10^9\text{ Pa}) = 1.968 \times 10^{20}\text{ Pa}^2$$ $$\delta_{tc} = \frac{7.225 \times 10^{15}}{1.968 \times 10^{20}} \approx 3.671 \times 10^{-5}\text{ m} \approx 36.71\text{ }\mu\text{m}$$ At the point of fracture instability, the opposing crack faces at the original crack tip have pulled apart by $36.7\text{ }\mu\text{m}$.
Step 2: Calculate plastic zone diameter $d_p$
$$\frac{K_{Ic}}{\sigma_y} = \frac{85.0}{480} \approx 0.17708\text{ m}^{1/2}$$ $$\left(\frac{K_{Ic}}{\sigma_y}\right)^2 = (0.17708)^2 \approx 0.03136\text{ m} = 31.36\text{ mm}$$ $$d_p = \frac{31.36\text{ mm}}{3\pi} = \frac{31.36}{9.4248} \approx 3.327\text{ mm} = 3,327\text{ }\mu\text{m}$$
Step 3: Comparison and physical significance of blunting
$$\frac{d_p}{\delta_{tc}} = \frac{3,327\text{ }\mu\text{m}}{36.71\text{ }\mu\text{m}} \approx 90.6$$ The plastic zone extends over $3.33\text{ mm}$ ($> 90\times$ larger than the CTOD). Physical meaning: An atomistically sharp fatigue crack begins with an initial tip radius of virtually zero ($\rho_t \to 0$). Under rising tensile load, dislocations nucleate from the tip and glide along slip lines inclined at $\pm 45^\circ$. This localized shear opens the crack faces into a rounded, U-shaped blunted notch. This physical stretching and blunting of the tip relaxes peak stresses and delays crack propagation until $\delta_t$ reaches the critical material-specific separation $\delta_{tc}$.
Key Results:
Critical CTOD: $\delta_{tc} = 36.7\text{ }\mu\text{m}$
Plastic zone diameter: $d_p = 3.33\text{ mm}$ ($91\times \delta_{tc}$)
CTOD directly measures the physical plastic blunting of the crack tip before crack tearing commences.

Proof testing provides $2\times$ tighter flaw size assurance than NDI inspection.

Problem 19 Environmental Fracture Hard

Hydrogen Embrittlement: Mechanisms and Delayed Catastrophic Failure

Hydrogen embrittlement (HE) is one of the most insidious failure mechanisms in ultra-high-strength steels ($\sigma_{\text{UTS}} > 1000\text{ MPa}$), titanium alloys, and nickel superalloys. Atomic hydrogen ($\text{H}^+$ or $\text{H}$) dissolves interstitials into the lattice from electroplating, pickling, welding, or cathodic protection, and diffuses rapidly to regions of high tensile hydrostatic stress ahead of crack tips.

1. Distinguish between Internal Hydrogen Embrittlement (IHE) and Environmental Hydrogen Embrittlement (EHE).
2. Explain the two dominant micromechanical theories of hydrogen embrittlement: (a) Hydrogen-Enhanced Decohesion (HEDE) model.
(b) Hydrogen-Enhanced Localized Plasticity (HELP) model.
3. Why are high-strength steels dramatically more susceptible to hydrogen embrittlement than low-carbon structural steels?
4. Describe the standard industrial baking heat treatment ($190^\circ\text{C}\text{ to }210^\circ\text{C}$ for $4\text{--}24\text{ hours}$) used immediately following electroplating of high-strength fasteners to prevent delayed fracture.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: IHE vs. EHE
- Internal Hydrogen Embrittlement (IHE): Hydrogen is absorbed into the bulk metal during prior manufacturing processes (steelmaking, arc welding with damp flux, acid pickling, or electrolytic cadmium/zinc plating) and remains trapped inside the part during service.
- Environmental Hydrogen Embrittlement (EHE): Hydrogen is generated continuously during service from external chemical/electrochemical reactions at the surface (e.g., aqueous corrosion, cathodic protection overpotentials, or exposure to high-pressure hydrogen gas).
Step 2: Two dominant microscopic theories
(a) Hydrogen-Enhanced Decohesion (HEDE): High hydrostatic tensile stress ($\sigma_m$) ahead of a crack tip attracts dissolved interstitial hydrogen atoms ($C_H = C_0 \exp(\sigma_m V_H / RT)$). The accumulated hydrogen donates electrons to the conduction band of host metal atoms, lowering the electron density between metal atoms and weakening the cohesive interatomic bond strength. As a result, brittle cleavage or intergranular separation occurs at stresses far below the normal cleavage strength.
(b) Hydrogen-Enhanced Localized Plasticity (HELP): Hydrogen atmospheres screen the elastic stress fields of dislocations, reducing the repulsive interaction between approaching dislocations. This dramatically increases dislocation mobility and accelerates localized slip along specific shear planes directly ahead of the crack tip. Macroscopically the fracture looks brittle, but microscopically it is caused by intense sub-micron localized plastic shear.
Step 3: Why high-strength steels are exceptionally vulnerable
In high-strength steels ($\sigma_y > 1200\text{ MPa}$), the peak hydrostatic tensile stress ahead of a crack tip reaches $\sigma_m \approx 3\sigma_y \approx 3600\text{--}4500\text{ MPa}$. Because hydrogen segregation scales exponentially with hydrostatic stress ($\propto \exp(\sigma_m V_H / RT)$), hydrogen concentration amplifies by thousands of times at the tip. Furthermore, high-strength steels have very low fracture toughness ($K_{Ic}$) and minimal crack-tip blunting, meaning even a few parts per million (ppm) of dissolved hydrogen is sufficient to trigger delayed catastrophic cracking.
Step 4: Post-plating baking de-embrittlement
Electroplated fasteners (e.g., Grade 8 / 10.9 bolts) are placed in a furnace at $190^\circ\text{C}\text{ to }210^\circ\text{C}$ within 1 to 4 hours of plating. At $200^\circ\text{C}$, the diffusion coefficient of hydrogen in BCC iron is high enough for mobile hydrogen atoms to diffuse out through the plating into the atmosphere, or diffuse away to deep irreversible metallurgical traps (carbide interfaces), permanently reducing diffusible hydrogen below the critical embrittlement threshold ($< 0.1\text{ ppm}$).
Key Results:
IHE absorbs hydrogen during manufacture; EHE absorbs hydrogen from service environments.
HEDE weakens atomic bond cohesive energy; HELP accelerates localized dislocation slip.
High-strength steels suffer massive hydrogen accumulation due to huge crack-tip hydrostatic stresses ($\sigma_m \approx 3\sigma_y$).
Baking at $200^\circ\text{C}$ for $4\text{--}24\text{ h}$ diffuses mobile hydrogen out of the steel, preventing catastrophic delayed fracture.

(c) Lower Shelf Energy (LSE) regime.

Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: The Three Regimes of the Charpy Curve
(a) Upper Shelf Energy (USE): At high temperatures, the metal is fully ductile. Dislocation slip occurs freely, absorbing massive plastic energy ($100\text{ to }250\text{ J}$). The fracture surface is $100\%$ dull, fibrous gray, exhibiting classic microvoid coalescence dimples under SEM.

(c) Lower Shelf Energy (LSE): At low temperatures, the metal becomes completely brittle. Therefore, the steel must have: $$\text{DBTT} \le -40^\circ\text{C} \quad (\text{tested and certified at } -40^\circ\text{C} \text{ with } K_V \ge 40\text{--}50\text{ J})$$

Upper shelf: $100\%$ ductile fibrous ($>100\text{ J}$); Lower shelf: $100\%$ brittle cleavage ($<10\text{ J}$).
Transition definitions: $50\%$ FATT (visual $50/50$ area) and $27\text{ J}$ absorbed energy criterion.

Crystal Structure Dependence of DBTT: BCC vs. FCC and HCP

A profound difference exists in the low-temperature impact behavior of different crystal structures: - Body-Centered Cubic (BCC) metals (ferritic steels, W, Mo, Cr) display a sharp, dramatic DBTT. - Face-Centered Cubic (FCC) metals (aluminum, copper, nickel, austenitic stainless steel) display NO DBTT: their impact energy remains high and ductile even down to liquid helium temperature ($4\text{ K} = -269^\circ\text{C}$). - Hexagonal Close-Packed (HCP) metals (Mg, Zn, Ti) display complex, moderate transition behavior.

1. Explain the fundamental dislocation physics responsible for the presence of a sharp DBTT in BCC metals and its complete absence in FCC metals.
2. Why are cryogenic storage tanks for Liquefied Natural Gas (LNG at $-162^\circ\text{C}$) and liquid nitrogen ($-196^\circ\text{C}$) constructed exclusively from austenitic stainless steel (e.g., AISI 304L), $9\%\text{ Ni}$ steel, or aluminum alloys (Al 5083), and NEVER from standard structural carbon steel?
3. What is the impact behavior of pure titanium (HCP) at cryogenic temperatures?
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Dislocation physics of BCC vs. FCC
- BCC Metals: Screw dislocations in BCC crystals have a non-planar core spread symmetrically across three intersecting $\{110\}$ planes. Moving a screw dislocation requires constricting this non-planar core, which has an immense intrinsic Peierls-Nabarro friction barrier. At low temperatures, thermal vibrations are insufficient to assist constriction, causing the yield stress $\sigma_y(T)$ to skyrocket. When temperature drops below DBTT, the yield stress surpasses the cleavage fracture stress ($\sigma_y > \sigma_F$). The crystal cleaves before dislocations can move, resulting in catastrophic brittle fracture.
- FCC Metals: Dislocation cores in FCC metals are strictly planar on close-packed $\{111\}$ planes. The intrinsic Peierls lattice friction is virtually zero ($\tau_{\text{PN}} \approx 10^{-5} G$). Dislocation slip requires negligible thermal activation, so yield strength increases only modestly as temperature drops. The yield stress never rises high enough to intersect the cleavage fracture stress ($\sigma_y \ll \sigma_F$ at all temperatures). Furthermore, FCC has 12 slip systems satisfying the Taylor criterion. Thus, FCC metals remain fully ductile down to absolute zero ($0\text{ K}$).
Step 2: Material selection for cryogenic LNG tanks ($-162^\circ\text{C}$)
At $-162^\circ\text{C}$, standard carbon and low-alloy steels are far below their DBTT (which is typically $-20^\circ\text{C}\text{ to }+10^\circ\text{C}$). A carbon steel tank would shatter like glass from the slightest pressure surge or vibration, causing catastrophic explosive LNG vaporization. Engineers specify: 1. Austenitic Stainless Steel (AISI 304L / 316L): The FCC austenite phase is stabilized by nickel ($8\text{--}12\%$) and remains exceptionally tough ($K_V > 100\text{ J}$ at $-196^\circ\text{C}$). 2. Step 1: Calculate fatigue parameters
Given: $\sigma_{\text{max}} = +420\text{ MPa}$, $\sigma_{\text{min}} = -140\text{ MPa}$: (a) Stress range $\Delta\sigma$: $$\Delta\sigma = \sigma_{\text{max}} - \sigma_{\text{min}} = 420 - (-140) = 420 + 140 = 560.0\text{ MPa}$$ (b) Stress amplitude $\sigma_a$: $$\sigma_a = \frac{\Delta\sigma}{2} = \frac{\sigma_{\text{max}} - \sigma_{\text{min}}}{2} = \frac{560.0}{2} = 280.0\text{ MPa}$$ (c) Mean stress $\sigma_m$: $$\sigma_m = \frac{\sigma_{\text{max}} + \sigma_{\text{min}}}{2} = \frac{420 + (-140)}{2} = \frac{280}{2} = 140.0\text{ MPa}$$ (d) Stress ratio $R$: $$R = \frac{\sigma_{\text{min}}}{\sigma_{\text{max}}} = \frac{-140}{420} = -\frac{1}{3} \approx -0.333$$ (e) Amplitude ratio $A$: $$A = \frac{\sigma_a}{\sigma_m} = \frac{280.0}{140.0} = 2.000$$ Note identity check: $A = \frac{1 - R}{1 + R} = \frac{1 - (-1/3)}{1 + (-1/3)} = \frac{4/3}{2/3} = 2.00$.

(a) Fully reversed cycling ($\sigma_{\text{max}} = -\sigma_{\text{min}} = \sigma_a$): $$\sigma_m = \frac{\sigma_a + (-\sigma_a)}{2} = 0$$ $$R = \frac{-\sigma_a}{\sigma_a} = \mathbf{-1.00}, \quad A = \frac{\sigma_a}{0} = \boldsymbol{\infty}$$ (b) Pulsating zero-to-tension cycling ($\sigma_{\text{min}} = 0, \sigma_{\text{max}} = 2\sigma_a$): $$\sigma_m = \sigma_a$$ $$R = \frac{0}{\sigma_{\text{max}}} = \mathbf{0.00}, \quad A = \frac{\sigma_a}{\sigma_a} = \mathbf{1.00}$$

Conversely, a compressive mean stress ($\sigma_m < 0$) is highly beneficial: it squeezes crack faces shut during a significant portion of the cycle (crack closure), preventing shear slip decohesion and greatly extending fatigue endurance limit.

Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Calculate fatigue life for $\sigma_a = 450\text{ MPa}$
From $\sigma_a = \sigma_f' (2N_f)^b$: $$(2N_f)^b = \frac{\sigma_a}{\sigma_f'} \implies 2N_f = \left(\frac{\sigma_a}{\sigma_f'}\right)^{1/b}$$ Given: $\sigma_a = 450\text{ MPa}$, $\sigma_f' = 1150\text{ MPa}$, $b = -0.085$: $$\frac{1}{b} = \frac{1}{-0.085} \approx -11.7647$$ $$\frac{\sigma_a}{\sigma_f'} = \frac{450}{1150} \approx 0.391304$$ $$2N_f = (0.391304)^{-11.7647} = \left(\frac{1}{0.391304}\right)^{11.7647} = (2.55556)^{11.7647}$$ $$\ln(2.55556) \approx 0.93827$$ $$\text{Exponent} = 11.7647 \times 0.93827 \approx 11.0384$$ $$2N_f = \exp(11.0384) \approx 62,217\text{ reversals}$$ $$N_f = \frac{62,217}{2} \approx 31,108\text{ cycles} \approx 3.11 \times 10^4\text{ cycles}$$

At $\sigma_a = 540\text{ MPa}$: $N_f = 3,642\text{ cycles}$

Low-Cycle Fatigue (LCF): The Coffin-Manson Relation and Transition Life

In Low-Cycle Fatigue (LCF, $N_f < 10^4$ cycles), cyclic loading exceeds the yield strength, and plastic strain amplitude governs damage accumulation. The total strain amplitude $\frac{\Delta\varepsilon}{2}$ is the sum of elastic and plastic components (Morrow total strain-life equation): $$\frac{\Delta\varepsilon}{2} = \frac{\Delta\varepsilon_e}{2} + \frac{\Delta\varepsilon_p}{2} = \frac{\sigma_f'}{E} (2N_f)^b + \varepsilon_f' (2N_f)^c$$ where the second term is the Coffin-Manson relation for plastic strain life. For a titanium alloy Ti-6Al-4V ($E = 110\text{ GPa}$): $\sigma_f' = 1250\text{ MPa}$, $b = -0.095$, $\varepsilon_f' = 0.35$, $c = -0.60$.

1. Define the transition fatigue life ($2N_t$) where the elastic strain amplitude equals the plastic strain amplitude ($\Delta\varepsilon_e / 2 = \Delta\varepsilon_p / 2$).
2. Calculate the transition life $2N_t$ (in reversals) and $N_t$ (in cycles) for this titanium alloy.
3. If an aerospace compressor disc operates at an imposed total strain range $\Delta\varepsilon = 0.018$ ($\Delta\varepsilon/2 = 0.009$), which component dominates: elastic or plastic strain? Calculate the expected fatigue life $N_f$.
4. Why is high tensile ductility ($\varepsilon_f'$) the most critical material property for surviving low-cycle fatigue, whereas high tensile strength ($\sigma_{\text{UTS}}$) is required for high-cycle fatigue?
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Definition of Transition Life $2N_t$
At the transition life $2N_t$, elastic strain amplitude equals plastic strain amplitude: $$\frac{\sigma_f'}{E} (2N_t)^b = \varepsilon_f' (2N_t)^c$$ Rearranging: $$(2N_t)^{b - c} = \frac{\varepsilon_f' E}{\sigma_f'} \implies 2N_t = \left(\frac{\varepsilon_f' E}{\sigma_f'}\right)^{\frac{1}{b - c}}$$ For lives shorter than $2N_t$ (LCF), plastic strain dominates; for lives longer than $2N_t$ (HCF), elastic strain dominates.
Step 2: Calculate transition life $2N_t$
Given: $\sigma_f' = 1250\text{ MPa}$, $E = 110 \times 10^3\text{ MPa}$, $\varepsilon_f' = 0.35$, $b = -0.095$, $c = -0.60$: $$\frac{\varepsilon_f' E}{\sigma_f'} = \frac{0.35 \times 110,000\text{ MPa}}{1250\text{ MPa}} = \frac{38,500}{1250} = 30.80$$ Exponent: $$b - c = -0.095 - (-0.60) = +0.505$$ $$\frac{1}{b - c} = \frac{1}{0.505} \approx 1.9802$$ Transition reversals: $$2N_t = (30.80)^{1.9802} \approx 893\text{ reversals}$$ Transition cycles: $$N_t = \frac{893}{2} \approx 446\text{ cycles}$$
Step 3: Fatigue life for $\Delta\varepsilon/2 = 0.009$
At $\Delta\varepsilon/2 = 0.009$, the total strain is large, so plastic strain dominates. Testing Coffin-Manson term alone as first estimate: $$\varepsilon_f' (2N_f)^c \approx 0.009 \implies (2N_f)^{-0.60} = \frac{0.009}{0.35} \approx 0.025714$$ $$2N_f = (0.025714)^{-1/0.60} = (0.025714)^{-1.6667} \approx 442\text{ reversals}$$ At $2N_f = 442$: $$\text{Plastic strain} = 0.35 \times (442)^{-0.60} \approx 0.00898$$ $$\text{Elastic strain} = \frac{1250}{110,000} \times (442)^{-0.095} = 0.01136 \times 0.559 \approx 0.00635$$ Total strain $= 0.00898 + 0.00635 = 0.01533 > 0.009$. Solving $\frac{1250}{110,000}(2N_f)^{-0.095} + 0.35(2N_f)^{-0.60} = 0.009$: At $2N_f = 1,400$ reversals: $$\text{Elastic} = 0.01136 \times (1400)^{-0.095} = 0.01136 \times 0.5015 \approx 0.00570$$ $$\text{Plastic} = 0.35 \times (1400)^{-0.60} = 0.35 \times 0.01306 \approx 0.00457$$ Sum $= 0.00570 + 0.00457 = 0.01027$. At $2N_f = 2,000$ reversals: $$\text{Elastic} = 0.01136 \times (2000)^{-0.095} = 0.01136 \times 0.4847 \approx 0.00551$$ $$\text{Plastic} = 0.35 \times (2000)^{-0.60} = 0.35 \times 0.01053 \approx 0.00369$$ Sum $= 0.00551 + 0.00369 = 0.00920 \approx 0.0090$. Thus $2N_f \approx 2,150\text{ reversals} \implies N_f \approx 1,075\text{ cycles}$.
Step 4: Ductility vs. Strength in LCF and HCF
- In Low-Cycle Fatigue (LCF): Deformation is strain-controlled. The material is repeatedly forced through large plastic strains. Plastic strain energy absorption is proportional to ductility ($\varepsilon_f'$). High ductility allows the material to accommodate plastic strain cycles without void nucleation. - In High-Cycle Fatigue (HCF): Strains are small and purely elastic. Failure is stress-controlled. Resisting dislocation emission requires high yield strength and high ultimate tensile strength ($\sigma_{\text{UTS}}$).
Key Results:
Transition life: $2N_t = 893\text{ reversals}$ ($N_t \approx 446\text{ cycles}$).
For $\Delta\varepsilon/2 = 0.009$: $N_f \approx 1,075\text{ cycles}$.
High ductility ($\varepsilon_f'$) governs LCF resistance, whereas high strength ($\sigma_{\text{UTS}}$) governs HCF resistance.

Microstructural Stages of Fatigue: Persistent Slip Bands and Crack Initiation

Fatigue failure progresses through three distinct physical stages: $$\text{Stage I: Microscopic Crack Initiation} \longrightarrow \text{Stage II: Stable Crack Propagation} \longrightarrow \text{Stage III: Catastrophic Overload}$$
1. Describe the formation of Persistent Slip Bands (PSBs) in Stage I under cyclic shearing: (a) What dislocation structure forms inside PSBs (ladder structure)?
(b) Describe how irreversible dislocation glide generates surface extrusions (ridges) and intrusions (notches).
2. Along which crystallographic planes and at what angle to the macroscopic tensile axis does a Stage I crack propagate?
3. What event marks the transition from Stage I initiation to Stage II propagation, and how does the crack growth direction change?
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Persistent Slip Bands (PSBs) and surface topography
(a) Ladder Dislocation Structure: Under cyclic plastic straining, dislocations rearrange to minimize elastic energy, forming thin, ribbon-like zones ($1\text{ to }2\text{ }\mu\text{m}$ wide) called Persistent Slip Bands (PSBs) that traverse entire grains. Inside the PSB, edge dislocations form a dense, periodic array of parallel dipolar walls ("ladder rungs") spaced $\sim 1\text{ }\mu\text{m}$ apart, with virtually dislocation-free channels between them where screw dislocations glide back and forth.
(b) Extrusions and Intrusions: During forward tensile loading, screw dislocations glide on slip plane 1 and cross-slip. Upon load reversal in compression, reverse glide occurs on a neighboring parallel slip plane 2 rather than the original plane (irreversible slip). Over thousands of cycles, this ratcheting net displacement pumps atomic slabs out of the surface, creating microscopic ridges called extrusions (height $\sim 1\text{ }\mu\text{m}$) and sharp micro-notches called intrusions (depth $\sim 1\text{ }\mu\text{m}$).
Step 2: Stage I crack propagation
The sharp notch created by a surface intrusion acts as an intense local stress concentration. A Stage I microcrack initiates at the root of the intrusion and propagates by shear along the primary active close-packed slip planes ($\{111\}$ in FCC) oriented at $45^\circ$ to the macroscopic tensile axis (the orientation of maximum shear stress $\tau_{\text{max}}$). Stage I crack growth is extremely slow (fractions of a nanometer per cycle) and extends only through 1 to 3 grain diameters ($10\text{--}50\text{ }\mu\text{m}$).
Step 3: Transition from Stage I to Stage II
The transition occurs when: 1. The microcrack reaches an impenetrable obstacle (a high-angle grain boundary or hard phase) that blocks single-plane coplanar shear. 2. The stress intensity factor $K_I$ at the crack tip grows large enough to activate multiple symmetric slip systems on intersecting conjugate planes simultaneously. At this transition, the crack turns $45^\circ$ and begins propagating perpendicular ($90^\circ$) to the maximum tensile stress axis via alternating plastic blunting and resharpening, entering Stage II macroscopic propagation.
Key Results:
PSBs contain dislocation ladder structures where irreversible cyclic slip creates surface extrusions and intrusions.
Stage I crack initiates at intrusion roots and propagates along $45^\circ$ maximum shear planes across $1\text{--}3$ grains.
Stage II transition turns the crack to $90^\circ$ perpendicular to tensile stress, advancing via striation formation.

Paris Law for Fatigue Crack Growth: $\frac{da}{dN} = C (\Delta K)^m$

In Stage II fatigue crack propagation, the rate of crack extension per cycle ($da/dN$) is related to the stress intensity factor range $\Delta K$ by the empirical Paris-Erdogan Law: $$\frac{da}{dN} = C (\Delta K)^m$$ where $\Delta K = K_{\text{max}} - K_{\text{min}} = Y \Delta\sigma \sqrt{\pi a}$, $C$ and $m$ are material constants, and $m$ is typically between $2.5$ and $4.0$ for structural metals.

A forged turbine rotor steel has Paris parameters: $C = 1.80 \times 10^{-11}\text{ m/cycle} \cdot (\text{MPa}\sqrt{\text{m}})^{-m}$ and $m = 3.00$. The threshold stress intensity factor range for fatigue crack growth is $\Delta K_{\text{th}} = 5.0\text{ MPa}\sqrt{\text{m}}$, and fracture toughness is $K_{Ic} = 75.0\text{ MPa}\sqrt{\text{m}}$. The rotor is subjected to a cyclic stress range $\Delta\sigma = 220\text{ MPa}$ ($R = 0$, $Y = 1.12$).

1. Calculate the crack growth rate $da/dN$ (in mm/cycle and $\mu\text{m/cycle}$) when the crack length is $a_1 = 3.0\text{ mm}$.
2. Calculate the crack growth rate when the crack has grown to $a_2 = 12.0\text{ mm}$.
3. By what factor did the crack growth rate accelerate between $a = 3\text{ mm}$ and $a = 12\text{ mm}$?
4. Calculate the threshold crack size $a_{\text{th}}$ below which fatigue crack growth cannot occur.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Calculate $da/dN$ for $a_1 = 3.0\text{ mm} = 0.0030\text{ m}$
$$\Delta K_1 = Y \Delta\sigma \sqrt{\pi a_1} = 1.12 \times (220\text{ MPa}) \times \sqrt{\pi \times 0.0030\text{ m}}$$ $$\sqrt{\pi \times 0.0030} = \sqrt{0.0094248} \approx 0.097081\text{ m}^{1/2}$$ $$\Delta K_1 = 1.12 \times 220 \times 0.097081 \approx 23.921\text{ MPa}\sqrt{\text{m}}$$ From Paris Law ($m = 3.00$): $$\frac{da}{dN} = C (\Delta K_1)^3 = (1.80 \times 10^{-11}) \times (23.921)^3$$ $$(23.921)^3 \approx 13,687.9$$ $$\frac{da}{dN} = (1.80 \times 10^{-11}) \times 13,687.9 \approx 2.464 \times 10^{-7}\text{ m/cycle} = 0.2464\text{ }\mu\text{m/cycle} = 2.46 \times 10^{-4}\text{ mm/cycle}$$
Step 2: Calculate $da/dN$ for $a_2 = 12.0\text{ mm} = 0.0120\text{ m}$
Notice that crack length increased by a factor of 4 ($12/3 = 4$). Since $\Delta K \propto \sqrt{a}$, $\Delta K_2 = \Delta K_1 \sqrt{4} = 2 \Delta K_1$: $$\Delta K_2 = 2 \times 23.921 = 47.842\text{ MPa}\sqrt{\text{m}}$$ Because $\Delta K_2 < K_{Ic} = 75$, the rotor is still in stable Stage II growth. $$\frac{da}{dN} = C (\Delta K_2)^3 = C (2 \Delta K_1)^3 = 8 \times \left(\frac{da}{dN}\right)_1$$ $$\left(\frac{da}{dN}\right)_2 = 8 \times (2.464 \times 10^{-7}\text{ m/cycle}) \approx 1.971 \times 10^{-6}\text{ m/cycle} = 1.971\text{ }\mu\text{m/cycle} \approx 1.97 \times 10^{-3}\text{ mm/cycle}$$
Step 3: Acceleration factor
$$\frac{(da/dN)_2}{(da/dN)_1} = \left(\frac{a_2}{a_1}\right)^{m/2} = (4)^{3/2} = (\sqrt{4})^3 = 2^3 = 8.0$$ Quadrupling the crack length accelerates the fatigue crack growth rate by exactly $8.0\times$ ($800\%$).
Step 4: Threshold crack size $a_{\text{th}}$
Crack growth ceases when $\Delta K \le \Delta K_{\text{th}} = 5.0\text{ MPa}\sqrt{\text{m}}$: $$\Delta K_{\text{th}} = Y \Delta\sigma \sqrt{\pi a_{\text{th}}} \implies a_{\text{th}} = \frac{1}{\pi}\left(\frac{\Delta K_{\text{th}}}{Y \Delta\sigma}\right)^2$$ $$Y \Delta\sigma = 1.12 \times 220 = 246.4\text{ MPa}$$ $$\frac{\Delta K_{\text{th}}}{Y \Delta\sigma} = \frac{5.0}{246.4} \approx 0.020292\text{ m}^{1/2}$$ $$a_{\text{th}} = \frac{1}{\pi} (0.020292)^2 = \frac{0.00041177}{\pi} \approx 1.31 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m} = 0.131\text{ mm} = 131\text{ }\mu\text{m}$$ Any surface micro-flaw shallower than $131\text{ }\mu\text{m}$ will never propagate under this cyclic stress range!
Key Results:
At $a = 3\text{ mm}$: $da/dN = 0.246\text{ }\mu\text{m/cycle}$ ($\Delta K = 23.9\text{ MPa}\sqrt{\text{m}}$)
At $a = 12\text{ mm}$: $da/dN = 1.971\text{ }\mu\text{m/cycle}$ ($\Delta K = 47.8\text{ MPa}\sqrt{\text{m}}$)
Growth rate accelerates by $8.0\times$ due to $(a_2/a_1)^{m/2} = 4^{1.5} = 8$.
Fatigue threshold flaw depth: $a_{\text{th}} = 0.131\text{ mm}$ ($131\text{ }\mu\text{m}$).

Integrating the Paris Law for Fatigue Life Prediction

A wide plate in a bridge girder subjected to a cyclic stress range $\Delta\sigma = 150\text{ MPa}$ ($R = 0$) contains an edge crack growing from an initial detectable length $a_0 = 2.0\text{ mm}$ ($0.0020\text{ m}$) to a critical length $a_c$. The steel has fracture toughness $K_{Ic} = 65.0\text{ MPa}\sqrt{\text{m}}$ and Paris law constants $C = 3.0 \times 10^{-11}\text{ m/cycle}\cdot(\text{MPa}\sqrt{\text{m}})^{-3}$ and $m = 3.00$. Assume $Y = 1.12$ is constant over the growth range.

1. Calculate the critical crack size $a_c$ at which final unstable fracture occurs.
2. Derive the closed-form equation for the total fatigue life $N_f$ by integrating $\frac{da}{dN} = C (Y \Delta\sigma \sqrt{\pi a})^m$ for $m \neq 2$: $$N_f = \frac{a_0^{1 - m/2} - a_c^{1 - m/2}}{C (Y \Delta\sigma \sqrt{\pi})^m (m/2 - 1)}$$ 3. Calculate the number of cycles $N_f$ required for the crack to grow from $a_0 = 2.0\text{ mm}$ to $a_c$.
4. Calculate the cycles required to reach $a = 2 a_0 = 4.0\text{ mm}$. What percentage of total fatigue life is consumed in just the first doubling of crack size?
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Calculate critical crack size $a_c$
$$a_c = \frac{1}{\pi}\left(\frac{K_{Ic}}{Y \Delta\sigma}\right)^2$$ $$Y \Delta\sigma = 1.12 \times 150\text{ MPa} = 168.0\text{ MPa}$$ $$\frac{K_{Ic}}{Y \Delta\sigma} = \frac{65.0}{168.0} \approx 0.386905\text{ m}^{1/2}$$ $$a_c = \frac{1}{\pi} (0.386905)^2 = \frac{0.149695}{\pi} \approx 0.047649\text{ m} \approx 47.65\text{ mm}$$
Step 2: Derivation of the integrated Paris equation
$$\frac{da}{dN} = C (Y \Delta\sigma \sqrt{\pi})^m a^{m/2}$$ Separating variables: $$dN = \frac{da}{C (Y \Delta\sigma \sqrt{\pi})^m a^{m/2}} = \frac{1}{C (Y \Delta\sigma \sqrt{\pi})^m} a^{-m/2} da$$ Integrating from $N = 0$ ($a = a_0$) to $N = N_f$ ($a = a_c$): $$N_f = \frac{1}{C (Y \Delta\sigma \sqrt{\pi})^m} \left[\frac{a^{1 - m/2}}{1 - m/2}\right]_{a_0}^{a_c} = \frac{a_0^{1 - m/2} - a_c^{1 - m/2}}{C (Y \Delta\sigma \sqrt{\pi})^m (m/2 - 1)}$$ For $m = 3.00$: $m/2 = 1.50$, so $1 - m/2 = -0.50$, and $m/2 - 1 = 0.50$: $$N_f = \frac{a_0^{-0.5} - a_c^{-0.5}}{0.50\,C (Y \Delta\sigma \sqrt{\pi})^3}$$
Step 3: Numerical calculation of total fatigue life $N_f$
Denominator constants: $$Y \Delta\sigma \sqrt{\pi} = (168.0\text{ MPa}) \times \sqrt{\pi} \approx 168.0 \times 1.77245 \approx 297.77\text{ MPa}\cdot\text{m}^{1/2}$$ $$(Y \Delta\sigma \sqrt{\pi})^3 = (297.77)^3 \approx 26,402,600$$ $$0.50 \times C \times (Y \Delta\sigma \sqrt{\pi})^3 = 0.50 \times (3.0 \times 10^{-11}) \times 26,402,600 \approx 3.9604 \times 10^{-4}$$ Crack length terms: $$a_0 = 0.0020\text{ m} \implies a_0^{-0.5} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{0.0020}} \approx 22.3607\text{ m}^{-1/2}$$ $$a_c = 0.04765\text{ m} \implies a_c^{-0.5} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{0.04765}} \approx 4.5810\text{ m}^{-1/2}$$ Numerator: $$a_0^{-0.5} - a_c^{-0.5} = 22.3607 - 4.5810 = 17.7797\text{ m}^{-1/2}$$ Fatigue life: $$N_f = \frac{17.7797}{3.9604 \times 10^{-4}} \approx 44,894\text{ cycles}$$
Step 4: Life to reach $a = 4.0\text{ mm}$ and life fraction
$$a_1 = 0.0040\text{ m} \implies a_1^{-0.5} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{0.0040}} \approx 15.8114\text{ m}^{-1/2}$$ $$\Delta(\text{Num}) = 22.3607 - 15.8114 = 6.5493\text{ m}^{-1/2}$$ $$N(2\to 4\text{ mm}) = \frac{6.5493}{3.9604 \times 10^{-4}} \approx 16,537\text{ cycles}$$ Percentage of total life: $$\% \text{Life} = \frac{16,537}{44,894} \times 100\% \approx 36.83\%$$ Notice that growing just $2.0\text{ mm}$ (from $2\text{ mm}$ to $4\text{ mm}$) consumes nearly $37\%$ of total life, while the remaining $43.65\text{ mm}$ of crack growth sweeps through the remaining $63\%$ of cycles. The majority of fatigue life is spent when the crack is tiny and growing slowly!
Key Results:
Critical crack size: $a_c = 47.65\text{ mm}$
Total fatigue life: $N_f = 44,894\text{ cycles}$
Cycles to reach $4\text{ mm}$: $N = 16,537\text{ cycles}$ ($36.8\%$ of total life consumed in first $2\text{ mm}$ of growth).

Fatigue cracks easily nucleate in this soft surface skin, slashing the fatigue limit by up to $60\%$.

Shot peening induces deep compressive residual stresses ($\sigma_{\text{res}} < 0$) enforcing crack closure.

The vibration causes oscillatory tangential micro-slip with tiny relative displacements of $5\text{ to }50\text{ }\mu\text{m}$.

(c) Strength reduction: The combined abrasive pitting and severe interfacial shear stresses nucleate microcracks at the edge of the contact patch, reducing the fatigue endurance limit by $50\%\text{ to }75\%$!

(a) Synergistic interaction: Cyclic stress repeatedly ruptures the protective passive oxide film at slip steps on the metal surface. The exposed bare metal anodically dissolves into the electrolyte, forming a sharp microscopic corrosion pit. The pit acts as a severe geometric stress concentrator that nucleates a fatigue crack. The cyclic stress then pumps fresh corrosive fluid in and out of the crack tip, preventing repassivation and accelerating crack growth.
(b) Destruction of the endurance limit: In dry air, steel exhibits a distinct horizontal endurance limit ($\sigma_e$). In a corrosive environment (saltwater, acid rain), the fatigue endurance limit completely disappears! The S-N curve continues to plunge downward with increasing cycles; given enough time, the component will fail at any stress amplitude, no matter how small.
(c) Frequency dependence: Mechanical fatigue depends primarily on the number of cycles ($N$), not wall-clock time. In contrast, electrochemical corrosion is a time-dependent chemical reaction. At low testing frequencies (e.g., $0.1\text{ Hz}$ wave action in offshore structures), there is extensive time per cycle for dissolution and hydrogen ingress, causing far greater crack advance per cycle than at high frequencies ($100\text{ Hz}$).

(c) Secondary (Steady-State) Creep: why is the creep rate $\dot{\varepsilon}_s$ constant, and why is this the most critical engineering design parameter?
(d) Tertiary Creep: why does the creep rate accelerate rapidly toward rupture ($t_r$)?
2. Why is lead ($T_m = 327^\circ\text{C} = 600\text{ K}$) susceptible to severe creep deformation at room temperature ($20^\circ\text{C} = 293\text{ K}$), whereas tungsten ($T_m = 3422^\circ\text{C} = 3695\text{ K}$) does not creep until $T > 1200^\circ\text{C}$?
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: The Four Features of the Creep Curve
(a) Instantaneous Strain ($\varepsilon_0$): The immediate elastic ($\sigma/E$) and possible instantaneous plastic strain upon applying the initial load at time $t = 0$.
(b) Primary (Transient) Creep: The creep rate continuously decreases with time ($d^2\varepsilon/dt^2 < 0$). Microstructural cause: Gliding dislocations multiply and intersect, forming dense tangles and subgrain cell walls. Strain hardening (work hardening) outpaces thermal recovery, progressively increasing the internal resistance to dislocation glide.
(c) Secondary (Steady-State) Creep: The creep rate reaches a constant minimum value: $$\dot{\varepsilon}_s = \frac{d\varepsilon}{dt} = \text{constant}$$ Microstructural cause: A dynamic dynamic equilibrium (balance) is established between work hardening (dislocation multiplication) and thermal recovery (dislocation annihilation and climb over obstacles). Engineering significance: Because secondary creep spans the vast majority ($>80\%$) of the component's service lifetime, the steady-state creep rate $\dot{\varepsilon}_s$ is the primary parameter used in design codes to predict dimensional tolerances and service life.
(d) Tertiary Creep: The creep rate accelerates rapidly ($d^2\varepsilon/dt^2 > 0$), culminating in rupture. Microstructural cause: 1. Microscopic grain boundary cavitation: vacancy condensation nucleates thousands of microscopic cavities along transverse grain boundaries. 2. Cross-sectional necking under constant load increases the true stress ($\sigma = P/A$), accelerating power-law deformation. 3. Microcracks coalesce, leading to intergranular creep rupture at time $t_r$.
Step 2: Homologous temperature comparison
Creep is governed by thermally activated vacancy self-diffusion, which becomes significant only when homologous temperature $T/T_m \ge 0.40$: - Lead at $20^\circ\text{C}$ ($293\text{ K}$): $$\frac{T}{T_m} = \frac{293\text{ K}}{600\text{ K}} \approx 0.49 \gg 0.40$$ Room temperature is half of lead's absolute melting point! Lead pipes and roof flashings creep and sag under their own weight at room temperature. - Tungsten at $20^\circ\text{C}$ ($293\text{ K}$): $$\frac{T}{T_m} = \frac{293\text{ K}}{3695\text{ K}} \approx 0.079 \ll 0.40$$ For tungsten to reach $T/T_m = 0.40$, temperature must reach $T = 0.40 \times 3695\text{ K} \approx 1478\text{ K} \approx 1205^\circ\text{C}$.
Key Results:
Primary: Creep rate decreases as work hardening dominates.
Secondary: Steady-state rate $\dot{\varepsilon}_s$ governed by perfect dynamic balance between work hardening and thermal recovery (climb).
Tertiary: Creep rate accelerates due to grain boundary cavitation, necking, and microcrack linkage.
Lead creeps at room temperature because $20^\circ\text{C}$ is $0.49 T_m$; tungsten requires $> 1200^\circ\text{C}$ to reach $0.40 T_m$.

Steady-State Creep Power Law: Stress Exponent and Activation Energy

The steady-state secondary creep rate $\dot{\varepsilon}_s$ is described by the Norton-Bailey power law: $$\dot{\varepsilon}_s = A \sigma^n \exp\left(-\frac{Q_c}{R T}\right)$$ where $A$ is a materials constant, $\sigma$ is applied stress, $n$ is the creep stress exponent, $Q_c$ is the activation energy for creep, and $R = 8.314\text{ J}/(\text{mol}\cdot\text{K})$.

In high-temperature creep tests conducted on a stainless steel: - Test 1: At $T_1 = 650^\circ\text{C}$ ($923\text{ K}$) and $\sigma_1 = 120\text{ MPa}$, $\dot{\varepsilon}_{s1} = 2.50 \times 10^{-8}\text{ s}^{-1}$. - Test 2: At $T_2 = 650^\circ\text{C}$ ($923\text{ K}$) and $\sigma_2 = 180\text{ MPa}$, $\dot{\varepsilon}_{s2} = 1.90 \times 10^{-7}\text{ s}^{-1}$. - Test 3: At $T_3 = 700^\circ\text{C}$ ($973\text{ K}$) and $\sigma_3 = 120\text{ MPa}$, $\dot{\varepsilon}_{s3} = 1.35 \times 10^{-7}\text{ s}^{-1}$.

1. Using Tests 1 and 2 (constant temperature), calculate the stress exponent $n = \frac{\ln(\dot{\varepsilon}_{s2} / \dot{\varepsilon}_{s1})}{\ln(\sigma_2 / \sigma_1)}$.
2. What does the magnitude of $n$ indicate regarding the dominant creep mechanism (diffusion creep vs. dislocation power-law creep)?
3. Using Tests 1 and 3 (constant stress), calculate the activation energy for creep $Q_c$ (in $\text{kJ/mol}$).
4. Compare $Q_c$ with the activation energy for self-diffusion in $\gamma$-iron ($Q_{\text{diff}} \approx 280\text{ kJ/mol}$) and explain the physical link.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Calculate stress exponent $n$ (from Tests 1 & 2 at $650^\circ\text{C}$)
$$\frac{\dot{\varepsilon}_{s2}}{\dot{\varepsilon}_{s1}} = \left(\frac{\sigma_2}{\sigma_1}\right)^n$$ Taking natural logarithms: $$n = \frac{\ln(\dot{\varepsilon}_{s2} / \dot{\varepsilon}_{s1})}{\ln(\sigma_2 / \sigma_1)} = \frac{\ln(1.90 \times 10^{-7} / 2.50 \times 10^{-8})}{\ln(180 / 120)} = \frac{\ln(7.60)}{\ln(1.50)}$$ $$\ln(7.60) \approx 2.02815, \quad \ln(1.50) \approx 0.405465$$ $$n = \frac{2.02815}{0.405465} \approx 5.002 \approx 5.00$$
Step 2: Dominant creep mechanism from $n = 5$
- Diffusional creep (Nabarro-Herring and Coble) is strictly Newtonian viscous flow with $n = 1.0$. - A stress exponent of $n = 4\text{ to }7$ is the definitive hallmark of Dislocation Power-Law Creep (Weertman dislocation climb-assisted glide). In this regime, dislocations glide along slip planes until they are blocked by forest obstacles or subgrain boundaries. To continue deforming, edge dislocations must climb over obstacles by absorbing or emitting vacancies, making dislocation climb the rate-limiting step.
Step 3: Calculate activation energy $Q_c$ (from Tests 1 & 3 at $\sigma = 120\text{ MPa}$)
$$\frac{\dot{\varepsilon}_{s3}}{\dot{\varepsilon}_{s1}} = \exp\left[-\frac{Q_c}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_3} - \frac{1}{T_1}\right)\right]$$ Taking natural logarithms: $$\ln\left(\frac{\dot{\varepsilon}_{s3}}{\dot{\varepsilon}_{s1}}\right) = -\frac{Q_c}{R} \left(\frac{1}{T_3} - \frac{1}{T_1}\right) = \frac{Q_c}{R} \left(\frac{1}{T_1} - \frac{1}{T_3}\right)$$ Given $T_1 = 923\text{ K}$, $T_3 = 973\text{ K}$: $$\frac{1}{T_1} - \frac{1}{T_3} = \frac{1}{923} - \frac{1}{973} = 0.00108342 - 0.00102775 \approx 5.567 \times 10^{-5}\text{ K}^{-1}$$ $$\frac{\dot{\varepsilon}_{s3}}{\dot{\varepsilon}_{s1}} = \frac{1.35 \times 10^{-7}}{2.50 \times 10^{-8}} = 5.40 \implies \ln(5.40) \approx 1.6864$$ $$Q_c = \frac{R \ln(\dot{\varepsilon}_{s3} / \dot{\varepsilon}_{s1})}{(1/T_1 - 1/T_3)} = \frac{8.314 \times 1.6864}{5.567 \times 10^{-5}} = \frac{14.0207}{5.567 \times 10^{-5}} \approx 251,854\text{ J/mol} \approx 251.9\text{ kJ/mol}$$
Step 4: Comparison with self-diffusion activation energy
$$\frac{Q_c}{Q_{\text{diff}}} = \frac{251.9\text{ kJ/mol}}{280.0\text{ kJ/mol}} \approx 0.90 \approx 1.0$$ The activation energy for steady-state creep ($Q_c \approx 252\text{ kJ/mol}$) closely matches the activation energy for lattice self-diffusion ($Q_{\text{diff}} \approx 280\text{ kJ/mol}$). Physical conclusion: This proves experimentally that power-law creep is rate-controlled by the diffusion of vacancies through the crystal lattice to allow edge dislocations to climb out of their glide planes over obstacles.
Key Results:
Stress exponent: $n = 5.00$ (identifies Dislocation Climb Power-Law Creep).
Activation energy for creep: $Q_c \approx 252\text{ kJ/mol}$.
$Q_c \approx Q_{\text{diff}}$ confirms that dislocation climb rate-controlled by vacancy self-diffusion governs steady-state high-temperature creep.

Diffusional Creep: Nabarro-Herring vs. Coble Creep

At low stresses and high homologous temperatures, deformation occurs by stress-directed vacancy diffusion without dislocation glide (diffusional flow, $n = 1.0$). Two distinct diffusional creep mechanisms exist: 1. Nabarro-Herring Creep (bulk lattice diffusion of vacancies through grain interiors): $$\dot{\varepsilon}_{\text{NH}} = \frac{A_{\text{NH}} D_L \sigma \Omega}{k_B T d^2}$$ 2. Coble Creep (vacancy diffusion channeled along grain boundary networks): $$\dot{\varepsilon}_{\text{Coble}} = \frac{A_{\text{Coble}} \delta D_{\text{gb}} \sigma \Omega}{k_B T d^3}$$ where $d$ is the grain diameter, $D_L$ is lattice diffusivity, $\delta$ is grain boundary width, $D_{\text{gb}}$ is grain boundary diffusivity, and $\Omega$ is atomic volume.

3. At what temperature regime does Coble creep dominate over Nabarro-Herring creep, and why?
4. Explain why coarse grains or single crystals are strictly required for high-temperature creep resistance, directly opposite to room-temperature Hall-Petch requirements.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Grain size sensitivity comparison
- Nabarro-Herring Creep: Vacancies diffuse across the full grain diameter $d$ through the lattice; the diffusion distance is $L \propto d$, and the gradient is $\Delta C / d \propto 1/d$. The strain rate scales inversely with the square of grain diameter: $$\dot{\varepsilon}_{\text{NH}} \propto \frac{1}{d^2}$$ - Coble Creep: Vacancies diffuse along the 2D network of grain boundaries. In addition to the diffusion distance scaling with $d$, the volume fraction of grain boundary channels also scales inversely with grain diameter ($f_{\text{GB}} \propto \delta / d$). Combining both effects yields an extraordinary cubic inverse dependence: $$\dot{\varepsilon}_{\text{Coble}} \propto \frac{1}{d^3}$$
Step 2: Grain coarsening calculation for Coble creep
Given: $d_1 = 5.0\text{ }\mu\text{m}$, $d_2 = 50.0\text{ }\mu\text{m}$: $$\frac{d_2}{d_1} = \frac{50.0}{5.0} = 10$$ Since $\dot{\varepsilon} \propto d^{-3}$: $$\frac{\dot{\varepsilon}_2}{\dot{\varepsilon}_1} = \left(\frac{d_1}{d_2}\right)^3 = \left(\frac{1}{10}\right)^3 = \frac{1}{1000} = 0.001$$ Increasing the grain size by a factor of 10 reduces the Coble creep rate by a factor of 1,000 ($99.9\%$ reduction)!
Step 3: Temperature regime of Coble vs. Nabarro-Herring
Grain boundary diffusion has a much lower activation energy than bulk lattice diffusion ($Q_{\text{gb}} \approx 0.5\text{ to }0.6\,Q_L$) because the disordered boundary has excess free volume and broken bonds. - At moderate homologous temperatures ($0.4\text{ to }0.65 T_m$), lattice diffusion is "frozen," but boundary diffusion is active ($D_{\text{gb}} \gg D_L$). Hence, Coble creep dominates completely. - At very high temperatures ($T > 0.75\text{--}0.80 T_m$), bulk lattice diffusion accelerates exponentially and overwhelms the narrow boundary channels, so Nabarro-Herring creep dominates.
Step 4: The High-Temperature vs. Room-Temperature Inversion
- At Room Temperature (Hall-Petch): Plastic deformation occurs by dislocation glide. Grain boundaries act as impenetrable barriers to dislocations. Therefore, fine grains make metals stronger ($\sigma_y \propto d^{-1/2}$). - At High Creep Temperatures: Plastic deformation is mediated by vacancy diffusion and grain boundary sliding. Grain boundaries act as rapid diffusion short-circuits and sources/sinks for vacancies. Therefore, grain boundaries weaken metals at high temperatures ($\dot{\varepsilon} \propto d^{-3}$). To maximize creep resistance, engineers eliminate grain boundaries entirely by casting single-crystal turbine blades.
Key Results:
Nabarro-Herring scales as $d^{-2}$; Coble scales as $d^{-3}$.
Increasing grain size by $10\times$ slashes Coble creep rate by $1,000\times$ ($0.1\%$).
Coble dominates at lower temperatures ($0.4\text{--}0.65 T_m$) because $Q_{\text{gb}} \approx 0.5 Q_L$.
Fine grains strengthen at room temperature (Hall-Petch) but destroy creep resistance at high temperatures, necessitating single-crystal alloys.

(b) Dislocation Glide / Low-Temperature Plasticity: Dominates at $T < 0.35 T_m$ and high stress ($\tau > \tau_y$). Dislocation motion is governed by short-range obstacle cutting and Peierls lattice friction.
(c) Power-Law Dislocation Creep: Dominates at $0.4 T_m \le T \le 0.8 T_m$ and moderate stresses ($10^{-4} \le \tau/G \le 10^{-2}$). Dislocation glide is rate-limited by vacancy diffusion-assisted dislocation climb over obstacles ($n = 4\text{ to }7$).
(d) Diffusional Flow: Dominates at low stresses ($\tau/G < 10^{-4}$) and elevated temperatures ($T > 0.4 T_m$). Stress-directed vacancy migration flows between grain boundaries without dislocation motion ($n = 1$): - Coble Creep: Dominates at lower temperatures ($0.4\text{--}0.65 T_m$) where grain boundary diffusion ($D_{\text{gb}}$) is much faster than lattice diffusion. - Nabarro-Herring Creep: Dominates at very high temperatures ($T > 0.75 T_m$) where bulk lattice diffusion ($D_L$) activates.
(e) Elastic Field: Low stresses below yield and low temperatures where only reversible elastic Hookean deformation occurs.
Step 2: Coble vs. Nabarro-Herring boundary
The boundary where both diffusional mechanisms contribute equally is found by setting $\dot{\varepsilon}_{\text{Coble}} = \dot{\varepsilon}_{\text{NH}}$: $$\frac{A_{\text{Coble}} \delta D_{\text{gb}}}{d^3} = \frac{A_{\text{NH}} D_L}{d^2} \implies d_{\text{boundary}} \approx C \delta \frac{D_{\text{gb}}}{D_L}$$ Because $D_{\text{gb}} / D_L \propto \exp\left(\frac{Q_L - Q_{\text{gb}}}{RT}\right)$, as temperature increases, the ratio $D_{\text{gb}} / D_L$ shrinks rapidly. Therefore: - In fine-grained materials, Coble creep expands across a wide temperature range. - In coarse-grained materials, Coble creep shrinks to a tiny sliver, and Nabarro-Herring creep dominates.
Step 3: Engineering design using Ashby maps
By pinpointing the component's exact operating conditions $(\sigma/E, T/T_m)$ on the map, the engineer identifies the active microscopic mechanism: 1. If operating in the Coble or Nabarro-Herring diffusional field, increasing grain size ($d \uparrow$) or eliminating grain boundaries entirely (single crystal) will dramatically lower creep rate ($\dot{\varepsilon} \propto d^{-3}$ or $d^{-2}$). 2. If operating in the Dislocation Power-Law field, changing grain size has virtually zero effect! Instead, the engineer must introduce fine insoluble precipitate particles (e.g., $\gamma'$ in superalloys) to block dislocation climb and looping.
Key Results:
Fields: Theoretical strength $\to$ Dislocation glide $\to$ Power-law climb creep $\to$ Diffusional flow (Coble/N-H) $\to$ Elastic.
Coble dominates at intermediate $T$ and fine grain sizes; Nabarro-Herring dominates near melting.
Ashby maps guide targeted metallurgical countermeasures: increase grain size against diffusional creep; add coherent precipitates against power-law climb creep.

The bronze has a Vickers hardness $H = 120\text{ HV} \approx 1.18 \times 10^9\text{ N/m}^2$ ($1.18\text{ GPa}$) and an adhesive wear coefficient $K = 2.4 \times 10^{-5}$. The shaft rotates at $N = 300\text{ rpm}$ with a journal radius $R = 40.0\text{ mm}$.

1. Calculate the sliding speed $v$ (in m/s) and the total sliding distance $L$ accumulated after $1000\text{ hours}$ of continuous operation.
2. Calculate the total volume of bronze worn away $V_{\text{wear}}$ (in $\text{mm}^3$).
3. The bearing contact area is $A = 2400\text{ mm}^2$. Calculate the uniform wear depth $h_{\text{wear}} = V_{\text{wear}} / A$ (in mm).
4. State the fundamental design principle implied by Archard's equation for maximizing wear resistance.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Calculate sliding speed $v$ and total sliding distance $L$
Shaft diameter: $D = 2R = 0.080\text{ m}$. Circumference per revolution: $$C_{\text{rev}} = \pi D = \pi \times 0.080\text{ m} \approx 0.251327\text{ m/rev}$$ Sliding speed: $$v = C_{\text{rev}} \times \left(\frac{300\text{ rev}}{60\text{ s}}\right) = 0.251327 \times 5.0\text{ rev/s} \approx 1.2566\text{ m/s}$$ Total operating time in seconds: $$t = 1000\text{ h} \times 3,600\text{ s/h} = 3.60 \times 10^6\text{ s}$$ Total sliding distance: $$L = v \times t = (1.2566\text{ m/s}) \times (3.60 \times 10^6\text{ s}) \approx 4,523,893\text{ m} \approx 4.524 \times 10^6\text{ m}$$
Step 2: Calculate worn volume $V_{\text{wear}}$
Given: $K = 2.4 \times 10^{-5}$, $W = 4,500\text{ N}$, $H = 1.18 \times 10^9\text{ N/m}^2$: $$V_{\text{wear}} = K \frac{W L}{H} = (2.4 \times 10^{-5}) \times \frac{(4500\text{ N}) \times (4.5239 \times 10^6\text{ m})}{1.18 \times 10^9\text{ N/m}^2}$$ $$\text{Numerator} = (2.4 \times 10^{-5}) \times (2.03575 \times 10^{10}\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}) = 4.8858 \times 10^5\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$$ $$V_{\text{wear}} = \frac{4.8858 \times 10^5}{1.18 \times 10^9} \approx 4.1405 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^3$$ Convert to $\text{mm}^3$ ($1\text{ m}^3 = 10^9\text{ mm}^3$): $$V_{\text{wear}} = (4.1405 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^3) \times 10^9\text{ mm}^3/\text{m}^3 \approx 414,050\text{ mm}^3 \approx 414.1\text{ cm}^3$$ (With proper boundary lubrication, $K$ drops by $1000\times$ to $10^{-8}$, giving $V_{\text{wear}} \approx 0.41\text{ cm}^3$).
Step 3: Calculate wear depth $h_{\text{wear}}$
Given contact area $A = 2400\text{ mm}^2$: $$h_{\text{wear}} = \frac{V_{\text{wear}}}{A} = \frac{414,050\text{ mm}^3}{2400\text{ mm}^2} \approx 172.5\text{ mm} \quad (\text{unlubricated dry sliding})$$ Under hydrodynamic oil film lubrication ($K = 2.4 \times 10^{-8}$): $$h_{\text{wear, lubricated}} = \frac{172.5\text{ mm}}{1000} \approx 0.173\text{ mm} = 173\text{ }\mu\text{m}$$
Step 4: Fundamental design rule for wear resistance
According to Archard's equation ($V_{\text{wear}} \propto 1/H$): To maximize sliding wear resistance: 1. Maximize surface hardness ($H$): Use case hardening (carburizing, nitriding, induction hardening) or hard ceramic coatings (TiN, CrN, diamond-like carbon DLC). 2. Minimize wear coefficient ($K$): Maintain full hydrodynamic fluid-film lubrication to eliminate direct metal-to-metal contact, and pair metallurgically incompatible metals that resist adhesive cold-welding.
Key Results:
Sliding distance: $L = 4,524\text{ km}$ ($v = 1.26\text{ m/s}$).
Dry worn volume: $V_{\text{wear}} = 414.1\text{ cm}^3$; Lubricated wear depth: $h_{\text{wear}} \approx 173\text{ }\mu\text{m}$.
Archard principle: Wear volume is inversely proportional to surface hardness ($V \propto 1/H$); maximize hardness and maintain hydrodynamic lubrication.

Comprehensive Failure Analysis: Jet Engine Turbine Shaft Forensic Diagnosis

A high-pressure jet engine turbine rotor shaft fabricated from nickel-base superalloy Inconel 718 fractured catastrophically during flight, triggering an uncontained engine shutdown. A forensic engineering metallurgical team conducts an investigation of the fractured shaft.

The laboratory examination reveals the following physical findings: - Visual Inspection: A single fracture surface originating at a sharp internal oil lubricating hole (fillet radius $R = 0.20\text{ mm}$). Clear macroscopic, curved beachmarks originate at the hole and radiate across $65\%$ of the shaft cross-sectional area. - SEM Fractography (Mid-Section): High-magnification ($15,000\times$) electron micrographs reveal regular, parallel striations perpendicular to the propagation direction, with an average spacing of $\delta = 0.45\text{ }\mu\text{m/cycle}$. - SEM Fractography (Origin Zone): Intergranular micro-cavities and micro-cracks along transverse grain boundaries surrounded by heavy chromium oxide scale ($\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_3$). - SEM Fractography (Final Zone): $100\%$ dimpled rupture (microvoid coalescence) inclined at $45^\circ$ to the shaft axis.

1. Synthesize these forensic findings and reconstruct the sequential chain of failure: (a) Identify the precise initiation site and root cause defect.
(b) Identify the mode and mechanism of Stage I crack initiation.
(c) Identify the mode and mechanism of Stage II crack propagation.
(d) Identify the final fracture mode in Stage III.
2. Using the measured striation spacing $\delta = 0.45\text{ }\mu\text{m/cycle}$ and Paris Law parameters for Inconel 718 ($C = 1.20 \times 10^{-11}\text{ m/cycle}\cdot(\text{MPa}\sqrt{\text{m}})^{-3.2}, m = 3.20$), calculate the effective cyclic stress intensity range $\Delta K$ acting during mid-life propagation.
3. Formulate three mandatory engineering corrective actions to permanently prevent future turbine shaft failures.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Sequential Forensic Failure Reconstruction
(a) Initiation Site & Root Cause: The fracture initiated at the sharp internal oil lubrication hole. The tiny fillet radius ($R = 0.20\text{ mm}$) acted as a severe geometric notch stress concentration ($K_t \approx 3.5\text{--}4.0$), amplifying centrifugal and torsional cyclic operating stresses.
(b) Stage I Initiation (Creep-Fatigue & Oxidation): The presence of intergranular micro-cavities and heavy chromium oxide scale at the origin proves that initiation was driven by high-temperature creep-fatigue interaction and localized oxidation. Prolonged engine cruise dwell times under high thermal and centrifugal stresses nucleated creep cavities along transverse grain boundaries at the notch root.
(c) Stage II Propagation (Mechanical Fatigue): The clear macroscopic beachmarks and fine microscopic fatigue striations ($\delta = 0.45\text{ }\mu\text{m}$) confirm classic Stage II cyclic mechanical fatigue crack propagation. The crack grew cycle-by-cycle over thousands of flight take-off/climb/cruise duty cycles, advancing across $65\%$ of the shaft cross-section without detection.
(d) Stage III Final Rupture: Once the fatigue crack consumed $65\%$ of the cross-section, the net remaining ligament could no longer support the maximum engine torque. The stress intensity factor exceeded the fracture toughness ($K_I \ge K_{Ic}$), triggering sudden, unstable ductile overload shear rupture (microvoid coalescence dimples) at $45^\circ$ shear lips.
Step 2: Calculate effective stress intensity range $\Delta K$
Because each fatigue striation corresponds approximately to the crack advance per cycle: $$\frac{da}{dN} \approx \delta = 0.45\text{ }\mu\text{m/cycle} = 4.50 \times 10^{-7}\text{ m/cycle}$$ From Paris Law $\frac{da}{dN} = C (\Delta K)^m$: $$(\Delta K)^m = \frac{da/dN}{C} \implies \Delta K = \left(\frac{da/dN}{C}\right)^{1/m}$$ Given $C = 1.20 \times 10^{-11}$, $m = 3.20$: $$\frac{da/dN}{C} = \frac{4.50 \times 10^{-7}}{1.20 \times 10^{-11}} = 37,500$$ $$\Delta K = (37,500)^{1/3.20} = (37,500)^{0.3125} \approx 26.85\text{ MPa}\sqrt{\text{m}}$$ During mid-life propagation, the crack tip experienced an effective cyclic stress intensity range of $\Delta K \approx 26.9\text{ MPa}\sqrt{\text{m}}$.
Step 3: Mandatory Engineering Corrective Actions
1. Design Modification (Reduce $K_t$): Increase the oil hole fillet radius from $R = 0.20\text{ mm}$ to $R \ge 1.50\text{ mm}$ and incorporate an elliptical chamfer relief, reducing the stress concentration factor $K_t$ from $3.8$ down to $< 1.8$.
2. Surface Engineering (Compressive Residual Stresses): Mandate automated internal shot peening or ball burnishing inside all oil lubrication bores to induce deep compressive residual stresses ($\sigma_{\text{res}} \le -600\text{ MPa}$), counteracting the tensile mean stress and preventing creep-fatigue crack nucleation.
3. NDI Inspection Protocol: Implement mandatory periodic ultrasonic or eddy current non-destructive inspection (NDI) of internal oil bores at intervals calculated using fracture mechanics integration ($N_{\text{inspect}} = N_f / 2 \approx 5,000\text{ flight cycles}$), guaranteeing detection of subcritical cracks before they exceed the safe threshold.
Key Results:
Root cause: Severe stress concentration at sharp oil hole ($R = 0.2\text{ mm}$) combined with high-temperature creep-fatigue and oxidation.
Stage II propagation: Cyclic mechanical fatigue advancing $0.45\text{ }\mu\text{m/cycle}$ across $65\%$ of cross-section ($\Delta K \approx 26.9\text{ MPa}\sqrt{\text{m}}$).
Stage III final failure: Rapid ductile shear overload rupture.
Corrective actions: Increase fillet radius to $R \ge 1.5\text{ mm}$, shot peen internal bore for compressive residual stress, and institute periodic ultrasonic NDI inspections.
