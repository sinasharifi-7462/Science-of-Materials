Problem 01 Dislocation Basics Medium

Theoretical Shear Strength vs. Actual Yield Strength of Crystals

In 1926, J. Frenkel calculated the theoretical shear strength $\tau_{\text{theo}}$ of an ideal defect-free crystal by assuming that adjacent atomic planes slide simultaneously over one another: $$\tau = \tau_{\text{theo}} \sin\left(\frac{2\pi x}{b}\right) \approx \tau_{\text{theo}} \frac{2\pi x}{b}$$ For small displacements, Hooke's law in shear gives $\tau = G \gamma = G (x / d)$, where $d$ is the interplanar spacing and $b$ is the Burgers vector.

1. Derive Frenkel's formula for theoretical shear strength in terms of shear modulus $G$, Burgers vector $b$, and interplanar spacing $d$. Assuming $b \approx d$, show that $\tau_{\text{theo}} \approx \frac{G}{2\pi} \approx \frac{G}{6.28}$.
2. For pure aluminum ($G = 26\text{ GPa}$), calculate $\tau_{\text{theo}}$.
3. The measured critical resolved shear stress of high-purity single-crystal aluminum is only $\tau_{\text{CRSS}} \approx 1.0\text{ MPa}$. Calculate the ratio $\tau_{\text{theo}} / \tau_{\text{CRSS}}$ and explain why the actual strength is three to four orders of magnitude lower.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Derivation of Frenkel's theoretical shear strength
For very small elastic displacements ($x \ll b$): $$\tau \approx \tau_{\text{theo}} \left(\frac{2\pi x}{b}\right)$$ From Hooke's law, with shear strain $\gamma = x/d$: $$\tau = G \gamma = G \left(\frac{x}{d}\right)$$ Equating the two expressions as $x \to 0$: $$\tau_{\text{theo}} \left(\frac{2\pi x}{b}\right) = G \left(\frac{x}{d}\right) \implies \tau_{\text{theo}} = \frac{G}{2\pi} \left(\frac{b}{d}\right)$$ For close-packed planes where the atomic repeat distance equals the interplanar spacing ($b \approx d$): $$\tau_{\text{theo}} \approx \frac{G}{2\pi} \approx 0.16\,G \approx \frac{G}{6}$$ More refined sinusoidal and interatomic potential models (Mackenzie, Tyson) give $\tau_{\text{theo}} \approx G/30 \text{ to } G/10$.
Step 2: Calculate $\tau_{\text{theo}}$ for aluminum
Given $G = 26\text{ GPa} = 26,000\text{ MPa}$: $$\tau_{\text{theo}} = \frac{26,000}{2\pi} \approx 4,138\text{ MPa} \approx 4.14\text{ GPa}$$
Step 3: Comparison with actual single-crystal yield strength
$$\frac{\tau_{\text{theo}}}{\tau_{\text{CRSS}}} = \frac{4,138\text{ MPa}}{1.0\text{ MPa}} \approx 4,138$$ Physical explanation: In a perfect crystal, plastic shear requires breaking all interatomic bonds simultaneously across the entire slip plane. In real metals, plastic slip is mediated by the glide of dislocations (line defects). A dislocation moves like a ripple in a heavy rug or a caterpillar crawling: only one line of atomic bonds breaks and reforms at any instant, reducing the required force by a factor of 1,000 to 10,000.
Key Results:
Frenkel formula: $\tau_{\text{theo}} = \frac{G}{2\pi}\frac{b}{d} \approx \frac{G}{2\pi}$
Theoretical shear strength for Al: $\tau_{\text{theo}} \approx 4.14\text{ GPa}$
Actual CRSS $= 1.0\text{ MPa}$ ($\sim 4100\times$ lower) due to dislocation-mediated slip.
Problem 02 Dislocation Basics Hard

Peierls-Nabarro Lattice Friction Stress

The intrinsic resistance of a crystal lattice to dislocation motion in the absence of thermal activation is given by the Peierls-Nabarro friction stress: $$\tau_{\text{PN}} = \frac{2G}{1-\nu} \exp\left(-\frac{2\pi w}{b}\right)$$ where $w$ is the dislocation core width, given by $w = \frac{d}{1-\nu}$, $d$ is the interplanar spacing, and $b$ is the magnitude of the Burgers vector.

1. Show that substituting $w$ into the formula yields: $$\tau_{\text{PN}} = \frac{2G}{1-\nu} \exp\left(-\frac{2\pi d}{b(1-\nu)}\right)$$ 2. For an FCC metal ($\nu = 0.34$, $G = 40\text{ GPa}$), dislocation glide occurs on close-packed $\{111\}$ planes with $d = a/\sqrt{3}$ and along $\langle 110\rangle$ with $b = a/\sqrt{2}$. Calculate the ratio $d/b$ and the resulting Peierls stress $\tau_{\text{PN}}$.
3. If the dislocation were forced to glide on the less densely packed $\{100\}$ planes where $d = a/2$ (same $b = a/\sqrt{2}$), calculate the new ratio $d/b$ and the resulting $\tau_{\text{PN}}$.
4. Based on your results, explain why slip in crystals occurs preferentially on planes with the largest interplanar spacing and smallest Burgers vector.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Formula substitution
Substituting $w = \frac{d}{1-\nu}$ into the exponential: $$\tau_{\text{PN}} = \frac{2G}{1-\nu} \exp\left(-\frac{2\pi d}{b(1-\nu)}\right)$$ The pre-exponential factor is: $$\frac{2G}{1-\nu} = \frac{2 \times 40\text{ GPa}}{1 - 0.34} = \frac{80}{0.66} \approx 121.2\text{ GPa} = 121,212\text{ MPa}$$
Step 2: Peierls stress for $\{111\}$ slip in FCC
$$\frac{d}{b} = \frac{a/\sqrt{3}}{a/\sqrt{2}} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{3}} = \sqrt{\frac{2}{3}} \approx 0.8165$$ The exponent argument is: $$\frac{2\pi d}{b(1-\nu)} = \frac{2\pi \times 0.8165}{0.66} \approx \frac{5.1302}{0.66} \approx 7.773$$ $$\tau_{\text{PN}, \{111\}} = 121,212 \times \exp(-7.773) = 121,212 \times (4.209 \times 10^{-4}) \approx 51.0\text{ MPa} \approx 1.3 \times 10^{-3} G$$ (In reality, core spreading and partial dislocations lower this to $\sim 10^{-5} G \approx 0.4\text{ MPa}$).
Step 3: Peierls stress for $\{100\}$ slip in FCC
$$\frac{d}{b} = \frac{a/2}{a/\sqrt{2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.7071$$ $$\frac{2\pi d}{b(1-\nu)} = \frac{2\pi \times 0.7071}{0.66} \approx \frac{4.4429}{0.66} \approx 6.7317$$ $$\tau_{\text{PN}, \{100\}} = 121,212 \times \exp(-6.7317) = 121,212 \times (1.1925 \times 10^{-3}) \approx 144.5\text{ MPa}$$ This is nearly 3 times higher than on $\{111\}$ planes.
Step 4: Fundamental rule of slip systems
Because the interplanar spacing $d$ appears in the negative exponent: 1. Maximizing $d$ minimizes $\tau_{\text{PN}}$ exponentially. Planes with the largest interplanar spacing are the most widely separated and have the highest atomic planar packing density. 2. Minimizing the Burgers vector $b$ (shortest repeat distance, along close-packed directions) also maximizes the ratio $d/b$ and minimizes the strain energy of the dislocation ($E \propto G b^2$). Therefore, dislocation slip always operates on close-packed planes along close-packed directions.
Key Results:
$\{111\}$ planes: $d/b = \sqrt{2/3} \approx 0.816 \implies \tau_{\text{PN}} \approx 51\text{ MPa}$
$\{100\}$ planes: $d/b = 1/\sqrt{2} \approx 0.707 \implies \tau_{\text{PN}} \approx 145\text{ MPa}$
Widely spaced, close-packed planes drastically minimize Peierls friction stress due to exponential sensitivity on $d/b$.
Problem 03 Crystallography of Slip Easy

Slip Systems in FCC, BCC, and HCP Crystal Structures

A slip system is defined as the combination of a slip plane and a slip direction lying within that plane.

1. For Face-Centered Cubic (FCC): identify the family of slip planes, the family of slip directions, and calculate the total number of independent slip systems.
2. For Body-Centered Cubic (BCC): identify the primary slip plane family and the two additional potential slip plane families at elevated temperatures (all sharing the same slip direction family), and calculate the total number of slip systems.
3. For Hexagonal Close-Packed (HCP): identify the basal slip planes and directions, and calculate the number of basal slip systems.
4. Explain why FCC metals (e.g., Cu, Al, Au) are ductile at all temperatures down to absolute zero, whereas HCP metals (e.g., Mg, Zn) exhibit limited room-temperature ductility.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Slip systems in FCC
- Slip planes: $\{111\}$ family (4 non-parallel octahedral planes: $(111), (\bar{1}11), (1\bar{1}1), (11\bar{1})$).
- Slip directions: $\langle 110\rangle$ close-packed directions lying in each plane (3 directions per plane: $\pm [1\bar{1}0], \pm [10\bar{1}], \pm [01\bar{1}]$).
- Total slip systems: $4 \times 3 = \mathbf{12\text{ slip systems}}$.
Step 2: Slip systems in BCC
- Slip directions: $\langle 111\rangle$ (the 4 body diagonals: $[111], [\bar{1}11], [1\bar{1}1], [11\bar{1}]$).
- Slip planes: - Primary: $\{110\}$ (6 planes $\times 2$ directions/plane $= 12$ systems) - Secondary (moderate $T$): $\{112\}$ (12 planes $\times 1$ direction/plane $= 12$ systems) - Tertiary (high $T$): $\{123\}$ (24 planes $\times 1$ direction/plane $= 24$ systems)
- Total potential slip systems: $12 + 12 + 24 = \mathbf{48\text{ slip systems}}$.
Step 3: Slip systems in HCP (Basal)
- Basal slip plane: $(0001)$ (only 1 unique parallel plane family).
- Close-packed directions: $\langle 11\bar{2}0\rangle$ (3 directions at $120^\circ$: $[2\bar{1}\bar{1}0], [\bar{1}2\bar{1}0], [\bar{1}\bar{1}20]$).
- Total basal slip systems: $1 \times 3 = \mathbf{3\text{ slip systems}}$.
Step 4: Taylor criterion and ductility
According to the Taylor criterion (von Mises condition), a polycrystalline material requires at least 5 independent slip systems to accommodate an arbitrary generalized plastic shape change without opening grain boundary voids or cracking.
- FCC metals have 12 slip systems, 5 of which are geometrically independent at all temperatures. Hence FCC metals exhibit high ductility even at liquid helium temperatures.
- HCP metals have only 3 basal slip systems (2 independent), which do not provide any deformation along the $c$-axis. At room temperature, non-basal slip (prismatic $\{10\bar{1}0\}$ or pyramidal $\{10\bar{1}1\}$) requires very high stress, leading to brittle intergranular fracture unless deformation twinning is activated.
Key Results:
FCC: $\{111\}\langle 110\rangle = 12$ slip systems.
BCC: $\{110\}\langle 111\rangle, \{112\}\langle 111\rangle, \{123\}\langle 111\rangle = 48$ slip systems.
HCP: $(0001)\langle 11\bar{2}0\rangle = 3$ basal slip systems.
FCC satisfies the 5 independent slip system Taylor criterion at all temperatures, while HCP fails it at low/room temperatures.
Problem 04 Schmid's Law Medium

Schmid's Law and Resolved Shear Stress Derivation

Consider a cylindrical single-crystal tensile specimen of cross-sectional area $A_0$ loaded axially with a tensile force $F$. Let $\mathbf{n}$ be the unit normal vector to a slip plane, and $\mathbf{s}$ be the unit vector along a slip direction within that plane. The angle between the tensile axis and $\mathbf{n}$ is $\phi$, and the angle between the tensile axis and $\mathbf{s}$ is $\lambda$.

1. Derive Schmid's law for the resolved shear stress $\tau_R$ acting along the slip direction in terms of nominal tensile stress $\sigma = F / A_0$, $\phi$, and $\lambda$.
2. Define the Schmid factor $m$. Prove that the maximum theoretical value of $m$ is $0.50$, and find the angles $\phi$ and $\lambda$ at which this maximum occurs.
3. What is the value of $\tau_R$ when: (a) $\phi = 90^\circ$, and (b) $\lambda = 90^\circ$? Explain the physical meaning of these two extreme orientations.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Derivation of Schmid's law
The slip plane is inclined at angle $\phi$ to the tensile axis. The area of the inclined slip plane $A_s$ is: $$A_s = \frac{A_0}{\cos\phi}$$ The component of the axial tensile force $F$ resolved along the slip direction $\mathbf{s}$ (at angle $\lambda$ to $F$) is: $$F_s = F \cos\lambda$$ The resolved shear stress $\tau_R$ is the shear force divided by the slip plane area: $$\tau_R = \frac{F_s}{A_s} = \frac{F \cos\lambda}{A_0 / \cos\phi} = \left(\frac{F}{A_0}\right) \cos\phi \cos\lambda = \sigma \cos\phi \cos\lambda$$
Step 2: Schmid factor and maximum value
The Schmid factor is defined as: $$m = \cos\phi \cos\lambda$$ Because $\mathbf{s}$ lies in the slip plane ($\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{s} = 0$), the vectors form a right triangle in the plane defined by $\mathbf{n}$ and $\mathbf{s}$. Therefore: $$\phi + \lambda \ge 90^\circ$$ When the tensile axis lies in the plane containing $\mathbf{n}$ and $\mathbf{s}$, $\lambda = 90^\circ - \phi$: $$m = \cos\phi \cos(90^\circ - \phi) = \cos\phi \sin\phi = \frac{1}{2}\sin(2\phi)$$ The maximum of $\sin(2\phi)$ is $1$, occurring at: $$2\phi = 90^\circ \implies \phi = 45^\circ \implies \lambda = 90^\circ - 45^\circ = 45^\circ$$ $$m_{\text{max}} = \frac{1}{2} = 0.50$$
Step 3: Physical interpretation of extreme cases
(a) When $\phi = 90^\circ$: $\cos\phi = 0 \implies \tau_R = 0$. The tensile axis lies parallel to the slip plane. The applied normal force produces zero force component across or along the plane.
(b) When $\lambda = 90^\circ$: $\cos\lambda = 0 \implies \tau_R = 0$. The tensile axis is perpendicular to the slip direction. There is zero force component directed along the direction of dislocation glide.
In both cases, no shear stress drives the dislocation, so plastic slip cannot be initiated on that system regardless of how high $\sigma$ is.
Key Results:
Schmid's Law: $\tau_R = \sigma \cos\phi \cos\lambda = m \sigma$
Maximum Schmid factor: $m_{\text{max}} = 0.50$ at $\phi = \lambda = 45^\circ$
$\tau_R = 0$ when $\phi = 90^\circ$ or $\lambda = 90^\circ$ (zero resolved driving shear force).
Problem 05 Schmid's Law Medium

Critical Resolved Shear Stress (CRSS) and Single-Crystal Yielding

A single-crystal cylinder of pure zinc (HCP structure) with cross-sectional area $A_0 = 12.0\text{ mm}^2$ is oriented such that the normal to its basal slip plane $(0001)$ makes an angle $\phi = 62.0^\circ$ with the tensile loading axis. The slip direction $[11\bar{2}0]$ makes an angle $\lambda = 38.0^\circ$ with the tensile axis. The critical resolved shear stress for basal slip in zinc at room temperature is $\tau_{\text{CRSS}} = 0.18\text{ MPa}$.

1. Verify whether the given angles $\phi$ and $\lambda$ satisfy the geometric constraint $\cos\phi + \cos\lambda \le \dots$ (check whether $\phi + \lambda \ge 90^\circ$).
2. Calculate the Schmid factor $m$ for this slip system.
3. Calculate the macroscopic tensile stress $\sigma_y$ required to initiate plastic yielding.
4. Calculate the required tensile force $F_y$ applied by the testing machine.
5. If the crystal were reoriented such that $\phi = 85^\circ$ and $\lambda = 15^\circ$, calculate the new yield stress $\sigma_y'$.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Geometric constraint check
Since the slip direction lies within the slip plane: $$\phi + \lambda = 62.0^\circ + 38.0^\circ = 100.0^\circ \ge 90.0^\circ$$ This satisfies the required condition for valid 3D angles between the tensile axis, plane normal, and in-plane direction.
Step 2: Calculate Schmid factor $m$
$$m = \cos\phi \cos\lambda = \cos(62.0^\circ) \cos(38.0^\circ)$$ $$\cos(62.0^\circ) \approx 0.46947$$ $$\cos(38.0^\circ) \approx 0.78801$$ $$m = 0.46947 \times 0.78801 \approx 0.36995 \approx 0.370$$
Step 3: Macroscopic tensile yield stress $\sigma_y$
Yielding initiates when $\tau_R = \tau_{\text{CRSS}}$: $$\tau_{\text{CRSS}} = \sigma_y \times m \implies \sigma_y = \frac{\tau_{\text{CRSS}}}{m} = \frac{0.18\text{ MPa}}{0.36995} \approx 0.4865\text{ MPa}$$
Step 4: Applied tensile force $F_y$
$$F_y = \sigma_y A_0 = (0.4865\text{ N/mm}^2) \times (12.0\text{ mm}^2) \approx 5.839\text{ N} \approx 5.84\text{ N}$$
Step 5: Reoriented crystal yield stress
For $\phi = 85^\circ$ and $\lambda = 15^\circ$: $$\cos(85^\circ) \approx 0.08716, \quad \cos(15^\circ) \approx 0.96593$$ $$m' = 0.08716 \times 0.96593 \approx 0.08419$$ $$\sigma_y' = \frac{\tau_{\text{CRSS}}}{m'} = \frac{0.18\text{ MPa}}{0.08419} \approx 2.138\text{ MPa}$$ Reorienting the crystal so the basal plane is nearly parallel to the tensile axis increases the required yield stress by a factor of $2.138 / 0.4865 \approx 4.4\times$.
Key Results:
Schmid factor $m = 0.370$
Yield stress $\sigma_y = 0.487\text{ MPa}$
Yield force $F_y = 5.84\text{ N}$
Reoriented yield stress $\sigma_y' = 2.14\text{ MPa}$ ($4.4\times$ higher).
Problem 06 Schmid's Law Hard

Identifying Active Slip System in FCC Crystal

A single crystal of copper (FCC) is loaded in tension along the crystallographic axis $\mathbf{t} = [123]$. The critical resolved shear stress is $\tau_{\text{CRSS}} = 0.70\text{ MPa}$. Recall that the slip systems in FCC are $\{111\}\langle 1\bar{1}0\rangle$.

1. Using the vector dot product formula: $$\cos\phi = \frac{|\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{t}|}{\|\mathbf{n}\| \|\mathbf{t}\|}, \quad \cos\lambda = \frac{|\mathbf{s} \cdot \mathbf{t}|}{\|\mathbf{s}\| \|\mathbf{t}\|}$$ evaluate the Schmid factors for the three slip directions in the $(111)$ slip plane: $\mathbf{s}_1 = [1\bar{1}0]$, $\mathbf{s}_2 = [10\bar{1}]$, and $\mathbf{s}_3 = [01\bar{1}]$.
2. Repeat for the $(\bar{1}11)$ slip plane with directions: $\mathbf{s}_4 = [110]$, $\mathbf{s}_5 = [101]$, and $\mathbf{s}_6 = [0\bar{1}1]$.
3. Identify the primary active slip system with the highest Schmid factor $m_{\text{max}}$.
4. Calculate the macroscopic tensile yield strength $\sigma_y$ of the copper crystal along $[123]$.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Evaluate Schmid factors for $(111)$ slip plane
Tensile axis $\mathbf{t} = [123]$: $$\|\mathbf{t}\| = \sqrt{1^2 + 2^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{1 + 4 + 9} = \sqrt{14}$$ For plane $\mathbf{n}_1 = [111]$: $$\|\mathbf{n}_1\| = \sqrt{1^2 + 1^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{3}$$ $$\mathbf{n}_1 \cdot \mathbf{t} = 1(1) + 1(2) + 1(3) = 6 \implies \cos\phi = \frac{6}{\sqrt{3}\sqrt{14}} = \frac{6}{\sqrt{42}}$$ Now for each direction in $(111)$ (note $\|\mathbf{s}\| = \sqrt{2}$): - $\mathbf{s}_1 = [1\bar{1}0]$: $$\mathbf{s}_1 \cdot \mathbf{t} = 1(1) - 1(2) + 0(3) = -1 \implies |\mathbf{s}_1 \cdot \mathbf{t}| = 1$$ $$m_1 = \frac{6 \times 1}{\sqrt{42}\sqrt{2}\sqrt{14}} = \frac{6}{\sqrt{42}\sqrt{28}} = \frac{6}{\sqrt{1176}} = \frac{6}{14\sqrt{6}} = \frac{3}{7\sqrt{6}} \approx 0.1750$$ - $\mathbf{s}_2 = [10\bar{1}]$: $$\mathbf{s}_2 \cdot \mathbf{t} = 1(1) + 0 - 1(3) = -2 \implies |\mathbf{s}_2 \cdot \mathbf{t}| = 2$$ $$m_2 = \frac{6 \times 2}{14\sqrt{6}} = \frac{6}{7\sqrt{6}} \approx 0.3499$$ - $\mathbf{s}_3 = [01\bar{1}]$: $$\mathbf{s}_3 \cdot \mathbf{t} = 0(1) + 1(2) - 1(3) = -1 \implies |\mathbf{s}_3 \cdot \mathbf{t}| = 1$$ $$m_3 = \frac{6 \times 1}{14\sqrt{6}} \approx 0.1750$$
Step 2: Evaluate Schmid factors for $(\bar{1}11)$ slip plane
For plane $\mathbf{n}_2 = [\bar{1}11]$: $$\mathbf{n}_2 \cdot \mathbf{t} = -1(1) + 1(2) + 1(3) = 4 \implies \cos\phi = \frac{4}{\sqrt{42}}$$ - $\mathbf{s}_4 = [110]$: $$\mathbf{s}_4 \cdot \mathbf{t} = 1(1) + 1(2) + 0 = 3 \implies m_4 = \frac{4 \times 3}{14\sqrt{6}} = \frac{12}{14\sqrt{6}} \approx 0.3499$$ - $\mathbf{s}_5 = [101]$: $$\mathbf{s}_5 \cdot \mathbf{t} = 1(1) + 0 + 1(3) = 4 \implies m_5 = \frac{4 \times 4}{14\sqrt{6}} = \frac{16}{14\sqrt{6}} = \frac{8}{7\sqrt{6}} \approx 0.4666$$ - $\mathbf{s}_6 = [0\bar{1}1]$: $$\mathbf{s}_6 \cdot \mathbf{t} = 0 - 1(2) + 1(3) = 1 \implies m_6 = \frac{4 \times 1}{14\sqrt{6}} \approx 0.1166$$
Step 3: Primary active slip system
Comparing all values, the highest Schmid factor is: $$m_{\text{max}} = m_5 = \frac{8}{7\sqrt{6}} \approx 0.4666$$ This corresponds to the primary active slip system: $(\bar{1}11)[101]$.
Step 4: Macroscopic tensile yield stress
$$\sigma_y = \frac{\tau_{\text{CRSS}}}{m_{\text{max}}} = \frac{0.70\text{ MPa}}{0.4666} \approx 1.50\text{ MPa}$$
Key Results:
Highest Schmid factor $m_{\text{max}} = 0.467$
Primary slip system: $(\bar{1}11)[101]$
Yield strength $\sigma_y = 1.50\text{ MPa}$
Problem 07 Dislocation Reactions Easy

Dislocation Energy and Frank's Rule of Dissociation

The elastic strain energy per unit length $E_{\text{el}}$ of a dislocation line is proportional to the square of its Burgers vector magnitude: $$E_{\text{el}} = \alpha G b^2$$ where $\alpha \approx 0.5\text{ to }1.0$, $G$ is shear modulus, and $b = \|\mathbf{b}\|$. Frank's rule states that a dislocation reaction $\mathbf{b}_1 \to \mathbf{b}_2 + \mathbf{b}_3$ is energetically favorable if $b_1^2 > b_2^2 + b_3^2$.

In an FCC lattice with lattice parameter $a = 0.361\text{ nm}$ (copper, $G = 48\text{ GPa}$):
1. Calculate the elastic strain energy per meter of a full dislocation $\mathbf{b}_1 = \frac{a}{2}[110]$ (take $\alpha = 0.5$).
2. Consider the potential dissociation into two Shockley partials: $$\frac{a}{2}[110] \to \frac{a}{6}[21\bar{1}] + \frac{a}{6}[121]$$ Verify vector conservation ($\mathbf{b}_1 = \mathbf{b}_2 + \mathbf{b}_3$).
3. Apply Frank's rule to determine whether this dissociation is energetically favorable, and calculate the percentage energy reduction.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Dislocation energy of full dislocation $\mathbf{b}_1$
$$\|\mathbf{b}_1\|^2 = \left(\frac{a}{2}\right)^2 (1^2 + 1^2 + 0^2) = \frac{a^2}{4}(2) = \frac{a^2}{2}$$ For copper ($a = 0.361 \times 10^{-9}\text{ m}$, $G = 48 \times 10^9\text{ Pa}$): $$b_1^2 = \frac{(0.361 \times 10^{-9})^2}{2} = \frac{1.3032 \times 10^{-19}}{2} \approx 6.516 \times 10^{-20}\text{ m}^2$$ $$b_1 = \sqrt{6.516 \times 10^{-20}} \approx 0.2553\text{ nm}$$ The energy per unit length is: $$E_1 = 0.5 G b_1^2 = 0.5 \times (48 \times 10^9) \times (6.516 \times 10^{-20}) \approx 1.564 \times 10^{-9}\text{ J/m} = 1.564\text{ nJ/m}$$
Step 2: Vector conservation check
$$\mathbf{b}_2 + \mathbf{b}_3 = \frac{a}{6}[2, 1, -1] + \frac{a}{6}[1, 2, 1] = \frac{a}{6}[2+1, 1+2, -1+1] = \frac{a}{6}[3, 3, 0] = \frac{a}{2}[1, 1, 0] = \mathbf{b}_1$$ Vector addition is satisfied perfectly.
Step 3: Frank's energy criterion
Calculate $b_2^2$ and $b_3^2$: $$b_2^2 = \left(\frac{a}{6}\right)^2 (2^2 + 1^2 + (-1)^2) = \frac{a^2}{36}(4 + 1 + 1) = \frac{6a^2}{36} = \frac{a^2}{6}$$ $$b_3^2 = \left(\frac{a}{6}\right)^2 (1^2 + 2^2 + 1^2) = \frac{a^2}{36}(1 + 4 + 1) = \frac{6a^2}{36} = \frac{a^2}{6}$$ Sum of product energies: $$b_2^2 + b_3^2 = \frac{a^2}{6} + \frac{a^2}{6} = \frac{2a^2}{6} = \frac{a^2}{3}$$ Compare with $b_1^2$: $$b_1^2 = \frac{a^2}{2} = 0.50\,a^2$$ $$b_2^2 + b_3^2 = \frac{a^2}{3} \approx 0.333\,a^2$$ Since $b_1^2 > b_2^2 + b_3^2$ ($0.50 > 0.333$), the reaction is highly energetically favorable! $$\text{Percentage energy reduction} = \frac{0.50 - 0.333}{0.50} \times 100\% = \frac{1/6}{1/2} \times 100\% = 33.33\%$$ The dissociation cuts the dislocation elastic strain energy by exactly one-third ($33.3\%$).
Key Results:
Dislocation energy $E_1 \approx 1.56\text{ nJ/m}$
$b_1^2 = a^2/2$, whereas $b_2^2 + b_3^2 = a^2/3$
Dissociation is energetically favorable with a $33.3\%$ decrease in strain energy.
Problem 08 Dislocation Reactions Hard

Shockley Partials and Stacking Fault Equilibrium Width

When a full dislocation in an FCC crystal dissociates into two Shockley partials, a strip of stacking fault (with surface energy $\gamma_{\text{SF}}$) is created between them: The two partials repel each other with an elastic repulsive force per unit length: $$F_{\text{rep}} = \frac{G (\mathbf{b}_1 \cdot \mathbf{b}_2)}{2\pi d}$$ where $d$ is the separation distance (ribbon width), $G$ is the shear modulus, and $\mathbf{b}_1 = \frac{a}{6}[21\bar{1}], \mathbf{b}_2 = \frac{a}{6}[121]$. The stacking fault exerts an attractive surface tension force per unit length $F_{\text{att}} = \gamma_{\text{SF}}$ pulling them together.

1. Calculate the dot product $\mathbf{b}_1 \cdot \mathbf{b}_2$ in terms of lattice parameter $a$.
2. Equating $F_{\text{rep}} = F_{\text{att}}$, derive the formula for the equilibrium stacking fault width $d$.
3. For copper ($a = 0.361\text{ nm}$, $G = 48\text{ GPa}$, $\gamma_{\text{SF}} = 45\text{ mJ/m}^2$), calculate the equilibrium width $d_{\text{Cu}}$.
4. For aluminum ($a = 0.405\text{ nm}$, $G = 26\text{ GPa}$, $\gamma_{\text{SF}} = 160\text{ mJ/m}^2$), calculate $d_{\text{Al}}$.
5. Explain how the difference in ribbon width explains why aluminum cross-slips easily while copper and brass do not.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Calculate dot product $\mathbf{b}_1 \cdot \mathbf{b}_2$

Step 4: Stacking fault width for aluminum

A screw dislocation cannot cross-slip onto an intersecting $\{111\}$ plane while dissociated, because the extended stacking fault ribbon is strictly confined to its original slip plane. To cross-slip, the two partials must first be pinched together (constricted) back into a full dislocation against the repulsive force.
- In aluminum, $\gamma_{\text{SF}}$ is very high ($160\text{ mJ/m}^2$), so the ribbon width is only $0.35\text{ nm}$ ($\approx 1$ atom wide). The constriction energy is negligible, allowing aluminum to cross-slip effortlessly, bypass obstacles, and form well-defined cellular subgrain networks (Stage III dynamic recovery).

Lomer-Cottrell Lock (Dislocation Junction Formation)

In FCC metals undergoing multiaxial slip, two dislocations gliding on intersecting $\{111\}$ planes can meet and react to form a sessile (immobile) dislocation junction known as a Lomer-Cottrell lock. Consider the reaction between dislocation $\mathbf{b}_1 = \frac{a}{2}[01\bar{1}]$ gliding on the $(111)$ plane and dislocation $\mathbf{b}_2 = \frac{a}{2}[101]$ gliding on the $(1\bar{1}1)$ plane.

1. Determine the Burgers vector $\mathbf{b}_3 = \mathbf{b}_1 + \mathbf{b}_2$ of the resulting product dislocation.
2. Determine the line of intersection $\mathbf{u}$ of the two slip planes $(111)$ and $(1\bar{1}1)$ by computing their cross product.
3. Show that the product dislocation lies along the intersection line $\mathbf{u}$ and calculate its energy using Frank's rule. Is the reaction energetically favorable?
4. Identify the plane containing both $\mathbf{b}_3$ and $\mathbf{u}$. Explain why this dislocation is strictly sessile (unable to glide), and describe its role in Stage II work hardening.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Burgers vector of the product dislocation
$$\mathbf{b}_3 = \mathbf{b}_1 + \mathbf{b}_2 = \frac{a}{2}[0, 1, -1] + \frac{a}{2}[1, 0, 1] = \frac{a}{2}[0+1, 1+0, -1+1] = \frac{a}{2}[110]$$
Step 2: Line of intersection $\mathbf{u}$ of the two slip planes
$$\mathbf{n}_1 = [1, 1, 1], \quad \mathbf{n}_2 = [1, -1, 1]$$ $$\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{n}_1 \times \mathbf{n}_2 = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 & 1 \end{vmatrix} = \mathbf{i}(1 - (-1)) - \mathbf{j}(1 - 1) + \mathbf{k}(-1 - 1) = 2\mathbf{i} - 0\mathbf{j} - 2\mathbf{k} = [2, 0, -2]$$ Normalizing to the standard crystallographic direction: $$\mathbf{u} = [10\bar{1}]$$
Step 3: Frank's rule check
$$b_1^2 = \left(\frac{a}{2}\right)^2 (0 + 1 + 1) = \frac{2a^2}{4} = \frac{a^2}{2}$$ $$b_2^2 = \left(\frac{a}{2}\right)^2 (1 + 0 + 1) = \frac{2a^2}{4} = \frac{a^2}{2}$$ $$b_1^2 + b_2^2 = \frac{a^2}{2} + \frac{a^2}{2} = a^2$$ For the product $\mathbf{b}_3 = \frac{a}{2}[110]$: $$b_3^2 = \left(\frac{a}{2}\right)^2 (1 + 1 + 0) = \frac{2a^2}{4} = \frac{a^2}{2}$$ Comparing: $$b_3^2 = 0.50\,a^2 < b_1^2 + b_2^2 = 1.00\,a^2$$ The reaction produces a $50\%$ reduction in strain energy and is spontaneous and highly favorable!
Step 4: Why the Lomer-Cottrell lock is sessile
The plane containing both the dislocation line $\mathbf{u} = [10\bar{1}]$ and Burgers vector $\mathbf{b}_3 = \frac{a}{2}[110]$ has normal: $$\mathbf{n}_{\text{plane}} = \mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{b}_3 \propto [1, 0, -1] \times [1, 1, 0] = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ 1 & 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \end{vmatrix} = [1, -1, 1] \dots \text{with partial splitting on } (001)$$ Crucially, when partial dislocations form, the lock forms a triangular sessile stair-rod dislocation wedge on $\{001\}$ planes. In FCC crystals, $\{001\}$ is not an active close-packed slip plane at ordinary temperatures. The dislocation is pinned along $\mathbf{u}$, acting as an immovable barrier (lock) that blocks subsequent dislocations on both intersecting slip planes. Dislocation pile-ups form against the lock, leading to linear, rapid strain hardening (Stage II work hardening).
Key Results:

Frank's rule: $b_3^2 = a^2/2 < b_1^2 + b_2^2 = a^2$ ($50\%$ energy drop)

Grain Boundaries as Dislocation Obstacles: Physical Mechanisms

A polycrystalline metal consists of many individual grains (crystallites) separated by grain boundaries.

1. Explain the two primary crystallographic mechanisms by which a high-angle grain boundary impedes the passage of a gliding dislocation.
2. Can a dislocation glide smoothly from Grain A into Grain B across a high-angle boundary without interruption? Explain why or why not.
3. How does the presence of grain boundaries explain why polycrystalline metals are invariably stronger than single crystals of the same composition?
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Two primary barrier mechanisms of grain boundaries
1. Crystallographic Misorientation: Adjacent grains possess different spatial crystallographic orientations. Because dislocation slip is strictly confined to specific close-packed planes (e.g., $\{111\}$ in FCC), when a dislocation gliding on a slip plane in Grain A reaches the boundary, the corresponding slip plane in Grain B does not align with it. To continue, the dislocation would have to suddenly change its trajectory into a non-coplanar plane, requiring an enormous energy penalty.
2. Atomic Disordering at the Boundary: A high-angle grain boundary is an atomically disordered transition zone characterized by broken, stretched, and misaligned bonds. There are no well-defined lattice planes or periodic potentials within the boundary, disrupting the regular glide path of dislocations.
Step 2: Can dislocations glide smoothly across?
No. In almost all cases, a dislocation cannot cross a high-angle boundary directly. Instead, when a dislocation reaches the grain boundary, it is stopped and pinned. As more dislocations arrive on the same slip plane from behind, they form a dislocation pile-up. The collective repulsive interactions among piled-up dislocations create an intense stress concentration at the boundary. Only when this local tip stress reaches a critical threshold does it activate a new, independent dislocation source (e.g., a Frank-Read source) in the adjacent Grain B, transferring plastic slip across the boundary indirectly.
Step 3: Polycrystalline vs. single-crystal strength
In a single crystal, once a dislocation starts moving at the critical resolved shear stress ($\tau_{\text{CRSS}} \approx 1\text{ MPa}$), it can travel vast macroscopic distances across the entire crystal without encountering internal boundaries. In contrast, in a polycrystal, dislocation glide is halted every few micrometers (the grain diameter $d$). Hundreds of grain boundaries per millimeter force continuous dislocation pile-up and source activation, raising the macroscopic yield strength by an order of magnitude (e.g., pure Al single crystal yields at $\sim 1\text{ MPa}$, while polycrystalline Al yields at $25\text{--}40\text{ MPa}$).
Key Results:
Two mechanisms: (1) Slip plane misorientation across the boundary, and (2) Atomic disorder destroying periodic slip paths.
Dislocations do not cross directly; they pile up and activate new sources in neighboring grains via stress concentration.
Polycrystals are much stronger because internal boundaries halt dislocation mean free path every few microns.

Dislocation Pile-Up Model Derivation of Hall-Petch Relation

In the classic Eshelby-Frank-Nabarro pile-up model, a Frank-Read source at the center of a grain of diameter $d$ emits dislocations that glide along a slip plane of length $L = d/2$ and pile up against the grain boundary under an effective resolved shear stress $\tau_{\text{eff}} = \tau - \tau_0$. 1. Substitute $n$ into $\tau_{\text{tip}} = \tau_c$ and show that: $$\tau = \tau_0 + \sqrt{\frac{2 G b \tau_c}{k_1}}\,d^{-1/2}$$ 2. Relate macroscopic tensile stress $\sigma$ to resolved shear stress $\tau$ using the Taylor orientation factor $\bar{M}$ ($\sigma = \bar{M}\tau$), and write the explicit expression for the Hall-Petch slope $k_y$.
3. Calculate the number of dislocations $n$ in a pile-up for iron ($G = 80\text{ GPa}$, $b = 0.248\text{ nm}$, take $k_1 \approx 1.0$) in a grain of diameter $d = 30\text{ }\mu\text{m}$ when $\tau_{\text{eff}} = 50\text{ MPa}$.
4. Calculate the concentrated tip stress $\tau_{\text{tip}}$ under these conditions.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Derivation of the shear Hall-Petch relation
From $\tau_{\text{tip}} = n (\tau - \tau_0) = \tau_c$: Substitute $n = \frac{k_1 (\tau - \tau_0) d}{2 G b}$: $$\tau_{\text{tip}} = \left[\frac{k_1 (\tau - \tau_0) d}{2 G b}\right](\tau - \tau_0) = \frac{k_1 d}{2 G b} (\tau - \tau_0)^2 = \tau_c$$ Solving for $(\tau - \tau_0)^2$: $$(\tau - \tau_0)^2 = \frac{2 G b \tau_c}{k_1 d}$$ Taking the square root of both sides: $$\tau - \tau_0 = \sqrt{\frac{2 G b \tau_c}{k_1}}\,d^{-1/2} \implies \tau = \tau_0 + k_s d^{-1/2}$$ where $k_s = \sqrt{\frac{2 G b \tau_c}{k_1}}$ is the shear Hall-Petch slope.
Step 2: Macroscopic tensile relation and $k_y$
For a random polycrystal, macroscopic tensile stress $\sigma = \bar{M}\tau$ and $\sigma_0 = \bar{M}\tau_0$, where $\bar{M} \approx 3.06$ for FCC and BCC: $$\sigma = \bar{M}\tau = \bar{M}\tau_0 + \bar{M} k_s d^{-1/2} = \sigma_0 + k_y d^{-1/2}$$ The Hall-Petch slope is: $$k_y = \bar{M} \sqrt{\frac{2 G b \tau_c}{k_1}}$$ This proves mathematically why the yield strength is strictly proportional to $d^{-1/2}$.

Step 4: Concentrated tip stress $\tau_{\text{tip}}$
$$\tau_{\text{tip}} = n \tau_{\text{eff}} \approx 37.8 \times 50\text{ MPa} \approx 1,890\text{ MPa} \approx 1.89\text{ GPa}$$ Notice the extraordinary magnitude of the stress concentration: an applied effective shear stress of just $50\text{ MPa}$ produces a localized tip stress of nearly $1.9\text{ GPa}$ ($n\times$ multiplication), easily sufficient to nucleate dislocations across the boundary in the neighboring grain.
Key Results:
Derived: $\tau = \tau_0 + \sqrt{\frac{2Gb\tau_c}{k_1}} d^{-1/2}$, so $k_y = \bar{M}\sqrt{\frac{2Gb\tau_c}{k_1}}$

Step 1: Physical explanation of simultaneous strengthening and toughening
Yield strength increases with smaller grain size because shorter slip path lengths limit the number of dislocations in a pile-up ($n \propto d$), requiring a higher applied stress to generate the critical tip stress for slip propagation ($k_y d^{-1/2}$).
Concurrently, fracture toughness increases because: 1. Cleavage microcracks nucleate from dislocation pile-ups or fractured grain boundary carbide particles. 3. Identify the dominant plastic deformation mechanisms that replace dislocation glide in nanocrystalline metals with $d < 10\text{ nm}$.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Dislocation pile-up limit
The classical Hall-Petch mechanism relies on dislocation pile-up to generate stress concentrations. A pile-up requires at least two dislocations on the same slip plane. The repulsive distance between two identical dislocations under yield stress is typically: $$x_0 \approx \frac{G b}{2\pi (1-\nu) \tau_y}$$ For typical metals, $x_0 \approx 10\text{ to }20\text{ nm}$. If the grain diameter $d < x_0$, a single grain is simply too small to contain two dislocations simultaneously. Dislocation pile-ups become physically impossible, eliminating the foundation of the Hall-Petch model.
Step 2: Grain boundary atom volume fraction $f_{\text{GB}}$
For a spherical grain of diameter $d$ surrounded by a boundary shell of thickness $\delta / 2$ (so total boundary thickness is $\delta = 1.0\text{ nm}$): The grain interior radius is $r_{\text{core}} = (d - \delta)/2 = (10 - 1)/2 = 4.5\text{ nm}$. At $d = 5\text{ nm}$, $f_{\text{GB}}$ exceeds $50\%$!
Step 3: Dominant deformation mechanisms in nanocrystalline metals
When $d < 10\text{ nm}$: 1. Grain Boundary Sliding (GBS): Individual grains rotate and slide past one another along the weak, disordered boundary network via atomic shuffles. 2. Coble Creep at Room Temperature: Rapid room-temperature vacancy diffusion along the immense network of grain boundaries ($\dot{\varepsilon} \propto D_{\text{gb}} / d^3$) produces significant diffusion-mediated plastic flow under modest stresses. 3. Dislocation Nucleation and Absorption: Full or partial dislocations nucleate directly at a grain boundary, sweep across the tiny grain without encountering obstacles, and are instantly absorbed into the opposite grain boundary without multiplying. Because grain boundary sliding and diffusion creep soften the material as $d$ decreases ($d\sigma/dd > 0$), the yield strength declines below $d_c \approx 10\text{--}15\text{ nm}$.

Pile-ups are impossible when $d < x_0 \approx 10\text{--}15\text{ nm}$.

Step 1: Mechanism of the upper-to-lower yield drop
In annealed/hot-rolled mild steel, all pre-existing dislocations are rigidly locked by Cottrell atmospheres of carbon and nitrogen atoms. 1. To initiate plastic flow, the applied stress must reach the high upper yield point ($\sigma_{\text{UYP}}$) to forcibly rip dislocations away from their solute atmospheres or nucleate new dislocations from grain boundaries. 2. This tiny plastic deformation breaks all dislocations away from their Cottrell atmospheres throughout the entire sheet without causing significant work hardening or thinning. When the customer later stamps the sheet, the material deforms with smooth, continuous yielding without any yield drop, stretcher-strain marks, or Lüders bands.

Yield drop occurs because freeing locked dislocations floods the lattice with mobile carriers, dropping required glide stress from $\sigma_{\text{UYP}}$ to $\sigma_{\text{LYP}}$.

Step 1: Mechanism of static strain aging
During the initial pre-straining, dislocations break free from their Cottrell atmospheres and multiply. Carbon and nitrogen interstitial solute atoms possess significant diffusion rates even near room temperature (activation energy for interstitial migration $\sim 0.8\text{ eV}$). Step 2: Dynamic Strain Aging (Portevin-Le Chatelier Effect)

Step 3: Dislocation velocity vs. Solute diffusion velocity
DSA arises from a dynamic synchronization between gliding dislocations and diffusing solute atoms: 1. When a dislocation is momentarily arrested at a forest obstacle (waiting time $t_w$), fast-diffusing carbon atoms catch up and form a solute cloud around it. 2. The applied stress rises until it reaches the breakaway threshold, abruptly tearing the dislocation free. 3. The dislocation surges forward at high velocity $v_d \gg v_s$ (causing an instantaneous drop in load). 4. Upon striking the next obstacle, the dislocation arrests again, and solute atoms catch up once more. This cyclic pinning and breakaway leads to a negative strain rate sensitivity ($d\sigma/d\dot{\varepsilon} < 0$), triggering plastic instability and propagating deformation bands.

The continuous pinning of dislocations by carbon/nitrogen minimizes mobile dislocation activity, raising hardness and tensile strength while causing a precipitous drop in ductility and impact energy. This embrittlement at elevated working temperatures is called blue brittleness.

Static aging: Carbon re-diffuses to unlocked dislocations during holding, restoring the yield drop.
PLC effect (Dynamic Strain Aging): Solute diffusion velocity matches dislocation waiting time, causing cyclic pinning/unpinning serrations.
Blue brittleness ($200\text{--}300^\circ\text{C}$): Intense dynamic strain aging causes high strength but severe embrittlement.

Upon cutting, the primary dislocation acquires a jog segment vector equal to $\mathbf{b}_f$: $$\mathbf{j} = \mathbf{b}_f = [100]$$ The jog segment has line direction $\mathbf{u}_{\text{jog}} = [100]$ and Burgers vector $\mathbf{b}_1 = [001]$. Because $\mathbf{u}_{\text{jog}} \cdot \mathbf{b}_1 = [100] \cdot [001] = 0$, the jog segment is pure edge character! Its glide plane is normal to $\mathbf{u}_{\text{jog}} \times \mathbf{b}_1 = [100] \times [001] = [0, -1, 0] = (010)$. However, forward motion of the screw dislocation requires the jog to move along $[001]$, perpendicular to its glide plane. Thus, it can only move by climb!
Step 3: Energy required to create a vacancy
Moving forward by distance $b$ creates one point defect (a vacancy). The energy penalty equals the vacancy formation energy: $$\Delta E = E_v^f = 1.0\text{ eV} = 1.0 \times 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J} = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J}$$
Step 4: Threshold shear stress to drag the jog
If the spacing between jogs along the dislocation line is $l_{\text{jog}} \approx 100\,b$, the mechanical work done by the applied stress $\tau$ over jump distance $b$ on the segment of length $l_{\text{jog}}$ is: $$W_{\text{mech}} = \tau b \times l_{\text{jog}} b = \tau b^2 l_{\text{jog}}$$ Equating mechanical work to vacancy formation energy: $$\tau_{\text{jog}} b^2 l_{\text{jog}} = E_v^f \implies \tau_{\text{jog}} = \frac{E_v^f}{b^2 l_{\text{jog}}}$$ For $b = 0.25 \times 10^{-9}\text{ m}$ and $l_{\text{jog}} = 100\,b = 25 \times 10^{-9}\text{ m}$: $$\tau_{\text{jog}} = \frac{1.602 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J}}{(0.25 \times 10^{-9}\text{ m})^2 \times (25 \times 10^{-9}\text{ m})} = \frac{1.602 \times 10^{-19}}{1.5625 \times 10^{-27}} \approx 1.025 \times 10^8\text{ Pa} \approx 102.5\text{ MPa}$$ This immense friction force required to drag non-conservative jogs is one of the most powerful physical contributions to work hardening in metals.
Key Results:
Kinks glide conservatively with zero activation energy; jogs on screw dislocations must climb non-conservatively.

Mechanism: Cross-slip $\to$ glide by distance $h$ $\to$ second cross-slip $\to$ edge segments pin and act as a new Frank-Read source.

Three Stages of Single-Crystal Work Hardening

1. Define Stage I (Easy Glide): explain why the work hardening rate $\theta_I = d\tau/d\gamma$ is extremely low ($\theta_I \approx 10^{-4} G$), and state the condition that ends Stage I.
2. Define Stage II (Linear Hardening): explain why the work hardening rate jumps to a high, universal value $\theta_{II} \approx G/300$ that is virtually independent of temperature and strain rate. What microstructural obstacles form?
3. Define Stage III (Dynamic Recovery): explain why the curve bends over ($d\tau/d\gamma$ decreases) and why the onset stress $\tau_{III}$ is strongly sensitive to temperature and stacking fault energy.
4. Why do polycrystalline metals rarely show Stage I easy glide?
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Stage I (Easy Glide)
- Microstructure: Dislocation glide occurs exclusively on the primary slip system with the highest Schmid factor. Dislocations glide over macroscopic distances and exit the crystal at free surfaces without intersecting other dislocation systems. - Hardening rate: $\theta_I = d\tau/d\gamma \approx 10^{-4} G$ (very flat). - End of Stage I: As the crystal deforms, lattice rotation gradually increases the Schmid factor on a secondary slip system until the critical resolved shear stress is reached on that system, activating conjugate slip.
Step 2: Stage II (Linear Rapid Hardening)
- Microstructure: Multiple intersecting slip systems are now active simultaneously. Gliding primary dislocations collide with secondary forest dislocations, forming sessile junctions (such as Lomer-Cottrell locks) and dislocation pile-ups. - Hardening rate: The hardening rate jumps dramatically to $\theta_{II} = d\tau/d\gamma \approx G/300$. - Temperature independence: Because forming Lomer-Cottrell locks and cutting forest trees are governed by long-range elastic interactions ($G b$), $\theta_{II}$ is athermal and virtually identical across all FCC metals.
Step 3: Stage III (Dynamic Recovery)
- Microstructure: At high stresses, the local stress concentration at dislocation pile-ups becomes large enough to force screw dislocations to constrict their partials and cross-slip out of their blocked primary planes. - Hardening rate: Cross-slipping allows dislocations of opposite sign to meet and annihilate or rearrange into low-energy cellular walls. This continuous annihilation during deformation is dynamic recovery, causing the hardening rate to decrease parabolically. - Sensitivity: Because constriction requires thermal activation and depends on stacking fault width ($d \propto 1/\gamma_{\text{SF}}$), $\tau_{III}$ decreases with increasing temperature and is much lower in high-SFE metals (Al) than in low-SFE metals (brass).
Step 4: Polycrystals and Stage I
In a polycrystal, individual grains are constrained by their neighbors. To maintain grain boundary compatibility without opening voids, the Taylor criterion requires each grain to activate at least 5 independent slip systems immediately upon yielding. Because multiple slip operates from the very start, polycrystals bypass Stage I entirely and enter directly into high Stage II/III hardening.
Key Results:
Stage I: Single slip, easy glide, $\theta_I \approx 10^{-4} G$.
Stage II: Multiple slip, Lomer-Cottrell locks, universal linear rate $\theta_{II} \approx G/300$.
Stage III: Dynamic recovery via cross-slip, temperature-dependent parabolic softening.
Polycrystals bypass Stage I because multi-slip is enforced from the onset of yield.

Orowan Dislocation Looping Bypass Mechanism

When precipitates coarsen beyond a critical radius $r_c$, or when particles are incoherent (such as equilibrium $\theta$ in Al-Cu or oxide dispersoids in ODS alloys), dislocations cannot shear them. Instead, the dislocation bows between adjacent particles and bypasses them by the Orowan looping mechanism, leaving behind a closed dislocation loop around each particle. The Orowan shear stress $\tau_{\text{Orowan}}$ is: $$\tau_{\text{Orowan}} = \frac{G b}{2\pi \sqrt{1-\nu}} \frac{1}{\lambda - 2r} \ln\left(\frac{2r}{r_0}\right) \approx \frac{G b}{L_{\text{eff}}}$$ where $L_{\text{eff}} = \lambda - 2r$ is the clear interparticle spacing. For spherical particles of radius $r$ and volume fraction $f$, the planar center-to-center particle spacing is $\lambda = r \sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{3f}} \approx 1.45\,\frac{r}{\sqrt{f}}$.

3. If excessive overaging coarsens the particles to $r_2 = 60.0\text{ nm}$ at the same volume fraction $f = 0.04$, calculate the new Orowan stress $\tau_{\text{Orowan}}'$.
4. Show that Orowan strengthening scales inversely with particle radius ($\tau_{\text{Orowan}} \propto 1/r$).

The overall yield strength of a precipitation-hardened alloy is governed by the competition between dislocation particle cutting (active at small radii) and Orowan dislocation looping (active at large radii): $$\tau_{\text{cutting}} = C_1 \sqrt{r}$$ $$\tau_{\text{looping}} = \frac{C_2}{r}$$ Peak aging (maximum yield strength $\tau_{\text{peak}}$) occurs at the critical transition radius $r_c$ where the two curves intersect: $\tau_{\text{cutting}}(r_c) = \tau_{\text{looping}}(r_c)$.

(b) Dislocation rearrangement (polygonization).
2. Why is vacancy diffusion essential for the recovery of edge dislocations, while screw dislocations can recover by glide alone?
3. Two parallel edge dislocations of opposite sign ($\mathbf{b}$ and $-\mathbf{b}$) are gliding on slip planes separated by a vertical distance $h = 4.0\text{ nm}$. Under thermal activation at $T = 500\text{ K}$, vacancies diffuse to the extra half-planes. Describe their interaction and state the final result of their mutual climb.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Primary mechanisms during recovery
(a) Dislocation Annihilation: Dislocations of opposite sign ($\mathbf{b}$ and $-\mathbf{b}$) gliding on the same slip plane attract each other, meet, and annihilate completely, eliminating their elastic strain fields.
(b) Polygonization (Subgrain Formation): When a metal is plastically bent, excess edge dislocations of the same sign accumulate randomly. At recovery temperatures, these edge dislocations climb out of their random slip planes and align themselves into vertical walls. This transforms a continuous crystal curvature into flat, polygon-like crystal blocks separated by planar low-angle tilt subgrain boundaries.
Step 2: Role of vacancy diffusion in climb
- Screw dislocations: Screw dislocations have their Burgers vector parallel to their line. They can cross-slip freely from one intersecting slip plane to another by glide alone, without requiring atomic diffusion.
- Edge dislocations: The Burgers vector of an edge dislocation is perpendicular to its line. It cannot change slip planes by glide. Moving out of its slip plane requires dislocation climb, which physically requires adding or removing atoms at the edge of the extra half-plane. This process requires the absorption or emission of vacancies, meaning recovery of edge dislocations is strictly rate-controlled by the activation energy of vacancy lattice self-diffusion ($Q_d$).
Step 3: Interaction of opposite-sign edge dislocations separated by $h = 4.0\text{ nm}$
The two opposite edge dislocations exert a mutual elastic attractive force on each other: - Horizontal glide brings them to a vertical alignment at $x = h = 4.0\text{ nm}$ (a metastable $45^\circ$ configuration). - At recovery temperatures, vacancy diffusion enables climb: the extra half-plane of the positive dislocation emits vacancies (shrinking downward), while the negative dislocation absorbs vacancies (growing upward). - As they climb toward each other across distance $h = 4.0\text{ nm}$, they meet and completely annihilate, destroying two lattice defects and releasing their combined elastic strain energy ($\Delta E \approx G b^2$).
Key Results:
Annihilation destroys opposite dislocations; polygonization aligns like dislocations into vertical low-angle tilt walls.
Edge dislocation climb requires atomic diffusion (vacancy migration), while screw dislocations recover via cross-slip.
Opposite edge dipoles separated by $4\text{ nm}$ climb toward each other and mutually annihilate.

Recrystallization Temperature $T_R$ and Influencing Factors

The recrystallization temperature ($T_R$) is defined as the temperature at which a heavily cold-worked metal complete recrystallizes into strain-free grains within precisely 1 hour. As an empirical rule of thumb: $$T_R \approx (0.3\text{ to }0.5)\,T_m \quad (\text{in Kelvin})$$

2. Explain the effect of the following parameters on $T_R$: (a) Increasing the percentage of prior cold work (%CW from $10\%$ to $80\%$).
(b) Alloy purity (zone-refined high-purity metal vs commercial-purity alloy).
(c) Annealing heating time (holding for 10 hours instead of 1 hour).

Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Calculate $T_R$ ranges in Kelvin and Celsius
(a) Lead: $T_m = 327 + 273.15 = 600.15\text{ K}$. $$T_R \approx (0.3 \times 600.15) \text{ to } (0.5 \times 600.15) = 180\text{ K to } 300\text{ K} \implies -93^\circ\text{C to } +27^\circ\text{C}$$ For high-purity lead, $T_R \approx 0^\circ\text{C}$. Lead recrystallizes spontaneously at room temperature; rolling lead at room temperature is technically hot working, so work hardening cannot accumulate!
(b) Copper: $T_m = 1085 + 273 = 1358\text{ K}$. $$T_R \approx (0.3 \times 1358) \text{ to } (0.4 \times 1358) = 407\text{ K to } 543\text{ K} \implies 134^\circ\text{C to } 270^\circ\text{C}$$
(c) Tungsten: $T_m = 3422 + 273 = 3695\text{ K}$. $$T_R \approx (0.33 \times 3695) \text{ to } (0.45 \times 3695) = 1219\text{ K to } 1663\text{ K} \implies 946^\circ\text{C to } 1390^\circ\text{C}$$ Working tungsten at $800^\circ\text{C}$ is still cold working ($T/T_m \approx 0.29 < T_R$).
Step 2: Influencing parameters
(a) Increasing %CW: Lowers $T_R$. Greater cold work stores higher dislocation density, providing a larger thermodynamic driving force ($\Delta G = \rho \frac{1}{2}Gb^2$) for nucleation of new grains.
(b) Alloy purity: Impurities dramatically raise $T_R$. Even $0.01\text{ wt}\%$ solute atoms segregate to moving high-angle grain boundaries, exerting a powerful solute drag force that drastically retards boundary mobility.
(c) Annealing time: Longer time lowers $T_R$. Recrystallization is a thermally activated kinetic process; extending the holding time allows the same transformation fraction to be achieved at lower temperatures.
Step 3: Critical cold work
Below a critical strain ($\%CW_{\text{crit}} \approx 2\text{--}5\%$), the stored dislocation energy is insufficient to nucleate any new strain-free grains, and recrystallization cannot occur at any temperature. Heating a metal deformed below $\%CW_{\text{crit}}$ produces only recovery or abnormal, monstrous grain growth.

Copper: $T_R \approx 135\text{--}270^\circ\text{C}$; Tungsten: $T_R \approx 950\text{--}1400^\circ\text{C}$.
$T_R$ decreases with higher %CW and longer time; increases sharply with impurities (solute drag).
Minimum critical deformation: $\%CW_{\text{crit}} \approx 2\text{--}5\%$.

Recrystallization Kinetics: Johnson-Mehl-Avrami-Kolmogorov (JMAK) Equation

The fraction of recrystallized material $X(t)$ as a function of isothermal annealing time $t$ follows the classical Johnson-Mehl-Avrami-Kolmogorov (JMAK) equation: $$X(t) = 1 - \exp\left(-k t^n\right)$$ where $k$ is a temperature-dependent rate constant and $n$ is the Avrami exponent.

In an isothermal annealing experiment on cold-rolled brass sheet at $350^\circ\text{C}$: - The fraction recrystallized is $X_1 = 0.20$ ($20\%$) after $t_1 = 10.0\text{ minutes}$. - The fraction recrystallized is $X_2 = 0.80$ ($80\%$) after $t_2 = 30.0\text{ minutes}$.
1. Linearize the JMAK equation into the form $\ln\left(\ln\frac{1}{1 - X}\right) = \ln k + n \ln t$.
2. Calculate the Avrami exponent $n$ and the rate constant $k$ (in $\text{min}^{-n}$).
3. Calculate the time $t_{0.50}$ required to reach $50\%$ recrystallization ($X = 0.50$).
4. Calculate the time $t_{0.99}$ required for virtually complete ($99\%$) recrystallization.
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Linearization of JMAK equation
$$1 - X = \exp\left(-k t^n\right)$$ Taking the reciprocal: $$\frac{1}{1 - X} = \exp\left(k t^n\right)$$ Taking the natural logarithm: $$\ln\left(\frac{1}{1 - X}\right) = k t^n$$ Taking the natural logarithm a second time: $$\ln\left[\ln\left(\frac{1}{1 - X}\right)\right] = \ln k + n \ln t$$
Step 2: Calculate Avrami exponent $n$ and rate constant $k$
- For $X_1 = 0.20$ at $t_1 = 10.0\text{ min}$: $$\ln\left(\frac{1}{1 - 0.20}\right) = \ln\left(\frac{1}{0.80}\right) = \ln(1.25) \approx 0.22314$$ $$Y_1 = \ln(0.22314) \approx -1.49994$$ $$\ln(t_1) = \ln(10.0) \approx 2.30259$$
- For $X_2 = 0.80$ at $t_2 = 30.0\text{ min}$: $$\ln\left(\frac{1}{1 - 0.80}\right) = \ln(5.0) \approx 1.60944$$ $$Y_2 = \ln(1.60944) \approx +0.47589$$ $$\ln(t_2) = \ln(30.0) \approx 3.40120$$
Subtracting the two equations: $$n = \frac{Y_2 - Y_1}{\ln(t_2) - \ln(t_1)} = \frac{0.47589 - (-1.49994)}{3.40120 - 2.30259} = \frac{1.97583}{1.09861} \approx 1.7985 \approx 1.80$$ Now solve for $k$: $$\ln k = Y_1 - n \ln(t_1) = -1.49994 - (1.7985 \times 2.30259) = -1.49994 - 4.1412 \approx -5.6411$$ $$k = \exp(-5.6411) \approx 3.549 \times 10^{-3}\text{ min}^{-1.80}$$
Step 3: Time for $50\%$ recrystallization ($t_{0.50}$)
$$\ln\left(\frac{1}{1 - 0.50}\right) = \ln(2.0) \approx 0.69315$$ $$k t_{0.50}^n = 0.69315 \implies t_{0.50}^n = \frac{0.69315}{3.549 \times 10^{-3}} \approx 195.31$$ $$t_{0.50} = (195.31)^{1/1.7985} = (195.31)^{0.5560} \approx 18.78\text{ minutes}$$
Step 4: Time for $99\%$ recrystallization ($t_{0.99}$)
$$\ln\left(\frac{1}{1 - 0.99}\right) = \ln(100) \approx 4.60517$$ $$t_{0.99}^n = \frac{4.60517}{3.549 \times 10^{-3}} \approx 1297.6$$ $$t_{0.99} = (1297.6)^{0.5560} \approx 54.04\text{ minutes}$$ Full recrystallization is accomplished in approximately $54\text{ minutes}$.
Key Results:
Avrami exponent $n = 1.80$
Rate constant $k = 3.55 \times 10^{-3}\text{ min}^{-1.80}$
$50\%$ recrystallization time: $t_{0.50} = 18.8\text{ min}$
$99\%$ complete recrystallization time: $t_{0.99} = 54.0\text{ min}$

Normal Grain Growth Kinetics and Temperature Dependence

The average grain diameter $d$ follows the parabolic growth law: $$d^n - d_0^n = K t$$ where $d_0$ is the initial grain size at $t = 0$, $n \approx 2$ (ideal theoretical value), and the rate constant is thermally activated: $$K = K_0 \exp\left(-\frac{Q_{\text{gg}}}{R T}\right)$$
An annealed brass specimen with initial grain diameter $d_0 = 10.0\text{ }\mu\text{m}$ is held at $600^\circ\text{C}$ ($873\text{ K}$). Assuming ideal parabolic kinetics ($n = 2$): - After $t_1 = 15.0\text{ minutes}$, the average grain diameter grows to $d_1 = 28.0\text{ }\mu\text{m}$.
1. Calculate the rate constant $K$ at $600^\circ\text{C}$ (in $\mu\text{m}^2/\text{min}$ and $\text{m}^2/\text{s}$).
2. Predict the grain size $d_2$ after $t_2 = 120.0\text{ minutes}$ (2 hours) at $600^\circ\text{C}$.
3. The activation energy for grain growth in brass is $Q_{\text{gg}} = 145\text{ kJ/mol}$. Calculate the rate constant $K'$ and the resulting grain size after 2 hours at $700^\circ\text{C}$ ($973\text{ K}$).
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Calculate rate constant $K$ at $600^\circ\text{C}$
From $d^2 - d_0^2 = K t$: $$d_0^2 = (10.0\text{ }\mu\text{m})^2 = 100.0\text{ }\mu\text{m}^2$$ $$d_1^2 = (28.0\text{ }\mu\text{m})^2 = 784.0\text{ }\mu\text{m}^2$$ $$K = \frac{d_1^2 - d_0^2}{t_1} = \frac{784.0 - 100.0}{15.0\text{ min}} = \frac{684.0}{15.0} = 45.60\text{ }\mu\text{m}^2/\text{min}$$ Convert to $\text{m}^2/\text{s}$: $$1\text{ }\mu\text{m}^2 = 10^{-12}\text{ m}^2, \quad 1\text{ min} = 60\text{ s}$$ $$K = \frac{45.60 \times 10^{-12}\text{ m}^2}{60\text{ s}} = 7.60 \times 10^{-13}\text{ m}^2/\text{s}$$
Step 2: Grain size after $120\text{ min}$ at $600^\circ\text{C}$
$$d_2^2 = d_0^2 + K t_2 = 100.0 + (45.60 \times 120.0) = 100.0 + 5472.0 = 5572.0\text{ }\mu\text{m}^2$$ $$d_2 = \sqrt{5572.0} \approx 74.65\text{ }\mu\text{m}$$
Step 3: Grain growth at $700^\circ\text{C}$ ($973\text{ K}$)
Using the Arrhenius relation: $$\frac{K(973)}{K(873)} = \exp\left[-\frac{Q_{\text{gg}}}{R} \left(\frac{1}{973} - \frac{1}{873}\right)\right]$$ $$\frac{1}{973} - \frac{1}{873} = 0.0010277 - 0.0011455 = -0.00011773\text{ K}^{-1}$$ $$-\frac{Q_{\text{gg}}}{R} = -\frac{145,000\text{ J/mol}}{8.314\text{ J}/(\text{mol}\cdot\text{K})} \approx -17,440.46\text{ K}$$ $$\text{Exponent} = (-17,440.46) \times (-0.00011773) \approx +2.0533$$ $$\frac{K(700^\circ\text{C})}{K(600^\circ\text{C})} = \exp(2.0533) \approx 7.7937$$ $$K(700^\circ\text{C}) = 45.60 \times 7.7937 \approx 355.39\text{ }\mu\text{m}^2/\text{min}$$ After $t = 120.0\text{ min}$ at $700^\circ\text{C}$: $$d^2 = 100.0 + (355.39 \times 120.0) = 100.0 + 42,647 = 42,747\text{ }\mu\text{m}^2$$ $$d = \sqrt{42,747} \approx 206.75\text{ }\mu\text{m}$$ Increasing the temperature by $100^\circ\text{C}$ increases the rate constant by nearly $8\times$, coarsening the grain diameter to nearly $207\text{ }\mu\text{m}$.
Key Results:
At $600^\circ\text{C}$: $K = 45.6\text{ }\mu\text{m}^2/\text{min}$ ($7.60 \times 10^{-13}\text{ m}^2/\text{s}$)
Grain size after 2 h at $600^\circ\text{C}$: $d = 74.7\text{ }\mu\text{m}$
At $700^\circ\text{C}$: $K' = 355.4\text{ }\mu\text{m}^2/\text{min} \implies d = 206.8\text{ }\mu\text{m}$ (massive coarsening).

Abnormal Grain Growth (Secondary Recrystallization) and Goss Texture

In normal grain growth, all grains grow uniformly with a steady-state monomodal grain size distribution. Under specific conditions, normal grain growth is completely pinned by fine precipitates (Zener pinning, $d_Z = 4r/3f$). Upon higher temperature annealing, a few select grains suddenly break free from pinning and consume the entire fine-grained matrix, growing to massive millimeter or centimeter dimensions. This is abnormal grain growth (secondary recrystallization).

1. Contrast normal grain growth and secondary recrystallization regarding driving force, grain size distribution, and kinetics.
2. In grain-oriented electrical steels (Fe-3%Si used in transformer cores), secondary recrystallization is deliberately engineered to produce the Goss texture $\{110\}\langle 001\rangle$. Why is the Goss texture of vital technological importance for electrical transformers?
3. How are manganese sulfide ($\text{MnS}$) or aluminum nitride ($\text{AlN}$) inhibitor particles used during hot and cold processing to trigger secondary recrystallization in silicon steel?
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Normal grain growth vs. Secondary recrystallization
- Normal Grain Growth: Driven by gradual reduction in total grain boundary curvature; grain size distribution remains monomodal and self-similar; kinetics follow continuous parabolic growth ($d \propto t^{1/2}$). - Secondary Recrystallization: Initiates only when normal grain growth is heavily inhibited by second-phase particles (e.g., $\text{MnS}$) or strong texture pinning. When inhibitor particles begin to dissolve or coarsen locally, a tiny fraction of grains with special high-mobility boundaries (often Goss orientation $\{110\}\langle 001\rangle$) break free. Driven by the immense boundary surface energy of the surrounding unrecrystallized fine matrix, these grains grow explosively, creating an extreme bimodal grain size distribution until they consume the entire volume.
Step 2: Technological importance of Goss texture $\{110\}\langle 001\rangle$
BCC iron is magnetically anisotropic: the body edge direction $\langle 100\rangle$ is the easy magnetization axis (requiring minimum magnetic field to achieve magnetic saturation and exhibiting minimal magnetic hysteresis loss). - In electrical transformer cores, alternating AC magnetic flux reverses 50 or 60 times per second. - The Goss texture aligns the $\{110\}$ plane parallel to the sheet surface and the easy $\langle 001\rangle$ magnetic axis strictly parallel to the rolling direction. - Stamping transformer laminations along this rolling direction aligns magnetic flux directly with the easy axis, cutting eddy current and hysteresis core losses by up to $70\%$, saving billions of kilowatt-hours of electrical energy worldwide annually.
Step 3: Role of $\text{MnS}$ / $\text{AlN}$ grain growth inhibitors
1. During hot slab reheating at $1400^\circ\text{C}$, $\text{MnS}$ dissolves completely. 2. Controlled hot and cold rolling with intermediate annealing precipitates an ultra-fine, dense dispersion of $\text{MnS}$ or $\text{AlN}$ nanoparticles ($r \approx 10\text{--}20\text{ nm}$). 3. These particles exert strong Zener drag ($F_Z \propto f/r$), completely freezing normal grain growth during primary recrystallization at $800^\circ\text{C}$ and maintaining an ultra-fine matrix ($d \approx 15\text{ }\mu\text{m}$). 4. During final high-temperature box annealing at $1100^\circ\text{C}\text{--}1200^\circ\text{C}$ in pure hydrogen, the inhibitor particles coarsen or dissolve, releasing only the strategically oriented Goss grains to consume the entire matrix, producing gigantic grains ($10\text{--}30\text{ mm}$ across) with $> 95\%$ perfect Goss alignment.
Key Results:
Secondary recrystallization is triggered when Zener pinning halts normal growth, enabling explosive growth of select grains.
Produces $\{110\}\langle 001\rangle$ Goss texture, aligning the easy magnetic axis $\langle 100\rangle$ with the transformer core flux, cutting core energy losses by $70\%$.
$\text{MnS}$/$\text{AlN}$ inhibitors pin normal grain growth until high-temperature annealing triggers abnormal Goss expansion.
Problem 50 Strengthening Superposition Hard

Comprehensive Strengthening Mechanism Superposition Design

Modern engineering alloys achieve superior yield strength by simultaneously combining all four fundamental strengthening mechanisms: 1. Lattice friction stress ($\sigma_0$) 2. Solid solution strengthening ($\Delta\sigma_{\text{ss}}$) 3. Grain boundary refinement ($\Delta\sigma_{\text{gb}} = k_y d^{-1/2}$) 4. Dislocation strain hardening ($\Delta\sigma_d = \bar{M}\alpha G b \sqrt{\rho_d}$) 5. Precipitation hardening ($\Delta\sigma_p$)
When combining mechanisms of differing physical nature, the total yield strength $\sigma_y$ is determined by the linear/Pythagorean superposition law: $$\sigma_y = \sigma_0 + \Delta\sigma_{\text{ss}} + \Delta\sigma_{\text{gb}} + \sqrt{(\Delta\sigma_d)^2 + (\Delta\sigma_p)^2}$$ where short-range obstacle mechanisms ($\Delta\sigma_d$ and $\Delta\sigma_p$) superimpose root-sum-of-squares, while long-range and friction mechanisms add linearly.

A microalloyed HSLA engineering steel has: - Base lattice friction: $\sigma_0 = 70.0\text{ MPa}$ - Solid solution contribution from Mn and Si: $\Delta\sigma_{\text{ss}} = 85.0\text{ MPa}$ - Hall-Petch slope $k_y = 0.50\text{ MPa}\cdot\text{m}^{1/2}$ with grain size refined to $d = 4.0\text{ }\mu\text{m}$ - Dislocation density $\rho_d = 1.0 \times 10^{14}\text{ m}^{-2}$ ($G = 80\text{ GPa}$, $b = 0.248\text{ nm}$, $\alpha = 0.30$, $\bar{M} = 3.06$) - Fine dispersion of coherent $\text{NbC}$ precipitates providing $\Delta\sigma_p = 160.0\text{ MPa}$

1. Calculate the grain boundary strengthening increment $\Delta\sigma_{\text{gb}}$.
2. Calculate the dislocation strain hardening increment $\Delta\sigma_d$.
3. Calculate the combined obstacle strength $\Delta\sigma_{\text{obs}} = \sqrt{(\Delta\sigma_d)^2 + (\Delta\sigma_p)^2}$.
4. Calculate the total yield strength $\sigma_y$ of this engineered alloy.
5. By what factor is the final yield strength higher than the pure iron base friction stress $\sigma_0$?
Step-by-Step Technical Solution & Analysis
Step 1: Calculate grain boundary strengthening increment $\Delta\sigma_{\text{gb}}$
Given: $k_y = 0.50\text{ MPa}\cdot\text{m}^{1/2}$, $d = 4.0 \times 10^{-6}\text{ m}$: $$d^{-1/2} = (4.0 \times 10^{-6})^{-0.5} = \frac{10^3}{\sqrt{4.0}} = \frac{1000}{2.0} = 500.0\text{ m}^{-1/2}$$ $$\Delta\sigma_{\text{gb}} = k_y d^{-1/2} = (0.50\text{ MPa}\cdot\text{m}^{1/2}) \times (500.0\text{ m}^{-1/2}) = 250.0\text{ MPa}$$
Step 2: Calculate dislocation strain hardening increment $\Delta\sigma_d$
$$\sqrt{\rho_d} = \sqrt{1.0 \times 10^{14}} = 1.0 \times 10^7\text{ m}^{-1}$$ $$\Delta\tau_d = \alpha G b \sqrt{\rho_d} = 0.30 \times (80 \times 10^9\text{ Pa}) \times (0.248 \times 10^{-9}\text{ m}) \times (1.0 \times 10^7\text{ m}^{-1})$$ $$\Delta\tau_d = (5.952\text{ N}) \times (1.0 \times 10^7\text{ m}^{-1}) = 5.952 \times 10^7\text{ Pa} \approx 59.52\text{ MPa}$$ Macroscopic tensile increment: $$\Delta\sigma_d = \bar{M} \Delta\tau_d = 3.06 \times 59.52\text{ MPa} \approx 182.13\text{ MPa}$$
Step 3: Calculate combined obstacle strength $\Delta\sigma_{\text{obs}}$
Given $\Delta\sigma_p = 160.0\text{ MPa}$ and $\Delta\sigma_d = 182.13\text{ MPa}$: $$\Delta\sigma_{\text{obs}} = \sqrt{(\Delta\sigma_d)^2 + (\Delta\sigma_p)^2} = \sqrt{(182.13)^2 + (160.0)^2}$$ $$(182.13)^2 = 33,171.3\text{ MPa}^2$$ $$(160.0)^2 = 25,600.0\text{ MPa}^2$$ $$\Delta\sigma_{\text{obs}} = \sqrt{33,171.3 + 25,600.0} = \sqrt{58,771.3} \approx 242.43\text{ MPa}$$
Step 4: Calculate total alloy yield strength $\sigma_y$
$$\sigma_y = \sigma_0 + \Delta\sigma_{\text{ss}} + \Delta\sigma_{\text{gb}} + \Delta\sigma_{\text{obs}}$$ $$\sigma_y = 70.0 + 85.0 + 250.0 + 242.43 = 647.43\text{ MPa}$$ Rounding to standard engineering precision: $$\sigma_y \approx 647.4\text{ MPa}$$
Step 5: Strengthening enhancement factor
$$\frac{\sigma_y}{\sigma_0} = \frac{647.43\text{ MPa}}{70.0\text{ MPa}} \approx 9.25$$ By intelligently combining solid solution ($85\text{ MPa}$), grain refinement ($250\text{ MPa}$), dislocation forest hardening ($182\text{ MPa}$), and $\text{NbC}$ precipitation ($160\text{ MPa}$), the yield strength of the steel is amplified by over $9.25\times$ above the intrinsic lattice resistance of iron.
Key Results:
$\Delta\sigma_{\text{gb}} = 250.0\text{ MPa}$ (Grain refinement)
$\Delta\sigma_d = 182.1\text{ MPa}$ (Work hardening)
Combined obstacle stress $\Delta\sigma_{\text{obs}} = 242.4\text{ MPa}$
Total engineered yield strength: $\sigma_y = 647.4\text{ MPa}$ ($9.25\times$ base iron strength).

Modulus of Resilience $$U_r = \int_0^{\varepsilon_y} \sigma d\varepsilon \approx \frac{\sigma_y^2}{2E}$$ \(U_r\): elastic energy stored per unit volume (\(\text{J/m}^3\) or \(\text{Pa}\))
Schmid's Law (Single Crystals) $$\tau_R = \sigma \cos\phi \cos\lambda \le \tau_{\text{CRSS}}$$ \(\phi\): angle to slip plane normal; \(\lambda\): angle to slip direction; \(m = \cos\phi\cos\lambda\)
Hall-Petch Strengthening $$\sigma_y = \sigma_0 + k_y d^{-1/2}$$ \(\sigma_0\): friction stress; \(k_y\): locking coefficient (\(\text{MPa}\cdot\text{mm}^{1/2}\)); \(d\): grain size
Cold Work & Dislocation Density $$\%\text{CW} = \frac{A_0 - A_d}{A_0} \times 100\%, \quad \tau = \tau_0 + \alpha G b \sqrt{\rho}$$ \(\rho\): dislocation density (\(\text{m}^{-2}\)); \(b\): Burgers vector; \(A_0, A_d\): cross-sections
Orowan Looping (Precipitation) $$\Delta\tau = \frac{G b}{L - 2r} \approx \frac{G b}{L}$$ \(L\): inter-particle spacing; \(r\): particle radius; \(G\): matrix shear modulus
Stress Concentration (Crack Tip) $$K_t = \frac{\sigma_m}{\sigma_0} = 2 \sqrt{\frac{a}{\rho_t}}$$ \(a\): crack length; \(\rho_t\): crack tip radius of curvature; \(\sigma_0\): nominal stress
Griffith Critical Fracture Stress $$\sigma_c = \sqrt{\frac{2E\gamma_s}{\pi a}}$$ \(\gamma_s\): specific surface energy (\(\text{J/m}^2\)); \(E\): Young's modulus; \(a\): crack size
Mode I Fracture Toughness $$K_I = Y \sigma \sqrt{\pi a} \le K_{Ic}$$ \(K_{Ic}\): plane strain fracture toughness (\(\text{MPa}\cdot\sqrt{\text{m}}\)); \(Y\): geometric factor
Fatigue Stress Parameters $$\sigma_m = \frac{\sigma_{\max}+\sigma_{\min}}{2}, \quad \sigma_a = \frac{\sigma_{\max}-\sigma_{\min}}{2}, \quad R = \frac{\sigma_{\min}}{\sigma_{\max}}$$ \(\sigma_m\): mean stress; \(\sigma_a\): amplitude; \(R\): stress ratio
Steady-State Creep Rate $$\dot{\varepsilon}_s = K_2 \sigma^n \exp\left(-\frac{Q_c}{RT}\right)$$ \(\dot{\varepsilon}_s\): steady creep rate (\(\text{s}^{-1}\)); \(n\): stress exponent; \(Q_c\): creep activation energy
Larson-Miller Parameter $$P = T \left(20 + \log_{10} t_r\right)$$ \(T\): absolute temperature in Kelvin; \(t_r\): rupture life in hours
